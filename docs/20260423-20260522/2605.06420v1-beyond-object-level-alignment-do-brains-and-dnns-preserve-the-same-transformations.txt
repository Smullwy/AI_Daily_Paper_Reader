Abstract 

Brain–DNN alignment is usually assessed through stimulus-level correspondence or stimulus-set geometry. Inspired by category theory, we operationalize a different question: do brain and model preserve the same candidate transformations among stimuli? We formalize this as approximate naturality — if a proxy-defined stimulus change is propagated through the brain side and then translated to the model side, the result should match translating first and then propagating through the model side, so that the naturality square approximately commutes. We quantify deviations from commutativity by a Naturality Violation Score (NVS) normalized to a permutation null, shifting alignment from per-stimulus sameness to preservation of structure under an explicitly chosen comparison map. As a proof of concept, a controlled five-factor synthetic setting shows that NVS separates complementary alignment failures that aggregate object- and geometry-level scalars cannot resolve. Applied to fMRI responses from the GOD dataset (5 subjects), 3 vision DNNs, and 3 external embedding spaces used as limited World-Model proxies, the axis-resolved analysis reveals a hierarchy crossover: semantic axes align most strongly toward HVC and deeper DNN layers (animacy has the lowest pooled NVS among the tested axes, 

NVS animacy = 0 .39 , compared with 0.52 for the next-best axis and 1.0 for the permutation-null baseline), whereas low- and mid-level visual axes align toward earlier visual cortex and shallower layers. Supporting analyses — a 15-axis appendix atlas, dissociation tests against RSA/CKA and encoding/decoding accuracy, and a W-less anchor-ablation control — support that the alignment is selective over candidate morphism families rather than uniform. NVS thereby turns brain–DNN comparison into a test of jointly preserved candidate transformations, relative to an explicitly chosen proxy space and permutation null, opening a path to richer proxy spaces and controlled world-side transformations. 

# 1 Introduction 

When brain and model assign similar codes to the same image, do they also move between images in the same way? Existing alignment metrics — encoding (Yamins et al., 2014) and decoding (Horikawa and Kamitani, 2017) accuracies, Brain-Score (Schrimpf et al., 2018), the Brain Hierarchy (BH) score (Nonaka et al., 2021), RSA (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008; Kriegeskorte, 2015), CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019), and Procrustes alignment — summarize how closely the two systems agree on individual stimuli or on overall stimulus-set geometry. In their standard use, these metrics do not directly test whether a specified stimulus transformation propagates compatibly through both systems: two systems can agree on per-stimulus predictions and on stimulus-set geometry yet still disagree about which changes among stimuli they treat as equivalent, stable, or meaningful. Inspired by category theory, we frame this as a question about approximate naturality : whether brain and model preserve the same proxy-defined candidate changes under translation, so that the 1

This shifts alignment from objectwise sameness to preservation of structure under an explicitly chosen comparison map. Concretely, B (brain) and M (model) are ambient vector spaces with stimulus points bs ∈ B, ms ∈ M . A tested stimulus change r : s → s′ is realized on each side by linear operators FB (r) : B → B and FM (r) : M → M (Fig. 1, left), which we approximate per edge from sampled stimulus pairs (formalized in §3). We call the cross-system maps η : B → M and 

η′ : M → B translators (Shirakawa et al., 2025): linear maps between brain activity and a target latent representation, with η a decoder and η′ an encoder. In the present framework they act as approximate intertwiners. For readability we write the forward square explicitly, 

η ◦ FB (r) = FM (r) ◦ η, (1) i.e., the two paths — propagate then translate, vs. translate then propagate — should agree (Fig. 1, right). The same square is asked of η′ : M → B in the reverse direction ( η′ ◦ FM (r) = FB (r) ◦ η′). Standard alignment metrics test only η(bs) ≈ ms at each stimulus individually; approximate naturality additionally constrains the change structure — whether the same candidate transformation is preserved across the two systems. The candidate morphisms r are specified through an external comparison space W : the proxy through which the same candidate stimulus change is mapped into both brain and model spaces, sitting at the apex of the cospan that links them (Fig. 1; full categorical setup in App. A1). The choice of W determines which transformations can be tested and is therefore part of the scientific question, not a nuisance parameter. In this paper, W is a limited World Model proxy in the broad neuro-AI sense (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Bisk et al., 2020), given by an embedding function 

FW : Stim → W ; we instantiate three: CLIP-text (Radford et al., 2021), DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024), and DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023), capturing language-grounded semantics, self-supervised visual structure, and human perceptual similarity, respectively. We do not treat these as full generative or causal world models, only as embedding spaces that supply a structured family of candidate morphisms. Differences within W parametrize world-structural changes via the linear representation hypothesis (Mikolov et al., 2013; Park et al., 2024) — the empirical observation that semantically meaningful features are encoded as approximately linear directions in modern embedding spaces, so 2that a single direction in W corresponds to one such change. Restricting to a single concept axis via a CAV (Kim et al., 2018) yields an axis-resolved version (formalized in §3; throughout this paper, “axis” refers only to this CAV-based parameterization). The Naturality Violation Score (NVS) is defined in §3 as the relative L2 residual of Eq. (1) , evaluated in both translation directions ( η, η ′)and normalized to a permutation null. We evaluate this in two stages. A five-factor synthetic toy world serves as a proof of concept 

(§4): morphism-level NVS recovers complementary failures that aggregate object- and geometry-level scalars collapse. On empirical data — GOD fMRI from 5 subjects, 3 vision DNNs, and 3 World Model proxies (§5) — we first fit conventional per-stimulus encoding/decoding mappings and only then evaluate naturality post hoc, without optimizing for commutativity. The axis-resolved analysis reveals a hierarchy crossover (Tab. The scope is deliberately limited: empirical results come from n = 5 subjects on a single dataset, depend on the chosen proxy space W (and the embedding FW that defines it), and use category-theoretic naturality as an operational analogy rather than a strict functorial claim — the operational maps used to realize each candidate morphism are fitted independently per edge without enforced composition (§3; App. A1). 

Contributions: (i) we reframe brain–DNN alignment as approximate naturality of the paired translators (η, η ′), building on linear decoding/encoding practice (Yamins et al., 2014; Horikawa and Kamitani, 2017) and the linear-representation hypothesis (Mikolov et al., 2013; Park et al., 2024) as an operational assumption for the chosen FW spaces; (ii) we define NVS, a permutation-normalized residual of that naturality square, and use a cospan reading to situate existing scalar metrics by what they retain and discard; (iii) a synthetic proof-of-concept demonstrates that NVS recovers complementary world-axis sharing that standard scalars collapse; (iv) on the GOD dataset (5 subjects, 3 vision DNNs, 3 World Model proxies) we report a hierarchy crossover in which alignment is selective and axis-dependent, with animacy as the strongest among the CAV-parameterized candidate morphism families tested. 

# 2 Related Work 

Most prior brain–DNN alignment work has asked whether the same objects or stimulus sets are represented similarly. Our framing is complementary: whether the same transformations or relations 

are preserved across the two systems. 

Encoding/decoding metrics. Encoding (Yamins et al., 2014; Naselaris et al., 2011) and decoding (Horikawa and Kamitani, 2017) accuracies provide per-stimulus correspondence between DNNs and brains; Brain-Score (Schrimpf et al., 2018) introduced these as a composite benchmark and was later developed into an integrative benchmarking framework (Schrimpf et al., 2020), while the BH score (Nonaka et al., 2021) summarizes the resulting ROI–layer hierarchy. In our framework these correspond to the closest object-level special cases of the cospan, obtained by choosing W =M or 

W =B and collapsing the question to stimuli rather than directed morphisms (App. A9). 3Representational similarity. RSA (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008; Kriegeskorte, 2015), CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019), and Procrustes alignment compare representational geometry through second-order pairwise statistics or global alignment transforms. These methods move from individual objects to stimulus-set structure, but still summarize agreement as a single scalar and do not isolate which candidate transformations are preserved. 

Critical re-evaluation of brain–DNN alignment. Recent work (Conwell et al., 2024) questions how strongly current scalar metrics actually distinguish brain-aligned from non-aligned representa-tions, motivating finer-resolution diagnostics. Sucholutsky et al. (2025) similarly argue alignment is plural; our proposal isolates preserved morphism families as one such aspect, complementary to integrative-benchmark approaches (Schrimpf et al., 2020). The aim here is not merely a finer scalar, but a shift in question: from global similarity to selective correspondence across morphism classes. Animacy is a long-standing organizing principle of the ventral stream (Konkle and Caramazza, 2013; Long et al., 2018), providing prior validation for the axis-level outlier we report. 

World models in neuro-AI. Ha and Schmidhuber (2018); Bisk et al. (2020) situate a world model as a latent representation through which perception is interpreted. We use FW as a linearized proxy for this role: not as another target representation to match, but as a structural anchor that specifies which candidate morphisms are being tested. 

Categorical / equivariant approaches. Categorical deep learning (Gavranović et al., 2024) and category-theoretic work in cognitive science (Ehresmann and Vanbremeersch, 2007; Phillips, 2022) provide formal language for structure-preserving mappings, but have not been applied to brain–DNN data. More broadly, treating brain–DNN alignment as preservation of selected relations under an externally supplied comparison map parallels the partial-structures view of scientific representation (da Costa and French, 2003), in which inter-system correspondence holds only on a designated subset of relations, and the comparison-map-relative reading of representation due to van Fraassen (2008). Equivariant networks (Cohen and Welling, 2016; Sanborn et al., 2023) build transformation-respecting structure directly into FM at design time, fixing which transformations a model must preserve. Our framework is complementary: FM is taken as given (a pre-trained vision DNN), and we measure post hoc which transformations brain and DNN do preserve in common, with the candidate class set by FW rather than baked in.

Because each NVS d is already normalized to its own permutation null, the symmetric mean NVS is itself a chance-referenced score: NVS = 1 .0 is chance, NVS = 0 is perfect commutativity, and lower is better. “Chance” here refers specifically to the permutation null that destroys cross-space pairing while preserving the marginal geometry of ΦB (∆ W ) and ΦM (∆ W ); NVS < 1 should therefore be read as evidence for structure relative to this null, not as an absolute proof of shared representation. full-vector regime uses the full ∆W = FW (s′) − FW (s) and yields NVS full , a single global score over all directions in W . # 5 Empirical study: brain–DNN alignment on GOD 

We next move from the toy proof of concept to empirical data, instantiating the cospan of §3 with a brain side B and a model side M on the GOD dataset (Horikawa and Kamitani, 2017). The GOD stimuli are natural object photographs drawn from ImageNet-linked categories. fMRI responses from 5 subjects viewing these images provide B as voxel patterns over 5 ROIs (V1, V2, V3, V4, and a higher-visual-cortex ROI HVC defined as LOC ∪ FFA ∪ PPA); each of 3 vision DNNs (AlexNet, ResNet-50, ViT-B/16) is fed the same images and provides M as activations over 8 representative layers (L1–L8). Three external World Model proxies supply FW for axis decomposition: CLIP-text 6from the per-image AMT captions, DINOv2 and DreamSim from the images themselves. Across the 1,200 single-trial training images we fit ΦB , ΦM , η, η ′ as defined in §3; NVS is then evaluated on the 50 trial-averaged 35 -trial test images, using the exhaustive 50 ×49 = 2 ,450 ordered test pairs. The full grid is 5 ROI × 8 layer × 3 FW × 6 axes (plus a full-FW row); per-direction η, η ′ values are in App. A3.10. We adopt a two-phase exploratory–confirmatory design (full protocol: App. A3): all choices underlying the headline confirmatory analyses — modeling form, axis set, proxy-viability criterion, cell grid, and test statistics — are fixed on Sub-01, and Sub-02–05 ( n= 4) are then analyzed under that frozen protocol as a held-out confirmatory cohort. Sub-01-only post hoc checks (e.g., the MLP ΦB , ΦM control) are reported separately in App. A8. The setup is also diagnostic: we first fit conventional object-level encode/decode-style mappings and only then ask how well those mappings satisfy the naturality criterion, rather than optimizing them for commutativity. As in the PoC, we report both the per-axis and unrestricted full-FW results. 

5.1 Per-axis NVS along candidate morphism classes 

The three World Model proxies are not equally informative for every tested axis. Held-out 5-fold CAV readout from FW is high for the semantic axes (animacy: 0.68 –0.91 ; real size: 0.57 –0.83 ), strong for DreamSim on the low/mid-level visual axes, modest for CLIP-text on curvilinearity and texture energy, and poor for DINOv2 on the low/mid-level visual axes (App. A3.8; Tab. A1). Under the proxy-viability criterion used throughout the paper (positive held-out CAV readout, 

R2 > 0), CLIP-text passes 4/6 main axes, DINOv2 2/6, and DreamSim 6/6; on the 15-axis atlas the corresponding counts are 12/15, 3/15, and 15/15 (App. A3.8). We treat these counts as diagnostics of the World Model proxies themselves: when a proxy-axis combination fails this screen, that proxy is not an adequate comparison space for that axis under the present setup. The downstream NVS values are nonetheless retained in the pooled summaries and figures, with the failed combinations marked explicitly rather than filtered out. We therefore treat FW as part of the scientific question rather than a nuisance-free substrate: different proxies support different candidate morphism families.

5.2 Comparing axes across architectures and proxies 

Within the tested axis set, animacy separates from the rest: 5-subject pooled NVS animacy = 0 .39 ±0.06 

(bootstrap 95% CI [0 .34 , 0.44] ), the lowest-ratio axis in 5/5 subjects across the six main scalar axes (because each axis is normalized to its own permutation null, cross-axis comparisons reflect relative preservation against axis-specific shuffled baselines rather than raw residual magnitudes); across the same six axes the remaining pooled means range 0.52 –0.72 (Tab. 2). Animacy stays low across all 9 (FW , DNN ) combinations whereas the low/mid-level axes depend more strongly on which World Model proxy is viable for them (App. A3.8); subject-resampling CIs and the 15-axis atlas (Apps. A4.2, A5) support the same axis ordering. The animate/inanimate distinction is a long-known organizing principle of ventral cortex (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008; Konkle and Caramazza, 2013); the morphism-level finding here is consistent with that prior without implying animacy is uniquely privileged. Crucially, “high-level” is not by itself sufficient for strong alignment: in the 15-axis atlas (App. A5), the affordance and material axes ( hold , ride , mat_metal , mat_natural ) all cluster at NVS a ≈ 0.73 –0.74 , well above NVS animacy (0.39 ) and the better low/mid-level axes. 

5.3 Unrestricted full-FW baseline 

Beyond the axis decomposition, the unrestricted full-FW comparison still separates the proxies (DreamSim ≈ 0.58 < CLIP-text ≈ 0.70 < DINOv2 ≈ 0.85 ) but answers a different question and lacks the per-axis structure: no full-FW cell reaches the per-axis animacy minimum (Tab. 3). The brain side B and model side M are the same in all three blocks; what changes is FW , which specifies the morphism family being tested. Lower full-∆ NVS for DreamSim therefore means the DreamSim-defined family propagates more compatibly through the same (B, M ) pair than the DINOv2-defined one, i.e. a morphism-family comparison rather than a global proxy-quality or DNN-quality ranking; this is why §1 treats W as part of the scientific question rather than a nuisance parameter. 5.4 Dissociation from encoding/decoding and similarity metrics 

NVS is not a re-description of encoding/decoding accuracy or representational similarity. The variance decomposition (App. A7.3) attributes only ≈ 34% of NVS variance to five readout-quality covariates — CAV CV R2 for the axis target plus pairwise-identification accuracies of ΦB , ΦM ,

η, η′ — and η, η ′ together add only ≤ 0.7% on top of the other three, while axis identity alone adds ≈ 34% on top of FW /DNN/subject controls. As expected for global geometry metrics, RSA and CKA on the same cells provide a single cell-level summary (peaking at rs = 0 .27 / 0.46 ) and cannot distinguish which axis drives alignment (App. A8.3); the session-bias PoC (App. A2) further 9dissociates RSA (collapses 1.00 → 0.29 under additive bias) from symmetric NVS ( 0.04 → 0.06 ,near-stable). Together, these results argue the main effect is not a by-product of simple readout strength or static geometry. 

Role of FW : W-less control. A W-less control (App. A6) replaces FW -derived directions by independent CAVs vB , v M in B and M , and stays close to the permutation-null baseline on the tested axes: the resulting per-axis ratios are systematically higher than their with-FW counterparts (Tab. A5). The reason is geometric: ΦB (vW ), ΦM (vW ) derived from a shared vW test both sides against the same world morphism, whereas independently optimized vB , v M are not constrained to do so, and the cosine cos (η(vB ), v M ) is small (typically below ∼ 0.15 on Sub-01/AlexNet across the tested axes). Choosing FW therefore controls which morphism class is tested, not how strongly a fixed one is detected. 

# 6 Discussion 

Our results recover an early-to-late ROI–layer ordering analogous to BH / Brain-Score-style analyses (Nonaka et al., 2021; Yamins et al., 2014; Schrimpf et al., 2018), but resolved by morphism class: animacy gives the strongest support among the main axes, with navigable and texture energy also coherent in the 15-axis atlas. Existing metrics (encoding/decoding, Brain-Score, RSA, CKA, CCA, Procrustes) sit within the cospan picture as what they retain or discard (App. A9); none directly tests agreement at the level of transformations . Full-∆ and per-axis NVS are complementary: the full-vector regime gives a single global magnitude, while the per-axis regime localizes which candidate transformations are preserved or missed (Tab. 1, §5.1). The W-less control (App. A6) confirms that alignment requires a shared world-side anchor. Compared with the BH score (Nonaka et al., 2021), NVS shows much weaker DNN dependence (variance decomposition: ≈ 0.001 to DNN dummies, ≈ 0.34 to axis identity), consistent with asking whether the same morphism class propagates through the cospan rather than how layer order maps onto V1 →HVC. 

Limitations. The empirical study uses one dataset ( n = 5 on GOD), three vision DNNs, and three 

FW proxies. η, η ′ are fit with standard encoding/decoding objectives and NVS is applied only post hoc ; NVS-aware training and morphism-level losses (Cao and Yamins, 2024a) are natural extensions. Sharper tests will require brain recording experiments with controlled world-side transformations. 

Broader impacts and conclusion. Axis-resolved alignment can improve interpretability, but “shared” claims could still be overstated. NVS reframes alignment as naturality of (η, η ′): which 

transformations propagate determines where alignment is strongest.

# A1 Categorical preliminaries 

General categorical definitions. A category C consists of objects and morphisms with associative composition and identities. A functor F : C → D assigns to each object of C an object of D, and to each morphism a morphism, preserving composition and identities. Given two functors F, G : C → D 

with the same source and target, a natural transformation η : F ⇒ G assigns to each object 

A ∈ C a morphism ηA : F (A) → G(A) in D such that the naturality square commutes for every morphism f : A → B in C. When F, G are constant on objects – the case used throughout this paper – all components ηA coincide; writing η for that single map, the naturality square becomes (Fig. A1): 

η ◦ F (f ) = G(f ) ◦ η. (3) The square lives entirely in D; the morphism f in C enters only through its images F (f ), G (f ).                      

> Figure A1: Naturality square (constant-on-objects specialization). For functors F, G :C → D that are constant on objects and a morphism f:A→Bin C, the natural transformation η:F⇒Gcollapses to a single map ηwhich must make the square commute in D.

Specialization to the brain–DNN setting. Our framework instantiates this picture as a motivational reading (the strict categorical conditions are relaxed below): 

• C = Stim : the stimulus category. Objects are stimuli s; morphisms r : s → s′ are stimulus changes. 

• D = Vect : the category of real vector spaces and linear maps. Both the brain pattern space 

B and the DNN activation space M are objects of Vect (with different dimensions, but in the same category). 

• FB : Stim → Vect sends every stimulus to B and each r to a linear operator FB (r) : B → B

(Ridge-fitted from ∆W 7 → ∆b). 

• FM : Stim → Vect sends every stimulus to M and each r to FM (r) : M → M .

• Both assignments are constant on objects (FB (s) = B and FM (s) = M for all s). Under the strict-categorical reading FB , F M would be functors and the per-stimulus components of a natural transformation ηs : FB (s) → FM (s) would all collapse to the single linear map 

η : B → M – the brain–DNN translator. We use these terms only to motivate the test; the operational version drops the functor / natural-transformation status (see Quivers and approximate functoriality below). 15 The general naturality condition (Eq. 3) specializes to the equation that NVS measures: 

η ◦ FB (r) = FM (r) ◦ η for every r ∈ Stim . (4) The corresponding square in Fig. 1 (right panel) takes bs, b s′ , m s, m s′ at the four corners as a concrete instantiation: bs moved by FB (r) should match ms moved by FM (r) once both are translated by η.

Role of the World Model. The World Model proxies (CLIP-text, DINOv2, DreamSim), each given by an embedding FW : Stim → W into a proxy space W , do not appear in the naturality square. They sit above Stim and select which morphisms r we test, via semantic deltas ∆W = FW (s′)−FW (s) ∈ W .Restricting ∆W to a single CAV direction inside W yields an axis-resolved r (and hence an axis-resolved NVS). Despite the shared letter F , the embedding FW plays a different role from the brain-and model-side assignments FB , F M above: FW parameterizes which stimulus changes are tested, while FB , F M describe how those changes are realized inside B and M .

Quivers and approximate functoriality. The morphisms we test form a quiver Q = ( Q0, Q 1) –a directed multigraph with Q0 stimuli and Q1 edges (e.g., the semantic-delta morphisms in §3). The operators FB (r), F M (r) are fitted independently per edge, so we do not assume they extend to a strict functor on the free category over Q; composition preservation is therefore not enforced. What we fit is an empirical operator family on the quiver Q, not a strict functor; “categorical-inspired” in our usage refers to this distinction. 

NVS as approximate naturality. Empirically, Eq. 4 holds only approximately. We define NVS η

(§3) as the relative L2 residual of this equation normalized to a permutation null – a quantitative measure of approximate naturality, with both a geometric reading (gap, in M , between the two paths through the square) and a statistical one (rate of approximation failure relative to chance).

A3.1 Subjects and fMRI data 

The Generic Object Decoding (GOD) dataset (Horikawa and Kamitani, 2017) contains fMRI responses from 5 human subjects viewing natural object images drawn from ImageNet-linked object categories. In the standard GOD zero-shot split used here, the training set contains voxel-response patterns for 

1,200 training images spanning 150 object categories, each image presented once, so “single-trial” refers here to a single-image fMRI response pattern. The test set contains voxel-response patterns for 50 held-out images from 50 categories that do not overlap the training categories; each test image was repeated 35 times, and the released test response for each image is the 35-trial mean fMRI pattern. Sub-01 served as the exploratory cohort : model family, axis set, proxy-viability criterion, cell grid, and the planned hypothesis tests (e.g., sign reversal between axis classes, animacy as the lowest-ratio axis) were all fixed on Sub-01 before any inspection of Sub-02–Sub-05. Sub-02 through Sub-05 ( n= 4) were then held out as the confirmatory cohort , evaluated under exactly that fixed protocol with no further model, hyperparameter, or test-statistic adjustment. This is a deliberate two-phase research-design commitment rather than a hyperparameter tuning exercise. We use 5 ROIs from the visual cortex (V1, V2, V3, V4, HVC = LOC ∪ FFA ∪ PPA). 

A3.2 World Model proxies FW

Why call them “World Model” proxies? We follow the broad neuro-AI usage in which a “World Model” is a learned latent representation that encodes structural regularities of the world the agent perceives, and through which other systems can be interpreted (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Bisk et al., 2020). We do not claim our FW proxies are full generative world models; rather, they are 

proxy embedding spaces satisfying three structural conditions that, in our framework, are what is needed to play the role of FW :1. Stimulus-aligned. FW provides a fixed embedding FW (s) ∈ RdW for every stimulus s, so 

∆W = FW (s′) − FW (s) is well-defined as the world-side delta tested in NVS. 2. Carries world-level structure beyond the brain or the DNN. CLIP-text encodes language-grounded semantics (captions describe categories, attributes, actions); DINOv2 encodes object-centric self-supervised image structure (instance distinctions, viewpoint invariances); DreamSim encodes human perceptual-similarity structure (judgments of which images “look alike” to people). Each captures a candidate organization of the world that is not a copy of B (fMRI) or any single vision DNN M , so FW is not collapsing into either side of the comparison. 3. Supports a meaningful axis decomposition. Each FW admits CAV-style axis directions (App. A3.8; Kim et al., 2018) that pass a held-out viability check on the candidate axes we test, ensuring that “ ∆W along axis k” is operationally well-posed. The viability screen (App. A3.8) is the empirical safeguard for condition 3: when a proxy fails on an axis, we mark it as inadequate for that morphism family rather than treating FW as a nuisance-free substrate. The three proxies were chosen to span complementary world-structure families (language, self-supervised vision, perceptual similarity) so that “which FW supports which axis” becomes itself part of the scientific question. 

The three proxies used here. 

• CLIP-text (Radford et al., 2021): per-image average CLIP text embedding from the OpenAI CLIP ViT-B/32 model (512-d). The five captions per image are AMT crowdsourced annotations supplied with the GOD stimulus set. Captures language-grounded semantic, affordance, and 17 material structure that humans naturally describe; weak on low-level photometric structure (captions rarely describe luminance or spatial frequency). 

• DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024): CLS token of ViT-B/14 self-supervised image embeddings (768-d). Captures object-centric self-supervised image structure trained without labels on the LVD-142M corpus; strong on category-level distinctions, weak on attribute axes that captions name (its viability screen passes only animacy, real size, and navigable on the 15-axis atlas). 

• DreamSim (Fu et al., 2023): ensemble of DINO, CLIP, and OpenCLIP backbones fine-tuned on the NIGHTS dataset of human triplet similarity judgments (512-d). Captures human perceptual similarity structure directly and yields the lowest pooled NVS full values among the tested proxies. 

A3.3 Vision DNNs 

The vision DNN side M is implemented by extracting features from three architectures spanning different inductive biases. From each network we select 8 representative layers (early to late) for analysis, giving the 5 ROI × 8 layer heatmap grid used throughout. 

• AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al., 2012): classical 8-layer CNN trained on ImageNet-1k. We use the 5 conv stages plus the 3 fully-connected layers (cnn1–cnn8 in the heatmaps), giving a clean shallow →deep progression with comparatively narrow receptive fields early on. 

• ResNet-50 (He et al., 2016): 50-layer residual CNN trained on ImageNet-1k. We sample 8 representative blocks distributed from the stem to the last bottleneck (layer1–layer8), retaining the canonical CNN locality bias but with much greater depth and skip connections. 

• ViT-B/16 (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021): vision Transformer with 12 transformer blocks operating on 16 × 16 patch tokens, trained on ImageNet-21k →1k. We sample 8 transformer blocks (layer1–layer8) covering early to late processing. Unlike the two CNNs, ViT-B/16 has global self-attention from the first block, so the “shallow →deep” axis is a depth axis but not a receptive-field axis. The three architectures differ in inductive bias (locality vs. global attention), depth (8 vs. 50 vs. 12 blocks), and training regime, so any architecture-invariant pattern across them is a stronger claim than a pattern specific to one model family. Concretely, AlexNet and ResNet-50 use the standard 

torchvision ImageNet-1k weights ( AlexNet_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1 , ResNet50_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V2 ); ViT-B/16 uses the torchvision ViT_B_16_Weights.IMAGENET1K_V1 checkpoint, originally pre-trained on ImageNet-21k and fine-tuned on ImageNet-1k. 

A3.4 Per-target Ridge regression and feature selection 

Linear translators η, η ′. The translator pair η : B → M (decoder direction) and η′ : M → B

(encoder direction) is modeled linearly, following the standard linear tradition in brain decod-ing/encoding studies (Kamitani and Tong, 2005; Naselaris et al., 2011; Horikawa and Kamitani, 2017; Nonaka et al., 2021). Concretely, each output dimension is fit with Ridge regression. For an output map ϕ : X → Y and output dimension j, we (1) compute |corr (x·,i , y ·,j )| across training samples; (2) select the top K = 500 input dimensions; and (3) fit Ridge regression with α = 100 .

World-to-brain and world-to-model maps ΦB , ΦM . ΦB and ΦM are implemented in a linear Ridge form for the main analysis, so that the maps in the empirical cospan remain first-order and directly comparable to the linear η, η ′ layer. We additionally tested nonlinear variants of ΦB , ΦM

using a single-hidden-layer MLP (input → 128 ReLU units with dropout 0.3 → output; two weight matrices, hence “2-layer” in the conventional MLP-counting sense), but report the linear results in 18 the main text and treat the nonlinear results as robustness checks rather than as the primary analysis (App. A8.2). Additional sensitivity checks for α and top-K are reported later in the appendix. 

A3.5 Semantic-delta protocol 

For training image pairs, we sample 30,000 pairs and compute ∆W = FW (s2)−FW (s1), ∆B = bs2 −bs1 ,and ∆M = ms2 − ms1 . ΦB and ΦM are fit from ∆W 7 → ∆B , ∆M . Test pairs use the 50 averaged test images; we evaluate all 50 × 49 = 2 ,450 ordered pairs. By contrast, η and η′ are fit on per-image correspondences rather than directly on deltas, following the usual per-stimulus encoding/decoding setup of Naselaris et al. (2011) and later brain-decoding work. Under the present linear translator setup, however, the same fitted map also induces the corresponding delta map, since for any linear operator L, L(x2 − x1) = L(x2) − L(x1). Fitting η and η′ at the image level therefore fixes their action on ∆B and ∆M as well. 

Linear Representation Hypothesis as the rationale for ∆W . Treating ∆W = FW (s′) − FW (s)

as a meaningful candidate stimulus morphism rests on the Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH; Mikolov et al., 2013; Park et al., 2024), which states that semantically meaningful features are encoded as approximately linear directions in modern representation spaces, so that vector-arithmetic differences (e.g. queen − woman ≈ king − man in the canonical word-vector example) recover interpretable transformations. Concretely, ∆W together with the projection onto a CAV concept axis (§A3.8) operationalizes “a real-world feature change” as a linear direction in W . The proxy-viability check (App. A3.8) is the empirical safeguard for this assumption per axis: when a proxy fails to support an axis as a linear readout target (held-out CAV CV R2 ≤ 0), we mark the corresponding 

(FW , axis ) combination as inadequate rather than rely on a non-linear direction. 

A3.6 Axis definitions 

We define each axis as a per-stimulus scalar target y(s) ∈ R, computed from the GOD stimulus annotations or directly from the stimulus image. This subsection gives the full 15-axis appendix atlas; see §1 and §5.1 for the six main-axis analyses. 

Low-level photometric axes. 

• luminance (main ): mean of the per-pixel CIE luminance ( Y channel of CIE XYZ) over the image, after standard sRGB-to-linear conversion. Indexes overall image brightness, a parameter long known to drive early visual responses and to vary systematically across natural-image categories (Oliva and Torralba, 2001). 

• saturation : mean of the saturation channel (HSV S) over the image. Indexes how chromatic vs. achromatic the stimulus is, a low-level feature dissociable from hue (Conway, 2009). 

• hue-a: mean of the CIELAB a∗ channel (red–green opponency). 

• hue-b: mean of the CIELAB b∗ channel (blue–yellow opponency). Together hue-a / hue-b

span the perceptually approximately uniform chromatic plane used in primate cone-opponent processing (Conway, 2009). 

Mid-level form/texture/spatial axes. 

• spatial_freq (main ): mean of the radially averaged Fourier-amplitude spectrum, weighted by the natural-image 1/f profile so that image-specific deviations from 1/f contribute to the scalar. Tracks the global spatial-envelope of the scene (Oliva and Torralba, 2001) and aligns with the spatial frequency tuning of V1 simple cells (Olshausen and Field, 1996). 19 • curvilinearity (main ): summary statistic of orientation-tuned Gabor responses, contrasting energy at curved boundaries against energy at near-rectilinear boundaries. Curvature is a mid-level feature with dedicated tuning maps in primate inferotemporal cortex (Bao et al., 2020). 

• texture_energy (main ): summary scalar of multi-scale, multi-orientation Gabor-energy statistics following the texture-statistics framework of Portilla and Simoncelli (2000). High texture energy images carry rich repeated micro-pattern, low values are dominated by smooth surfaces. 

• object_area_ratio : ratio of segmented foreground-object area to image area, computed from per-stimulus segmentation masks supplied with the GOD annotations. 

High-level semantic / affordance / material axes. 

• animacy (main ): binary indicator y ∈ { 0, 1} via WordNet (Fellbaum, 1998) hypernymy, true if the stimulus’s WordNet synset is a hyponym of animal.n.01 . Animacy is a long-known organizing principle of human ventral cortex (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008; Konkle and Caramazza, 2013; Long et al., 2018). 

• real_size (main ): continuous 1–7 scalar following Konkle and Oliva (2012), assigned via a WordNet hypernym walk against a hand-curated heuristic table that maps superordinate categories (insect, mammal, vehicle, building, etc.) to a perceived real-world size rating. 

• navigable : binary affordance indicator, true if the stimulus depicts a region a human could plausibly walk into; constructed from the GOD per-image affordance annotations following the navigability literature (Greene and Oliva, 2009; Bonner and Epstein, 2017). 

• hold / ride : binary affordance indicators (graspable / rideable), constructed analogously from the GOD per-image annotations. 

• mat_metal / mat_natural : binary material indicators (metallic surface / natural material such as wood/stone/skin), following the material-perception taxonomy of Sharan et al. (2014). The four mid-level visual scalars (luminance, spatial frequency, curvilinearity, texture energy) and the color scalars (saturation, hue-a, hue-b) are computed per stimulus from cached per-pixel statistics released alongside the GOD stimulus archive ( code/_god_visual_features_cache.npz ,

code/_god_visual_features_extras_cache.npz ); animacy and real size are derived from the GOD WordNet synset annotations; the affordance and material axes use the GOD per-image binary annotations. All reported axes are scalar (1-D). 

A3.7 Concept-axis CAVs 

For each axis and each FW , we learn a CAV vaxis ∈ RdFW by Ridge regression of the axis-target scalar 

y(s) onto FW embeddings of the same stimuli, following the concept-activation-vector formulation of Kim et al. (2018). To train ΦB and ΦM for a given axis, we instantiate axis-specific world deltas by sampling a scalar coefficient α ∼ N (0 , 1) and setting ∆W = α v axis , so the input world delta used to fit these maps varies only along that one CAV direction. For test pairs, by contrast, we start from the observed image-pair delta ∆W = FW (s′) − FW (s) and project it onto vaxis to extract the component of the empirical stimulus change that lies along the chosen axis. A3.9 Evaluation 

Evaluation is performed on the full set of ordered test-image pairs. For each pair, we compute the two path outputs in each direction and take the Euclidean residual in the corresponding target space. As in §3, NVS η and NVS η′ are the per-direction residuals normalized by their permutation-null residuals, and the reported quantity is their symmetric mean NVS = 12 (NVS η + NVS η′ ). The permutation null shuffles pair indices independently in ΦB (∆ W ) and ΦM (∆ W ), preserving the marginal geometry on each side while destroying the tested cross-space correspondence. 

A3.10 Bidirectional aggregation of NVS η, NVS η′

Averaging the two directions follows the logic of prior bidirectional brain–DNN comparisons that combine encoding- and decoding-side evidence into a single summary score, such as the hierarchy score of Nonaka et al. (2021). The two directions also vary together rather than disagreeing: across subject-averaged cells the per-cell NVS η and NVS η′ values are positively correlated (Pearson r = 0 .65 ,Spearman ρ = 0 .64 on the six-axis panel; r = 0 .64 , ρ = 0 .63 on the 15-axis atlas), so the symmetric summary is not averaging out qualitatively different signals. 21 Figure A2: Per-subject counterpart of Fig. 3 (Sub-01–05, 3 + 2 layout). # A9 Reduction of existing metrics to NVS 

Several commonly used brain–DNN alignment metrics can be discussed within the same broad 

W –B–M picture, but they should not all be read as exact algebraic reductions of NVS. Some are 30 close to object-level special cases obtained by choosing a particular comparison space; others are better viewed as partial projections of the broader alignment problem (for example, to stimulus-set geometry rather than directed morphisms). Tab. A9 uses this deliberately modest “cospan reading” only to clarify what each metric retains and what it discards. The reading is closest to exact for the object-level encoding/decoding cases; for RSA, CKA, CCA, and Procrustes it is better understood as a structural analogy indicating which aspect of the broader alignment problem is retained.

31 Under this interpretation, NVS contains the object-level encoding/decoding cases as near-degenerate limits of the same setup, while RSA, CKA, CCA, Procrustes, and related approaches are more cautiously interpreted as projections or complements of the broader morphism-level question rather than as literal algebraic reductions. 

Relation to the plural-alignment view of Sucholutsky et al. (2025). Sucholutsky et al. (2025) argue that “representational alignment” is not a single quantity: different measures (encod-ing/decoding accuracy, RSA-style geometry, behavior-matched probes, neural predictivity) probe genuinely different aspects of how two systems correspond, and that these aspects can dissociate. The cospan reading in Tab. A9 is a concrete realization of this stance: each existing metric is a particular projection of the W –B–M picture that retains some aspects (e.g., pointwise prediction, second-order geometry, shared subspaces, orthogonal alignment) and discards others (e.g., directed 

∆W , axis identity, morphism class). NVS adds a missing projection to that pluralist landscape — preservation of directed candidate morphisms under an explicitly chosen FW — rather than claiming to subsume the others. In particular, our dissociations in §5.4 (NVS disagreeing with ΦB predictive accuracy, with CAV R2, and with RSA/CKA on the same cells) are empirical instances of the kind of measure-dependent partial alignment that Sucholutsky et al. (2025) predict: a single ROI ×layer cell can score well on one projection and poorly on another, so reporting a single scalar erases the structure that the morphism-level reading recovers.
