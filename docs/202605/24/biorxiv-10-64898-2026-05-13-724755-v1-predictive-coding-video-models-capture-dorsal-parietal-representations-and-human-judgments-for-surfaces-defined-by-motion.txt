Abstract 

Stimulus-computable models have transformed our understanding of ventral visual processing, yet comparable progress in modeling the dorsal visual stream have lagged behind. Classical motion-energy models capture only local signals and fall short of representing coherent structure from motion, while image-trained neural networks discard the temporal structure essential to motion-based computations. This leaves the dorsal pathway without a computational account linking dynamic visual inputs to the neural activity underlying shape process-ing. We address this gap by combining human psychophysics, chronic neural recordings from macaque dorsal and ventral cortices, and systematic evaluation of a large-scale model zoo. Using texture-masked rotating objects that isolate motion-defined surface geometry from static cues, we found that both visual path-ways carry decodable representations of object surfaces, with dorsal regions more closely tracking human behavioral judgements. Encoding analyses reveal that predictive coding video models–trained to predict spatiotemporal features in nat-ural videos–best predict neural responses in the inferior parietal lobule (IPL), a downstream region of the dorsal visual pathway. These models outperform alter-native models, including both classical motion filters and multimodal foundation

Computational models have become a central tool for probing the mechanisms of visual processing. Over the past decade, artificial neural networks optimized for object recognition have emerged as leading image-computable models of the ventral visual stream, capturing key organizational features of the ventral hierarchy when mapped onto neural spiking activity [1, 2]. However, this progress has been confined to static form and object categorization. Despite the dorsal pathway’s critical role in processing motion and guiding action, dorsal-pathway models have not kept pace with their ven-tral counterparts at downstream stages [3, 4]. Neither a leading stimulus-computable model nor a benchmark of neural data and standardized evaluation has been estab-lished, leaving a major gap in our ability to link dynamic visual inputs to the neural responses underlying scene understanding. Motion is ubiquitous in natural environments, arising from self-motion, the move-ments of other agents, and the displacement of objects. To act in such environments, the visual system must recover three-dimensional geometry from such dynamic input. To navigate effectively, the visual system must rapidly extract coherent structure from constantly changing inputs, enabling figure–ground segregation and recovering surface geometry to support visuomotor coordination. The classical dorsal–ventral framework assigns motion processing to the dorsal pathway and form analysis to the ventral stream [5–8], but recovering surface geometry from objects in motion does not respect this dichotomy – surface geometry is both a property of object form and one that emerges from object motion. This ambiguity motivates an investigation of how both pathways contribute to the processing of surface geometry from motion. Recovering surface geometry from motion sequences has long been formalized in computer vision as Structure-from-Motion (SfM) [9, 10], providing theoretical hypotheses for biological motion-based reconstruction. At the level of local motion processing, much is known. Neurons in middle temporal area (MT) are essential for detecting motion coherence and carry early signatures of depth processing [11–13]. However, estimating surface geometry requires integration across spatial and tempo-ral scales that extends well beyond MT, implicating MST and parietal regions for optic-flow integration and ventral areas including the lateral occipital complex for coherent object percepts [14, 15]. Crucially, without models capable of processing dynamic inputs and predicting these higher-level responses, the dorsal stream lacks a mechanistic account linking visual input to neural representation. Here, we address this gap. To isolate surface-from-motion processing, we designed a novel stimulus set of rotating abstract objects with identical textures applied to both foreground and background, camouflaging static boundaries and forcing the visual system to rely on motion-based grouping to recover three-dimensional structure. We recorded neural activity from downstream regions of both visual pathways– inferior temporal (IT) cortex in the ventral stream and the inferior parietal lobule (IPL) in the dorsal stream–while macaques passively viewed these stimuli, and we measured human behavioral judgments on a dynamic surface-matching task using the same stimuli. Applying neural decoders to populations from both pathways, we 3

Furthermore, to reveal the computational principles driving these representations, we evaluated a diverse set of models spanning classical motion energy filters, supervised and self-supervised convolutional networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs), image foundation models, supervised video classification models, and predictive coding video models (i.e., models trained to predict masked content from natural video; the term as used here is defined in Section 2.4). Using encoding analyses to map model features directly onto neural population responses, we asked which computational framework best captures the transformation from dynamic visual input to cortical representation. Our results demonstrate that predictive coding video models–trained to predict spatiotemporal features from natural videos–provide the strongest correspondence to human behavior and neural activity, particularly in downstream dorsal regions, for processing surface geometry in dynamic scenes. By establishing a stimulus-computable baseline for visual processing regions, this work provides computational insights for developing world models that account for geometric scene understanding in the primate brain. 

# 2 Results 

2.1 Shape-from-motion stimuli 

We designed our stimulus set to isolate surface geometry perception by leveraging motion cues. Identical textures were applied to both foreground objects and their static backgrounds, effectively camouflaging luminance and chromatic boundaries that would otherwise support object-background segmentation (Fig. 1a). Under this texture-masking paradigm, 3D surfaces become visible only through differential motion patterns as objects rotate. Observers must therefore rely on motion-based grouping to integrate continuous light patterns into coherent surface geometry, rather than exploiting discontinuities in luminance, color, or texture. This design directly targets the visual system’s capacity for estimating surface geometry from motion cues. Each 400 ms video stimulus was temporally structured to separate motion-driven responses from luminance transients (Fig. 1b). Static frames during the initial and final 100 ms bracketed a 200 ms dynamic period containing the rotating object. This temporal structure allowed us to examine both the object-discrimination performance and the time course over which motion information is integrated into surface geom-etry representations. Based on this stimulus configuration, we systematically varied stimulus parameters to examine how surface geometry estimation depends on viewing conditions (Fig. 1c). Twenty unique abstract objects were presented across three sizes (6 o, 16 o, and 27 o visual angle), six texture conditions (five texture types plus an untex-tured control), and seven non-overlapping viewpoints spanning approximately 50 o of rotation. This factorial design yielded 2,520 unique video stimuli, creating a multi-dimensional parameter space for comparing behavioral sensitivities with their neural correlates. 4

2.2 Quantifying surface geometry estimation with dynamic object recognition 

Measuring perceptual sensitivity to surface geometry poses an experimental challenge: the physical structure of an object—characterized by depth, surface normals, and curvature—varies continuously across space rather than falling into discrete categories. We addressed this challenge using a match-to-sample task in which observers viewed a briefly presented target object (400 ms video clip) followed by two choice objects, one matching the target’s surface geometry and one differing (Figure 2a). Critically, surface textures always differed between the target and choice objects, requiring observers to rely on motion-revealed surfaces rather than low-level texture matching. Because no established metric quantifies the perceptual dissimilarity between arbi-trary 3D shapes, we could not titrate task difficulty along a known dimension as in classical psychophysical paradigms. Instead, we sampled broadly across object pairs and stimulus conditions, allowing difficulty to emerge empirically from a factorial design spanning 20 objects, multiple textures, and viewpoints (see Methods). Human observers (N=1063, tested online) performed well above chance across all conditions when objects rotated in video clips (Figure 2b, dynamic). On the other hand, perfor-mance dropped substantially when the same objects were presented as static images, confirming that observers (N=295; Figure 2b, static) relied on motion-defined sur-face geometry rather than residual static cues to discriminate surfaces. Condition-wise consistency with human behavioral patterns serves as our primary dependent variable for measuring the behavioral alignment of neural and model activity throughout our analyses. 

2.3 Neural decoding of object recognition in motion 

Human observers readily discriminate motion-defined shapes even under challenging texture-masking conditions, demonstrating that the visual system can extract sur-face geometry from motion cues. Where in the brain is this computation performed, and how do neural populations represent shape-from-motion? The classical division of 6

To adjudicate between these possibilities, we recorded neural activity from downstream regions of both pathways in macaque monkeys viewing the same stimulus set. 

2.3.1 Chronic recordings from downstream regions in the dorsoventral visual pathways 

In the ventral pathway, we targeted subregions of the inferior temporal (IT) cortex, which represents the culmination of the ventral pathway and is directly linked to object recognition [15, 16]. Selecting a corresponding downstream region in the dorsal pathway was less straightforward, as the neural substrates implicated in processing surface geometry are less well characterized than ventral substrates for object recog-nition. Based on previous reports, we chose the inferior parietal lobule (IPL) as a dorsal pathway counterpart to IT cortex. This decision was guided by anatomical and functional characterizations of each cortical area, relative to their respective hierarchy. Both regions occupy analogous positions within their respective pathways–hierarchical levels where visual neurons become increasingly multimodal, interfacing extensively with non-visual areas. IT cortex, while central to object recognition and category representation, connects extensively with regions processing semantic and mnemonic information [17–19]. Similarly, IPL is implicated in processing higher-order motion-based visual information while also exhibiting strong responsiveness to somatosensory inputs and auditory cues, reflecting a multimodal integration profile that mirrors IT cortex’s interface with semantic networks [20–22]. This parallel organization moti-vated our selection of IT cortex and IPL as counterparts across the dorsoventral visual pathways. In downstream regions of each respective pathway, we implanted chronic electrode arrays to record neural activity from populations across the entire stimulus set (2,520 videos). Chronic recordings were essential to our benchmark, as the large stimulus space required tracking the same neurons across multiple sessions to obtain suffi-cient trials per stimulus condition. In the ventral pathway, we targeted anterior (aIT), central (cIT), and posterior (pIT) subregions of inferior temporal cortex, spanning the hierarchical gradient from visual to increasingly semantic representations [15]. In the dorsal pathway, we targeted areas 7op (parietal operculum) and Tpt (temporo-parietal), located at the confluence of the intraparietal sulcus and lateral sulcus [23, 24]. Ventral and dorsal pathway recordings were conducted in separate animals, with ven-tral pathway recordings replicated across both hemispheres in one monkey and dorsal pathway recordings replicated across two monkeys (see Methods). Notably, extended recording stability deep within sulcal regions of the IPL (inferior parietal lobule; Sup-plemental Figure S1) represents a methodological advance, allowing us to characterize the same neural population across thousands of stimulus conditions that would be difficult to track in acute recording sessions (details for tracking the same population from raw extracellular recordings are described in Methods). 7

2.3.2 Both visual pathways carry decodable information about motion-defined surface geometry 

If surface geometry estimation were strictly segregated by pathway, we would expect robust readouts of shape information in one stream but not the other. To test this prediction, we analyzed neural readouts during the 200 ms dynamic period by applying the same 2AFC paradigm used in the human behavioral task. For each trial, we applied a distance-based decoder–the distance between neural population responses to the sample stimulus and each choice stimulus–to predict which choice stimulus depicts the same surface geometry as the sample stimulus based on the neural population pattern. This approach does not require trained parameters, providing a conservative estimate 8

Neural readouts from both IT cortex and IPL discriminated abstract objects based on their motion-defined surface geometry (Fig. 3b). Decoding accuracy emerged rapidly after motion onset and was sustained throughout the dynamic period, indicating that both pathways maintain representations of motion-defined surface geometry. 

2.3.3 Dorsal regions show greater alignment with human behavior 

Although both pathways carry decodable shape information, this does not necessarily imply equivalent contributions. We next tested whether the two pathways differ in their representations with human behavioral judgements. First, we asked which populations might approach human-level performance when scaled up in population size. A neural scaling analysis revealed that decoding accuracy increased with population size in both pathways, but with different scaling trajectories (Figure 3c). Subregions in IPL approached human behavioral performance with fewer units than IT populations, indicating that dorsal representations are more efficiently organized for surface geometry discrimination. Next, we measured the consistency between neural representations and human behavioral performance. We correlated neural decoding accuracy with human dis-crimination performance across matched stimulus conditions. IPL decoding accuracy showed stronger correlation with human behavior than IT decoding accuracy (Figure 3b; bottom row). Interestingly, the lowest behavioral correspondence was observed in aIT, the most downstream ventral region. The divergence in behavioral correspon-dence admits the possibility that downstream ventral regions may not represent the primary information driving perceptual judgments on surface estimation tasks. Over-all, these findings indicate that while both IT cortex and IPL carry information for reading out motion-defined surface geometry, dorsal parietal representations exhibit stronger linkage to human perceptual judgments. 

2.4 Computational models of estimating structure from dynamic inputs 

What computations transform motion cues into neural representations of surface geometry? To address this question, we draw from the past decade of work demon-strating strong alignment between the internal representations of some artificial neural network (ANN) models and ventral visual stream representations. To ask which, if any, video processing networks have internal functional alignment with IPL neural represen-tations, we evaluated a large model zoo including classical models of motion processing with prior neural validation [14, 25, 26] (Figure 3c; black line) and a diverse set of ANNs spanning image or video pretraining, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers (ViTs), and various training objectives. Previous benchmarking efforts have established some CNNs as the leading models of the ventral visual path-way [2]. However, the corresponding models of the dorsal pathway remain unclear, in part because systematic comparison with video stimuli has lagged behind image-based 9

Our model set spans a progression from those derived from image-based super-vised learning to those derived from video-based self-supervised learning. CNNs (282 models, 1215 total layers) process static images through hierarchical spatial filters, trained with supervised classification objectives prioritizing object categorization. Vision Transformers (ViT, 103 models, 441 total layers) replace convolutions with global attention (queries) over image patches but similarly operate on static frames with supervised training [27]. DINOv2 (4 models, 52 total layers) retains the ViT architecture but removes reliance on labels through self-supervised distillation, where a student network learns to match a teacher network’s representation across augmented views to promote generalization [28]. PerceptionEncoder (6 models, 77 total layers) extends this approach as a large-scale foundation model combining self-supervised and weakly supervised objectives to learn general-purpose embeddings for image and video understanding, though it does not incorporate temporal learning constraints [29]. The remaining model types that we tested depart from the above models as they operate on video and they were trained explicitly on temporal structure. TimeS-former (3 models, 39 total layers) extends transformers to video through factorized spatiotemporal attention, but retains supervised action classification objectives [30]. VideoMAEs (video masked auto-encoders, 10 models, 130 total layers) depart from supervised training and learn spatiotemporal structure by reconstructing masked video patches, forcing the model to internalize regularities of visual features evolving over time [31]. V-JEPA (7 models, 223 total layers) advances this approach further by predicting latent representations of masked regions rather than reconstructing pixels, emphasizing abstract spatiotemporal structure over low-level visual details [32]. This progression from supervised image classification through self-supervised image learn-ing, to self-supervised video models that learn predictive regularities in both space and time provides a framework for identifying which computational paradigms lead to models that best approximate the adult neural mechanisms of extracting surface geometry from motion. We refer to V-JEPA and VideoMAE collectively as predictive coding video mod-els , as both are trained with self-supervised objectives that require predicting masked spatiotemporal content from visible context in natural videos. We adopt this termi-nology to emphasize the shared training strategy – learning representations through prediction of withheld sensory content, including future time steps from past context – which we take to be a computationally consequential property linking these mod-els to cortical function; we do not claim architectural correspondence. Our usage is broader than the classical predictive coding framework in neuroscience [33, 34], which posits a hierarchical architecture in which top-down predictions are compared against bottom-up input and prediction errors propagate downstream. 10

2.4.1 Predictive coding video models exhibit best surface-matching performance and alignment to human behavior 

We treated model activations as candidate surrogates for neural responses by evalu-ating each model on the same match-to-sample task used for human participants and neurons by applying the same neural decoding pipeline. Model activations were inte-grated over the object-rotation period (100–300 ms from stimulus onset), matching the temporal window and spike-count integration used in neural decoding (see Methods for extracting model features). For each trial, we computed cosine distances between model responses to the sample video and each choice video (target vs. lure), with the model’s choice determined by which stimulus yielded a similar response, or a shorter distance between the two choices. Across all model architectures, downstream encoding layers in predictive coding video models achieved high match-to-sample accuracies, with middle to later layers showing peak performance (Figure 4a, b), consistent with the expectation that features relevant for shape-from-motion processing build up over the model hierarchies. To measure consistency with human behavioral judgments, the same procedure used to measure consistency between neural decoding and behavior was applied. The mean model decoding accuracy was computed for each size x sample texture x choice texture condition, and these condition-wise accuracies were correlated to the human condition-wise behavioral accuracies. Again, we find that deeper layers in predictive coding video models show the highest consistency with human behavior (Figure 4c). To directly compare the model families, we summarized the accuracy and behavioral consistency of each model using its best-performing layer (Figure 4c). 2.4.2 Predictive coding video models approach neural readouts of surface-from-motion stimuli 

Next, we examined which model types show strongest consistency between human behavioral patterns and neural readouts from different cortical regions. We extended the previous decoding analysis by organizing neural readout patterns and behavioral patterns in terms of models (Figure 5). Models that align with both human psy-chophysical patterns and neural readout pattern constrain the space of candidate computations underlying surface processing in each cortical region. The ventral pathway revealed a more graded pattern along its hierarchy within IT cortex. While predictive coding video models maintained their advantage in upstream ventral regions (pIT, cIT), this pattern reversed in the most downstream region (aIT), where CNN architectures achieved the highest model-neural consistency while alternative architectures systematically diverged. This dissociation suggests that the computations underlying motion-based surface estimation may be differentially dis-tributed across visual pathways, with downstream dorsal regions more closely aligned to surface geometry and downstream ventral regions retaining stronger correspondence to image-based (static) form processing. 2.5 Encoding analyses: computations transforming pixels to representations of surface-from-motion 

Having established that predictive coding video models best match patterns of human behavioral performance and neural decoding performance on our shape-from-motion task, we next asked whether these same models provide the closest correspondence to neural representations. Unlike decoding approaches that infer stimulus properties from neural activity, encoding models provide forward mappings from stimulus fea-tures to predicted neural responses, offering mechanistic descriptions of candidate transformations from visual input to spiking activity [35, 36]. A fundamental challenge in characterizing these transformations is obtaining neu-ral data spanning sufficient stimulus conditions relative to the intrinsic dimensionality of the representational space. Regression-based encoding requires that the number of unique stimuli be commensurate with the number of independent features needed to characterize neural response patterns—a quantity that remains unknown a priori but is likely high given accumulating evidence for high-dimensional population codes [1, 37]. This constraint motivated our use of chronic arrays to track neurons across multiple recording sessions, maximizing the number of unique stimulus conditions per neuron. We present two complementary encoding analyses: univariate prediction of indi-vidual unit responses, which measures shared feature selectivity between model units and single neurons, and multivariate prediction preserving population geometry, which captures the representational structure that can be utilized for downstream readout. 

2.5.1 Univariate analysis – predicting individual unit responses 

We first evaluated models using an established approach for predicting single-neuron responses, extending methods validated on static images to our dynamic video stimuli. Following conventions in neural benchmarking [1, 2], we used Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression to map model features onto individual neural units. (see Appendix S5.1 for details). 13

Information critical for decoding–pairwise distances between stimulus representations and geometric structure–may be invisible to unit-by-unit prediction yet essential for population-level computation. To complement our univariate analysis, we mapped model populations to neu-ral populations using an approach that aligns coordinated activity with respect to their geometric structure embedded in population responses (detailed procedure is described in Supplement S5.2). Rather than predicting individual neurons indepen-dently, we quantified population-level alignment using Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA). CKA emphasizes the high-variance directions that dominate each population’s response, and its values track how well one population can be linearly decoded from the other. This favors CKA as a suitable metric for assessing how geometric alignment could be exploited in downstream linear readouts, complementing the unit-by-unit perspective of encoding models. We re-applied the same hold-out policy used in the previous section, as well as quantifying population similarity based on the same inte-grated responses during the dynamic period (100-300 ms), treating neural and model responses equivalently while accounting for region-specific response latencies. Comparing models by CKA similarity revealed patterns that both confirmed and extended the univariate findings (Figure 7). First, predictive video models achieved the highest geometric alignment with neural populations in both IT cortex and IPL subregions. Second, the advantage of predictive video models over PerceptionEn-coder – a contrastive vision–language model trained on a curated pool of 5.4 billion image–text pairs (MetaCLIP) with 86 billion total samples seen, plus 22 million sam-ples of video–text finetuning, substantially exceeding the data scale of any other model evaluated here – was more pronounced for population geometry than for single-unit predictivity. Third, the two analyses revealed distinct layer-wise profiles across model classes. For predictive video models, population-level alignment accelerated at ear-lier stages in the hierarchy than the later stage acceleration observed in univariate analyses. In other model classes, alignment declined at downstream layers in both analyses, but the decline was more pronounced in terms of population geometry than for single-unit predictivity, indicating that geometric correspondence with neural rep-resentations degrades faster than univariate predictivity in the deeper layers of these models. Together, these findings indicate that predictive video training objectives led to population geometry that aligns with neural representations in ways that single-unit predictivity, and other model classes, do not capture. A supplementary analysis performed across time series, rather than analyzing a single integrated response pat-tern, yielded consistent rank ordering of model types, indicating that our assessments are robust to how temporal information is aggregated(Supplementary Figure S6). Overall, both univariate and multivariate analyses converged on predictive coding video models as top performers, strengthening our conclusions about computations of extracting surface-from-motion. 2.6 Establishing leading models of downstream areas of the dorsal pathway 

The preceding sections evaluated models of surface-from-motion processing along four complementary measures: behavioral accuracy and behavioral consistency with human observers, single-site neural predictivity, and population-level representational geometry. Across these measures, the convergence of behavioral patterns with neu-ral readouts in IPL identifies these downstream dorsal regions as more closely linked to human judgments of surface geometry than regions downstream of the ventral visual pathway. To provide a computational account of these behaviorally-relevant regions, we consolidate encoding and decoding analyses on unseen conditions. For each region, we ask which models best predict neural responses on unseen conditions, and whether the behavioral judgements decoded from those predicted responses match neural read-outs. Our approach was as follows. 16

We employed two alignment procedures corresponding to our two encoding analyses: unit-wise PLS regression (2.5.1) and population-level CKA optimization (2.5.2). In both cases, we partitioned the 90 unique conditions into training sets (75 conditions) and test sets (15 conditions) according to object identity and surface tex-ture, ensuring that held-out stimuli comprised objects and textures never encountered during the model-to-neuron mapping. When evaluated on encoding fidelity alone (y-axes, Figure 8), predictive coding video models (VideoMAE and V-JEPA) achieved the highest alignment with neural responses in downstream regions of both dorsal and ventral visual pathways. However, jointly evaluating encoding fidelity and readout correspondence reveals a pathway dis-sociation. In dorsal regions, predictive coding video models generalize on both metrics, achieving encoding and decoding alignment that closely approximate the recorded neural populations. In downstream ventral regions, by contrast, these same models achieve comparably high encoding alignment but produce behavioral readouts that diverge markedly from those of biological neurons. This dissociation – high encoding similarity paired with poor decoding correspondence – raises a possibility that the ven-tral pathway engages distinct computational strategies for surface-from-motion that are not captured by our model zoo, warranting further investigation. Together, the convergence of behavioral prediction and neural encoding fidelity supports a specific computational insight. Predictive coding objectives (predicting masked stimulus features or embedded features across space and time) selectively align models toward downstream dorsal responses, establishing a mechanistic baseline for processing surface information in dynamic inputs. In other words, such training objectives promote representations that compress statistical redundancies in dynamic scenes that preserve geometric structure while discounting nuisance variables such as surface texture. Our results suggest predictive coding video models as a new baseline for computational accounts of the dorsal visual pathway, providing the first stimulus-computable models whose internal representations jointly approximate both neural population geometry and behavioral readout in dorso-parietal cortex during surface-from-motion estimation. 17

# 3 Discussion 

We investigated how the brain estimates surface geometry from motion cues by com-bining human psychophysics, macaque neurophysiology, and computational modeling. Using texture-masked stimuli that isolate motion-based surface perception–challenging for computer vision models yet readily perceived by humans–we report three principal findings. First, both visual pathways contain explicit (linearly decodable) surface geometry representations. Contrary to strict functional segregation, downstream regions in both dorsal (IPL) and ventral (IT cortex) pathways supported robust decoding of object identity from motion-defined shape. This finding challenges the classical view that motion processing engages primarily dorsal structures while shape representations reside predominantly in the ventral visual pathway. Instead, our results indicate that surface geometry information is distributed across both streams. Second, downstream dorsal representations show preferential correspondence to surface geometry and behavior. Although both pathways carry shape information, IPL populations exhibited stronger correspondence to human behavioral performance than IT populations. This asymmetry suggests that IPL representations are more directly linked to perceptual decisions about surface geometry, possibly reflecting a causal role, even though shape information is present in both. Third, our model evaluation identifies a candidate baseline model for the dorsal visual pathway. A notable asymmetry exists in modeling visual processes–while CNNs trained on object classification have become established baseline models of ventral 18

It is The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted May 18, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.05.13.724755 doi: bioRxiv preprint stream processing, no comparable consensus exists for the dorsal pathway. Classical motion energy models capture local motion processing in V1 and MT [25, 26], but do not extend to downstream parietal regions involved in 3D surface geometry. More recent efforts have explored action-recognition networks or geometry-aware architec-tures, yet none have achieved the benchmark status that CNNs hold for mirroring ventral stream representations. Our findings suggest that predictive coding video mod-els – with training objectives for predicting spatial and temporal features – may fill this gap. Among the diverse models evaluated, V-JEPA and VideoMAE models achieved the highest alignment with neural activity in both pathways, with preferential corre-spondence to dorsal regions, and the closest match to human behavioral performance. This preferential alignment with downstream dorsal areas positions predictive coding video models as candidate baselines for cortical processing in downstream stages of the dorsal visual hierarchy. Beyond their empirical alignment, predictive coding video models offer theoret-ical and practical insights for computational modeling of vision. A distinguishing feature of V-JEPA and VideoMAE, relative to others in our model zoo, is their train-ing objective: learning to predict/compress spatiotemporal regularities from natural videos without explicit supervision using annotated data. This objective resonates with predictive coding frameworks in neuroscience, which propose that the brain learns internal models to anticipate incoming sensory input [33, 39]. Under this view, the dorsal pathway may be understood not merely as a motion-processing system, but as a pathway specialized for processing regularities that support geometric inference in dynamic scenes. Notably, predictive coding models trained on substantially smaller datasets surpassed large-scale foundation models, suggesting that training objectives emphasizing temporal prediction could matter more than dataset scale–both for dis-criminating arbitrary object shapes and for capturing cortical representations for processing surface geometry. The dissociation between model scale and neural align-ment has practical implications for computational modeling. Learning objectives that capture generative or causal structure in natural videos may be a productive direction for developing brain-like representations in artificial systems, potentially bestowing human-like efficiencies relevant to complex scene understanding. Although our investigation provides new insights, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, we recorded from passively viewing animals, leaving unclear how these representations relate to those engaged during active behavior. We assumed that surface geometry representations emerge automatically regardless of task demands or viewing conditions [43], and, indeed, we observed that the visual evoked IPL neurally representations appear sufficient to explain human judgements at reason-able numbers of neurons. Nevertheless, it remains possible that even more advanced functional representations may arise in IPL when behavioral affordances and action requirements are varied [6, 7]. Second, our cortical coverage was limited, and dorsal and ventral regions were recorded in separate animals. The persistent gap between neural decoding and human behavior suggests that our recordings capture only part of a broader network supporting shape-from-motion. Finally, our analyses focused on representations that generalize across surface textures at matched viewpoints. # Methods 

Behavioral task design. We quantified perceptual sensitivity to motion-defined sur-face geometry using a match-to-sample task embedded in a 2AFC framework. On each trial, observers viewed a sample video (400 ms, 60 fps) depicting a rotating, texture-masked object, followed by two choice videos (also 400 ms each, looped until response). One choice depicted the same object as the sample and the other a different object; surface textures were always swapped between sample and choice stimuli, ensuring that correct responses required extracting shape from motion cues rather than match-ing low-level image features. This design grounds behavioral measurement in signal detection theory [44], enabling well-characterized metrics of perceptual sensitivity. Our approach departs from classical psychophysical paradigms such as orienta-tion discrimination or contrast detection, in which experimenters systematically vary a physical parameter (e.g., angular difference, stimulus contrast) to map stimulus manipulations onto perceptual thresholds. No analogous metric exists for the percep-tual dissimilarity between arbitrary 3D shapes, precluding precise a priori control of task difficulty. We therefore adopted a broad-sampling strategy: rather than titrat-ing difficulty along a single dimension, we constructed a factorial design crossing 20 unique object geometries with multiple surface textures, viewpoints, and object sizes. The full combinatorial space of object pairs, sizes, textures, and viewpoints was prohibitively large; we defined 90 conditions based on object size, sample texture, and choice texture, then sampled across object pairs and viewpoints within each condition. This design allows task difficulty to emerge empirically from the stimulus space rather than being imposed by assumption. Behavioral data were collected from online participants (N=1063) who completed the task in a web browser (Figure 2a). Human observers performed well above chance across all 90 conditions (Figure 2b). 

Target sites for electrophysiology recordings. We recorded extracellular activity using chronic implants in both visual pathways. In the ventral pathway, we recorded from inferior temporal (IT) cortex. Three Blackrock Utah arrays (96 channels each) were implanted on the cortical surface targeting anterior (aIT), central (cIT), and posterior (pIT) inferior temporal cortex. In one male rhesus macaque (Macaca mulatta), we collected IT responses in the left hemisphere and repeated the same experiment in the right hemisphere of the same animal to verify our results. Across both hemispheres, all arrays were placed on the surface cortex below the superior tem-poral sulcus (STS). Approximate stereotactic coordinates are defined by a David Kopf macaque stereotax and guided by macaque atlases [23, 24]: (a) aIT: +12 mm rostral to ear bar zero (EBZ)/+2 mm dorsal to EBZ, (b) cIT: +7 mm rostral/+4mm dorsal to EBZ, and (c) pIT: +2mm rostral/+6 mm dorsal to EBZ. In the dorsal pathway, a single Modular Bionics N-Form array was implanted to simultaneously target two subregions of the inferior parietal lobule (IPL) – area 7op 20

Second, we used a NeuralGlider Inserter (by Acutated Neuroscience, Inc.) to reduce cortical tissue dimpling and aids array insertion via ultrasonic micro-vibration. 

Recordings from IT cortex (ventral visual pathway). Recordings were obtained from both hemispheres across two rounds of recording sessions per hemi-sphere (4 sessions total per region), yielding populations of visually driven neurons in three subregions along the posterior-to-anterior axis: posterior IT (pIT; n = 82 units), central IT (cIT; n = 107 units), and anterior IT (aIT; n = 137 units). During each session, the animal performed passive fixation while 2,520 surface-from-motion video stimuli were presented 400ms, and spatially centered to the center of the population receptive fields ( fovea, see S2). Peri-stimulus time histograms (PSTHs) were computed in 10 ms bins spanning -200 to 600 ms relative to stimulus onset (80 time bins). To increase population size, units from all four recording sessions were aggregated by concatenation along the unit dimension, with up to 24 repeated presentations per stimulus. For sessions with fewer than 24 repeats, missing entries were padded with NaN values, and the mean response was estimated across available repeats (minimum of 20 repeats). 

Recording from IPL (dorsal visual pathway). Extracellular voltage preprocessing. Raw extracellular voltages were acquired using the Intan RHD2000 recording system at a sampling rate of 30 kHz. Because the full stimulus set (2,520 videos) required presentation across multiple recording sessions spanning days to weeks, we developed a stitching procedure to concatenate raw voltage traces from all sessions into a single continuous binary file per cortical region. Each session’s sample count was verified against the corresponding timestamp record to ensure sample-level integrity, and cumulative indexing was maintained to preserve the temporal registration of each session within the aggregate trace. This stitching step produced one C×T matrix per region, where C is the number of recording channels and T is the total number of samples across all recording sessions, serving as the input to spike sorting. Spike sorting was performed using Kilosort 2.0 [ ? ] on the stitched binary files. Region-specific channel maps reflecting the physical geometry of each array were provided to Kilosort. Sorted results were exported to the Phy template, a graphical user interface, for visual inspection. We applied no additional data inclusion criteria beyond the default quality metrics provided by Kilosort. All units were retained for analysis provided that their spike waveforms exhibited biophysically plausible extracellular signatures (e.g., characteris-tic negative–positive deflection shape and consistent amplitude). Units were excluded only when waveform inspection revealed clear mechanical or electrical noise artifacts. 

Distance-based linear decoder. To assess the discriminability of object representations in neural population activity, we employed a distance-based match-to-sample (M2S) decoder. On each trial, the decoder was presented with a sample stimulus defined by a particular object identity, texture, viewpoint, and size. The decoder compared the neural population response to the sample against two alter-natives: a choice stimulus depicting the same object in a different texture and same viewpoint, and a lure stimulus depicting a different object rendered in the same tex-ture as the choice. The decoder classified the sample as the object whose population response was closest in cosine distance: 

d(r1, r2) = 1 − r1 · r2

|r1|, |r2|

where r1 and r2 are population response vectors (firing rates across all recorded units). The decoder selected the choice over the lure when 

d(rsample , rchoice ) < d (rsample , rlure ), yielding a two-alternative forced-choice accuracy for each trial. 

Logistic regression classifier . As an alternative readout, we trained an L2-regularized logistic regression classifier to decode object identity or surface geometry information that persists different textures. The classifier was evaluated using a leave-one-texture-out cross-validation scheme, in which models were trained on responses to all but one texture and tested on the held-out texture, ensuring that training and testing stimuli were never identical. We performed both 20-way multiclass clas-sification (all objects simultaneously) and pairwise binary classification (all object pairs). The same training and testing time windows were used as in the M2S analysis. 22

This classifier-based approach yielded decoding accuracies highly consistent with the distance-based M2S decoder, confirming that the reported object discriminability is robust to the choice of readout method (Supplemental Figure S3). 

Applying linear decoders to neural responses across time. The choice and lure representations were constructed from a training time window by averaging firing rates across stimulus repetitions, while the sample response was drawn from individual repetitions within a separate testing time window. We evaluated three training windows (0-100 ms , 100-300 ms , and 0-400 ms relative to stimulus onset) while sliding the 100 ms testing window in 20 ms steps from -100 ms to 600 ms . All firing rates were latency-adjusted to account for response onset differences across brain regions. Decoding accuracy was computed for each trial by averaging over valid stim-ulus repetitions, and overall accuracy was obtained by averaging across all object, texture, viewpoint, and size combinations. 

Extracting features from stimulus-computable models. For each model, we extracted features from the dynamic frames of each stimulus video (12 frames spanning the 100–300 ms motion period of a 60 fps video; onset and offset transients in the first and last 6 static frames were discarded). For image-computable models, features were extracted independently for each frame. For video-computable models, all 12 dynamic frames were provided as a single input and spatiotemporal features were extracted accordingly. When a video model’s expected context length exceeded 12 frames, we symmetrically padded the first and last frames to match the required length. All input preprocessing (pixel normalization, spatial resizing) followed the inference procedures specified by each model’s authors. Across all architectures, we restricted our analyses to activations from feedforward encoding layers spanning each model’s hierarchy, excluding auxiliary components such as predictor networks in V-JEPA or decoder networks in VideoMAE. To obtain a common representational format, we applied a two-stage dimensionality reduction pipeline uniformly across models. In the first stage, PCA (250 components) was fit on features extracted from an independent set of shape-from-motion stimuli comprising different object geometries and surface textures than those used in the main experi-ment. In the second stage, the learned PCA projections were applied to features from all experimental videos. This projection preserved a substantial amount of geometric structure of frame-by-frame time series (see Supplement S6 for further details). Fea-tures were extracted across a range of layers spanning each model’s full hierarchy to enable layer-resolved comparisons with neural data.

# S2 Receptive fields estimated from each target region 

Receptive fields (RFs) were mapped for each recorded unit using a moving bar paradigm adapted from Viswanathan & Nieder (2017) [46]. to 12 o, at 3 o intervals), covering 135 unique locations. At each grid position, the bar was swept across the location along multiple directions and orientations; the evoked spiking response at each position was computed by integrating spike counts over the stimulus presentation window after stimulus onset, with respect to the neural unit’s response latency. # S4 Partial Least Squares (PLS) regression-based mapping 

PLS regression discovers a low-dimensional basis by iteratively extracting components that maximize covariance between feature and response spaces. The resulting weight matrix WP LS = [ w1, w 2, ..., w k] consists of basis vectors ordered by their contribution to explained variance, where each component wi is orthogonal by design. This super-vised dimensionality reduction explicitly targets variance relevant to the observed neurons, typically achieving high prediction accuracy with relatively few components. The learned basis effectively filters out feature dimensions uncorrelated with neural responses, implementing an efficient compression that prioritizes neuron-by-neuron predictive power. 29

S5.2 Centered Kernal Alignment (CKA)-optimized mapping 

PLS-based mapping (unit-wise explained-variance) alone is insufficient for characteriz-ing geometric properties embedded in coordinated activity. For example, populations can achieve similar explained variance across neurons (average explained-variance), while exhibiting fundamentally different population-level geometric properties ([38]; also see Supplemental Figure S4). This is because PLS-regression finds linear combi-nations of model response to maximize variance explained across stimulus conditions, but does not necessarily preserve geometric signal embedded in population activity as the learned basis for mapping units are not rotation-invariant – in this scenario, geometrical similarities from linear regression is an artifact, not an objective. On the other hand, a CKA-based optimization approach instead finds a linear mapping W by maximizing the alignment between mapped artificial neural network features ( XW ) and biological neural responses ( Y ), where the optimization criterion is based on geometrical similarity, such as CKA (XW, Y ). Using debiased linear CKA [47] to handle the high-dimensional, low-sample regime typical of neuroscience data, we optimize for a weight matrix WCKA that preserves the inner product structure between all pairs of neural population responses. This approach maintains pairwise distances and angles between activity patterns across conditions or stimuli. Unlike PLS, this method is unsupervised with respect to individual neuron identities—it preserves the geometric structure of population responses rather than targeting specific neural predictions. The resulting basis typically produces denser mappings than PLS solutions, as it must maintain all geometric relationships rather than just those relevant for prediction. The distinct algebraic structures above reflect a fundamental trade-off in neural system identification, particularly relevant when analyzing different response charac-teristics, e.g., visual versus motor cortices [1, 38, 48]. While regression-based mapping produces sparse, low-rank mappings optimal for predicting individual neural responses, geometry-based mapping preserves the relational structure determining how neural populations distinguish between stimulus—wise pairwise distances, angles, and mani-fold geometry that may be critical for neural readouts in downstream areas of a brain region. Empirical comparisons using split-half reliability as noise ceilings reveal that while PLS may achieve higher explained variance for individual neurons, CKA-based mappings better preserve coordinated activity within a neural population (correlation structure). The choice between these approaches should align with the scientific ques-tion: PLS for maximizing prediction accuracy of individual unit responses, CKA-based optimization for discovering transformations that preserve the geometric structure underlying neural computation. The dissociation between unit-level prediction accuracy and population-level geo-metric structure parallels recent findings in the neural network interpretability literature. Melander and colleagues demonstrate that hidden units with similar acti-vation magnitudes can exert fundamentally different causal contributions to network output, and that decomposing these contributions at the population level reveals 31

The choice of retaining the first 250 components may appear arbitrary; here we provide a simulation-based justification (Supplemental Figure S7). Model features extracted from video foundation models (e.g., V-JEPA2) can occupy feature spaces with millions of dimensions, far exceeding the number of simul-taneously recorded neurons. To bring these representations into a commensurate dimensionality while preserving as much geometric structure as possible, we considered widely-used dimensionality reduction methods to seek which method might lead to a 34

To assess how well this procedure preserves temporal geometric structure, we con-ducted a series of controlled simulations. We generated random geodesic trajectories on the 2-sphere ( S2 for mathematical convenience), providing ground-truth structure with known geometry. Each 3-D time point was embedded into a high dimensional space (e.g., 100,000) via a random orthogonal projection with additive Gaussian noise (e.g., σ = 0.1), mimicking the high-dimensional aspect of model features. We then applied PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP to features at each time point, reducing back to a low-dimensional space while keeping the same number of time points, exactly as in our analysis pipeline. We quantified geometric fidelity using two metrics: (1) Spear-man correlation between pairwise distances in the original and reduced spaces, and (2) Procrustes alignment score between the original and reduced trajectories (after optimal rotation, scaling, and translation). We swept across the number of retained PCA components (k = 5, 10, ... 1,000). Both metrics improved rapidly with increasing k and began to plateau around k = 100–250. At k = 250, PCA achieved high pairwise distance correlation and Procrustes scores, substantially outperforming t-SNE and UMAP. Geometric fidelity was robust to the number of time points examined and degraded only at high noise levels ( σ > 0.3), consistent with a signal-to-noise regime well within what is expected for pretrained model features (even with moderate dropout and batch-normalization stochasticity; we disabled dropout during model evaluation). Taken together, these simulations indicate that retaining 250 PCA components strikes a practical balance. It preserves the dominant geometry of high-dimensional feature trajectories, reduces dimensionality by several orders of magnitude, and yields a feature space that is commensurate in size with recorded neural populations. We therefore use k = 250 throughout our analyses. 35
