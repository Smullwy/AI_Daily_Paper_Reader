1 Abstract 

2 Clinical adoption of new biomedical techniques depends on establishing reference 3 values against which individual patients can be compared. In resting-state functional 4 MRI (rsfMRI), most biomarker research has relied on the case-control paradigm, whose 5 underlying assumptions are often invalid as diseases are frequently heterogeneous, 6 limiting biomarker generalizability. Normative modeling offers a complementary 7 alternative by characterizing individual deviations against a reference population. 8 However, in rsfMRI, normative modeling has been applied almost exclusively to 9 functional connectivity, with limited attention to age trajectories and sex effects. We 10 address these gaps by developing a spatial normative model of four rsfMRI metrics that 11 capture complementary features of the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal 12 across age and sex. Five publicly available datasets were aggregated to form a sample 13 of 1,978 participants aged 10-30 years. Four metrics were computed for each of 110 14 grey matter regions: amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), fractional 15 amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF), regional homogeneity (ReHo), and 16 Hurst exponent. A machine-learning model based on hierarchical Bayesian regression 17 with a non-Gaussian likelihood was fitted per metric, modeling non-linear age effects, 18 sex, and multi-site acquisition. Models were well calibrated across all four metrics, with 19 fALFF showing the strongest predictive performance and Hurst exponent the weakest. 20 Normative trajectories varied across brain regions for each metric, but on average, the 21 median of each distribution remained bounded across regions, while the spread was 22 more regionally variable. All four metrics showed predominantly negative slopes with 23 age, indicating a decrease in each metric over the age window. 28 Introduction 

29 An important aspect of biomedical research is defining the clinical relevance of new 30 techniques to encourage clinical adoption. This requires the establishment of expected 31 reference baseline values to understand if a specific patient falls within a reference 32 range. The case-control paradigm provides a method to investigate differences between 33 the ‘average’ patient and a control group, providing group-level effect sizes as outcomes 34 that are relatively simple to interpret and well understood across clinical and research 35 environments [1]. This approach has become popular due to design and implementation 36 simplicity, and cost-effectiveness [1]. However, this approach can inherently omit 37 details, potentially hiding meaningful biological subtypes, individual patterns, or outliers 38 within each group [2]. Furthermore, the case-control approach assumes that the groups 39 being compared are internally homogeneous and distinguishable from one another 40 [2,3]. Nevertheless, this assumption is often questionable as diseases are frequently 41 heterogeneous [2,4,5], meaning two individuals can have the same diagnosis, yet 42 different symptoms [4,6]. 43 In response to the limitations, emerging approaches, such as normative modeling, a 44 statistical framework to understand population heterogeneity, are now being considered. 45 This framework defines a model that captures ‘normal’ variation in one or more 46 biological or clinical measures within the population to establish a ‘normative’ frame of 47 reference [2,7]. Normative modeling is a powerful tool because it can capture individual 48 variations, allowing for the understanding of heterogeneity trends thereby enabling 49 personalized assessments [7-9]. Furthermore, normative modeling represents a 50 paradigm shift in disease conceptualization, framing disorders as significant deviations

It is a widely used technique due 56 to its experimental simplicity: it does not require a study paradigm and involves minimal 57 participant engagement, making it highly flexible [15,16]5/28/2026 8:37:13 AM. rsfMRI 58 has become a valuable tool for identifying biomarkers of neurological and psychiatric 59 disorders [17,18], which are increasingly recognized as having a biological basis, yet 60 continue to be diagnosed exclusively through behavioral criteria [19,20]. Most research 61 has relied mainly on functional connectivity (FC), regional homogeneity (ReHo), 62 amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), and fractional ALFF (fALFF) [21]. 63 Regardless of the rsfMRI-derived metric used, the case-control paradigm has remained 64 the predominant framework for biomarker discovery across various diseases [18]. 65 It is relevant to note that the case-control approach has several shortcomings, 66 further reflected in the search for biomarkers using rsfMRI. For instance, most studies 67 are characterized by relatively small sample sizes, heterogeneous populations, limited 68 generalizability of findings, and proposed biomarkers with low disease specificity, as 69 demonstrated by several meta-analyses [20,22-25]. Thus, while the case-control 70 paradigm in rsfMRI offers value, it has faced challenges in producing clinically 71 translatable biomarkers [5,18]. Normative modeling has been investigated as an 72 alternative approach to address these challenges. However, only a limited number of 73 studies have applied normative models to rsfMRI [19,26,27].

74 Beyond the scarcity of normative rsfMRI studies, large gaps in our understanding of 75 brain health and function exist. A key gap is characterizing the healthy brain across the 76 lifespan, as functional changes occur throughout life, with particularly pronounced shifts 77 occurring during early development and in older age [28,29]. Furthermore, sex 78 differences across the lifespan also affect many aspects of brain function that may or 79 may not be regionally specific [30,31]. The omission of age and sex effects in the 80 normative model design, an exclusive focus on FC analyses, and relatively small 81 sample sizes persist in fMRI normative modeling research. Moreover, while FC is a 82 widely used measure of brain function, it captures only a single feature of the BOLD 83 signal. Numerous complementary features can also be extracted from the BOLD signal 84 in rsfMRI [32]. Incorporating these diverse characteristics into normative modeling 85 frameworks could support a more comprehensive and individualized characterization of 86 deviations in brain function [27,33]. 87 The field of brain rsfMRI research lacks a comprehensive understanding of 88 normative modeling which includes sex and age, and other features of the BOLD signal 89 [11,33], creating a research gap. Therefore, the main objective of this research was to 90 develop a spatially normative model of rsfMRI that incorporates several features of the 91 BOLD signal, enhancing the characterization of biological heterogeneity of brain 92 function. Moreover, we argue that thoroughly characterizing normative populations is a 93 critical prerequisite to studying disease. Establishing a reference framework could allow 94 individual-level predictions, as normative modeling conceptualizes disease as a 95 deviation from this normative baseline [5,19]. 96

98 Data description 

99 All data were pooled under local ethics approval, in addition to the ethical guidelines 100 of each database and corresponding sites accessed. Five publicly available datasets 101 (ABIDE-I [34], ABIDE-II [35], ADHD200 [36], FCP [37], and INDI [38]) were aggregated 102 to create a larger rsfMRI sample. Detailed information for each database can be found 103 in their respective publications. Beyond the specific criteria of each database, inclusion 104 in the aggregated sample required participants aged 10-30 years, with available 105 structural MRI and rsfMRI scans collected in 3T systems, and basic demographic data 106 (age and sex). Participants aged 10-30 were chosen because this age range is common 107 in neuroimaging research and in existing open-access datasets [39,40]. Furthermore, 108 this age window has shown spatiotemporal changes in brain functional organization, 109 with network-specific maturation peaking during adolescence and extending into early 110 adulthood [41,42]. 111 Participants with corrupted files or missing demographics were excluded from this 112 study. To prevent participant duplication across databases, each dataset was cross-113 referenced against known repository overlaps documented in the literature, and a 114 unified participant registry was maintained throughout data curation. For datasets with 115 longitudinal or multiple acquisitions, only the baseline session was pooled. 116 Data processing

119 rsfMRI guidelines [21,32]. Briefly, analysis involved deletion of the first five functional 120 volumes, slice timing and motion correction, spatial smoothing (Gaussian kernel, FWHM 121 = 5mm), 4D global normalization, brain extraction, spatial normalization to the MNI152 122 T1 2mm template, and high-pass temporal filtering (0.01Hz). Quality assessment of 123 preprocessed data included visual inspection of the raw data, co-registration, and 124 assessment of spatial normalization accuracy. Subjects with excessive head motion 125 (mean framewise displacement, FD > 0.5mm) were excluded from further analysis to 126 minimize its impact on signal quality [48]. 127 Following preprocessing, four rsfMRI metrics describing different features of the 128 BOLD signal were computed for each subject: (1) amplitude of low-frequency 129 fluctuations (ALFF) and (2) fractional ALFF (fALFF), depicting the frequency-based 130 intensity of the BOLD signal; (3) regional homogeneity (ReHo), indicative of local 131 connectivity; and (4) Hurst exponent, reflecting BOLD signal autocorrelation. Specific 132 additional preprocessing steps were applied to each metric as required [16,32]. ALFF, 133 fALFF, and ReHo maps were retained in their raw units, without applying typical within-134 subject normalization. After processing, each rsfMRI parametric map was parcellated 135 into 110 grey matter regions of interest (ROIs) using the Harvard-Oxford cortical and 136 subcortical atlases, and the mean value within each ROI was extracted and used as the 137 input feature for normative modeling. 138 Data analysis

278 This research estimated spatial normative trajectories of four common rsfMRI 279 metrics (ALFF, fALFF, ReHo, and Hurst exponent) that reflect age- and sex-related 280 variation across brain regions, while accounting for MRI scan-related effects. Sex was 281 included as a linear coefficient on the mean, without interacting with the age B-spline.

339 The HBR-SHASH model used in this research is a robust approach that allows to (1) 340 model non-Gaussian distributions [52,60]; (2) estimate predictive uncertainty [2]; (3) 341 perform multi-site modeling [50]; and (4) use federated (i.e., decentralized) model 342 learning [33,51]. Nevertheless, this approach has some disadvantages worth 343 mentioning. 344 First, the models are estimated independently for each response variable (i.e., per 345 brain region), so inter-regional correlations are ignored [51]. For instance, these 346 normative models could omit local spatial patterns in ReHo, as it reflects local BOLD 347 synchrony between a voxel and its neighbors. Moreover, HBR requires prior 348 distributions over model parameters as part of the Bayesian inference pipeline [53]. 349 Normative modeling has been predominantly applied to structural neuroimaging [59,74-

Therefore, we used weakly informative 352 priors guided by a preliminary characterization of rsfMRI metrics in a small dataset [32]. 353 This approach constrains the posterior distribution from improbable parameter values 354 [53] while still being flexible [51]. Nonetheless, the model could potentially underperform 355 compared to strongly informative priors, particularly for sites with small sample sizes. 356 Another limitation is related to using a B-spline basis function. A relatively narrow 357 age window was used to build normative trajectories of rsfMRI metrics, using a B-spline 358 basis expansion with 3 degrees and 3 knots to model non-linear effects of age on these 359 metrics. The choice of degrees and knots in a B-spline basis is inherently arbitrary, with 360 no consensus on their specification. This issue can be understood with the bias-361 variance trade-off: too few knots or low polynomial degrees risk underfitting the data, 362 producing a model with high bias but low variance that fails to capture non-linear 363 behaviours. Contrarily, too many knots or high degrees risk overfitting, yielding models 364 with low bias but high variance that are more sensitive to noise than signal [78,79]. 365 Moreover, a single B-spline configuration applied uniformly across brain regions may be 366 inappropriate. Previous research has shown intra-regional differences in the 367 developmental trajectories of FC across age, showing different patterns: U-shaped, 368 inverted U-shaped, and positive or negative linear trends [80,81]. A comprehensive 369 investigation on the effects of age and model flexibility is needed to properly understand 370 the linearity or non-linearity of rsfMRI metrics across brain regions. 371 An additional key limitation is the definition and setup of batch effects. In practice, multi-site neuroimaging studies treat the site 380 as the main batch effect, as each site typically has unique and consistent hardware and 381 acquisition parameters [59,75,76,84]. This impedes the use of more categories as batch effects, 384 potentially leaving residual non-biological variance in the data. In such cases, we 385 believe the most viable option is the use of the site as the main batch effect, yet it is 386 important to recognize this is a limitation, as rsfMRI metrics have been shown to be 387 sensitive to acquisition parameters [85,86]. We developed four spatial normative frameworks of rsfMRI metrics 397 (ALFF, fALFF, ReHo, and Hurst exponent), each capturing a distinct aspect of the 398 BOLD signal, as a function of age and sex, accounting for MRI scan-related effects. 399 However, this work is broad but not comprehensive. Other rsfMRI metrics can 400 provide additional features of the BOLD signal. Further inclusion of more metrics would 401 enrich the multifaceted characterization of the BOLD signal. Moreover, this initial 402 framework focused on a relatively narrow age window with an unbalanced sex ratio. 403 Further work is needed to characterize the normative trajectories across a wider 404 lifespan and more balanced sex ratios, which would allow further investigation of age-405 and sex-related effects on the BOLD signal. We believe these future directions are 406 becoming increasingly feasible with the growing availability of rsfMRI databases. Future 407 work should also investigate how different rsfMRI metrics, when used jointly within a 408 normative framework, can provide complementary information on brain function. 409 Overall, this normative characterization of rsfMRI metrics in young population 410 provides a reference across several features of the BOLD signal that indirectly reflect 411 brain function. The development of these frameworks has the potential to improve the 412 understanding of brain function while providing a reference to assess heterogeneity and 413 individual deviations. Future work should apply these models to clinical cohorts and link 414 individual deviations to clinically relevant outcomes to establish their importance to 415 disease. 416
