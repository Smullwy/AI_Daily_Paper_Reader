ABSTRACT 38 Motor planning entails a progressive transformation of neural representations—from abstract motor 39 goals, which represent intended action-outcomes independent of any particular effector (i.e., the body 40 part executing the action), to effector-specific movement plans. Functional MRI (fMRI) studies have shown 41 that parametric variations in parietal activity patterns reflect the encoding of intended force intensities in 42 effector-specific regions, even before detailed movement parameters are specified. However, how these 43 intended force intensities are initially represented in an abstract, effector-independent format and 44 subsequently transformed into effector- and movement-specific plans remains unclear. To address this, 45 human participants performed a delayed grip-force task during fMRI. They first prepared two of four 46 possible force intensities, then received a cue indicating which hand should apply which force, and finally 47 executed both grips simultaneously. Using time-resolved support vector regression (SVR) combined with a 48 searchlight approach, we identified brain regions that parametrically code grip-force intensities across two 49 6-second delay periods. During the first delay, above-chance decoding was observed in the precuneus 50 (PCu), whereas during the second delay it emerged in effector-specific regions, including the contralateral 51 intraparietal sulcus (pIPS/aIPS), primary somatosensory cortex (S1), dorsal premotor cortex (PMd), and 52 supplementary motor area (SMA). Cross-decoding confirmed effector-independent coding in the PCu, 53 while cross-temporal generalization revealed stable representations in the contralateral IPS and PMd from 54 the second delay through execution. Together, these findings indicate a progressive transformation from 55 abstract representations of intended force intensity in the PCu to effector- and movement-specific plans 56 in the IPS and PMd. 57

1 INTRODUCTION 61 

Motor planning is a complex neural process that underpins the ability to anticipate, represent, and specify 62 parameters of upcoming actions prior to execution. It involves selecting the appropriate effector (i.e., the 63 body part used to perform an action; Gallivan et al., 2011), maintaining relevant motor parameters in 64 working memory (WM), and transforming abstract motor goals (i.e., representations of the intended 65 

action outcome prior to specification of effector or movement details; Majdandžić et al., 2007; Gallivan et 66 al., 2011) into executable movement plans (Boettcher et al., 2021). In essence, motor planning can be 67 interpreted as a sequence of processes that unfold over time: first, the intended abstract goal is 68 represented; next, the effector is selected; and finally, the detailed movement required to achieve the goal 69 is specified. Neuroimaging research has extensively investigated the neural correlates of goal encoding 70 (Soon et al., 2008, 2013) and effector selection (Heed, 2011, 2016; Leoné et al., 2014), providing evidence 71 that motor goals can be represented in effector-specific brain regions even before movement details are 72 specified (Seegelke & Heed, 2025; Caccialupi et al., 2025b). However, it remains unclear how motor goals 73 are represented in an abstract format prior to effector selection, and how these representations are 74 transformed into effector-specific movement plans (Kim et al., 2021). 75 

Foundational neurophysiological studies in non-human primates identified preparatory signals 76 reflecting planned movements in the dorsal premotor cortex (PMd; Cisek & Kalaska, 2004, 2010; Hoshi & 77 Tanji, 2006), supplementary motor area (SMA; Hoshi & Tanji, 2004), and posterior parietal cortex (PPC; Cui 78 & Andersen, 2007, 2011; Andersen & Cui, 2009). Complementary neuroimaging studies in humans, using 79 delayed-response paradigms (e.g., Hamilton & Grafton, 2003), have consistently shown that secondary 80 motor areas, particularly the PMd, are involved in specifying movement features of grasping actions. For 81 instance, univariate fMRI analyses in delayed grip-force tasks have shown that the BOLD signal amplitude 82 in the PMd and intraparietal sulcus (IPS) covaries with anticipated grip-force intensity, indicating 83 parametric coding of motor parameters prior to execution (Cole & Rotella, 2002; Chouinard et al., 2005; 84 Nowak et al., 2009; van Nuenen et al., 2012; Mizuguchi et al., 2014). More recent fMRI multivoxel pattern 85 analysis (MVPA) studies have further shown that above-chance decoding in parietal and premotor cortices 86 can predict movement properties, such as movement sequences (Yokoi & Diedrichsen, 2019; Ariani et al., 87 2021, 2022), kinematics of grip types (Gallivan et al., 2011b; Ariani et al., 2015; Ruiz et al., 2024), and 88 anticipated grip-force intensities (Caccialupi et al., 2025a, b). These findings corroborate the involvement 89 of parieto-frontal networks in representing movement properties and anticipating detailed motor 90 parameters, consistent with the final stage of motor planning. 91 

Building on these findings, recent fMRI MVPA studies investigated where in the brain more abstract 92 action features can be decoded independently of motor movements (Gallivan et al., 2011b; Ricciardi et al., 93 2013; Errante et al., 2021). In particular, information about intended action outcomes, or “motor goals”, 94

These findings align with 96 a two-stage model of motor planning in which abstract goals are first represented and then transformed 97 into movement-specific plans (Wong et al., 2015; Boettcher et al., 2021). Consistently, the IPS has been 98 recently found to contribute to the transformation of neural codes underlying the selection of intended 99 actions into motor codes during an early planning phase (Caccialupi et al., 2025a). While both effector-100 independent and effector-specific information has been decoded from the IPS (Ariani et al., 2015; Gallivan 101 et al., 2016; Heed et al., 2016; Caccialupi et al., 2025a, 2025b), the anterior IPS (aIPS) has been found to 102 primarily supports effector-specific planning for hand actions (Leoné et al., 2014), whereas more posterior 103 and medial PPC regions, including posterior IPS (pIPS) and precuneus (PCu), encode intended goals in an 104 effector- and movement-independent format (Bode & Haynes, 2009; Gallivan et al., 2011, 2013; Heed et 105 al., 2016; Turella et al., 2020). Together, these results indicate that distinct PPC subsections may represent 106 action features of a motor plan at different levels of abstraction (Turella et al., 2020), providing a neural 107 substrate for transformation of abstract motor goals into detailed, effector-specific movement plans. 108 

The encoding of abstract motor goals and their transformation into detailed movement plans likely 109 rely on neural mechanisms similar to those involved in WM transformations, which have been extensively 110 investigated using fMRI decoding techniques (Haynes & Rees, 2006; Norman et al., 2006). Time-resolved 111 MVPA in delayed-response paradigms has been essential for testing how, where, and when information is 112 represented across different task periods (Schmidt et al., 2017; Hebart et al., 2018). For example, in WM 113 studies, time-resolved decoding has been employed to track transition processes from low-level sensory 114 features to more abstract representational codes across visual, tactile, and auditory modalities (Schmidt et 115 al., 2009, 2017, 2021; Wu et al., 2018; Hebart et al., 2018; Uluç et al., 2018, 2020). These studies further 116 indicate that transformations into more abstract representations occur in higher-order sensory or 117 multimodal cortices, depending on task demands (Christophel et al., 2017). Recently, we extended the use 118 of time-resolved MVPA to decode action-related representations, tracking the transformation processes 119 underlying the selection of intended actions compared to detailed movement planning (Caccialupi et al., 120 2025a). In particular, using a delayed grip-force task, we found that intended grip-force intensities were 121 initially represented in the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC) and later transformed into motor codes 122 in the l-PMd, revealing distributed neural patterns encoding grip-force parameters during grip-force 123 selection and movement planning. In a subsequent study (Caccialupi et al. 2025b) we showed that 124 contralateral IPS encodes intended grip-force intensities in an effector-specific format during an early 125 planning stage. However, since effector information was always available to participants, these studies did 126 not directly address where abstract motor goals (i.e., intended grip-force intensities) are represented 127

The present study therefore investigates where and when abstract and effector-specific 130 representations of grip-force intensities can be parametrically decoded from fMRI data. We modified our 131 previous paradigm (Caccialupi et al., 2025a, 2025b) and employed a delayed grip-force task in which 132 participants first maintained two of four possible force intensities, then selected which hand to prepare for 133 which force, followed by bimanual execution. Using time-resolved MVPA with support vector regression 134 (SVR), we decoded parametric representations of grip-force intensity during two subsequent delay periods. 135 In the first 6-second delay, participants maintained the to-be-applied force intensities without knowing 136 which hand would be used for which force. In the second delay, effector information was specified in 137 preparation for execution. Additionally, cross-decoding analyses assessed whether grip-force intensities 138 were represented in an effector-specific or effector-independent format, and temporal generalization 139 tested whether multivariate activation patterns during execution were already present during the second 140 delay, reflecting a movement-specific format during motor planning. Based on previous literature, we 141 hypothesize that above-chance decoding will be initially observed in medial PPC regions, followed by 142 lateralized action-specific regions, including the contralateral PMd and IPS. 143

2 MATERIALS AND METHODS 145 

2.1 Participants 146 

All participants ( N = 31) were healthy and right-handed, as assessed by the Edinburgh Handedness 147 

Inventory (EHI; Oldfield, 1971). Before the experiment, they provided written informed consent to 148 participate in the study in accordance with protocols approved by the local ethics committee of Freie 149 

Universität Berlin (003/2021, Berlin, Germany). 150 

To account for potential biases and confounds, only N = 22 participants were included in the analysis 151 (age: 28.9 ± 5.53, 11 female). Three participants were excluded due to excessive head movement (>3 mm), 152 and six were excluded for frequent grip force application during at least one delay period ( ≥75% of trials in 153 at least one run for condition). To further ensure that the analyses were not confounded by early motor 154 responses, we excluded from the dataset all trials in which the grip force was applied during any delay 155 period (cut-off: mean grip-force ≥ 0.05 on a scale from 0 to 1). 156

2.2 Experimental procedure 158 

Before entering the MRI scanner, participants underwent a training session lasting 60 to 90 minutes 159 approximately. Initially, the grip-force transducers were calibrated to participants’ maximum grip-force 160 (i.e., the average of the maximum force applied by the right and left hands). Next, participants were 161 introduced to the four grip-force levels that served as target grip-force intensities in the main experiment. 162 In this training phase, they were familiarized with the transducer by performing sixteen trials with real-163 time visual feedback of the applied grip-force. In forty-eight further trials, they were trained on the 164 experimental task (see below). Next, participants entered the MRI scanner, and the grip-force transducers 165 were calibrated anew. During the acquisition of a structural MRI scan, participants were further trained on 166 the experimental task. Finally, they performed the delayed grip-force task (illustrated in Figure 1A ) in four 167 runs during fMRI scanning. 168

2.3 Grip-force assessment and stimuli 183 

Based on the visual presentation of a Grip-force cue and an Effector cue , participants were instructed to 184 simultaneously apply two different grip forces bimanually, one with the right hand and one with the left, 185 using two cylindrical MR-compatible grip-force transducers ( i.e. , force fibre optic response pads , Current 186 Designs, HHSC-1x1-GRFC-V2; illustrated in Figure 1B ). 2.4 Experimental task 200 

During fMRI scanning, participants performed a delayed grip-force task. Each trial started with the 201 presentation of a grip-force cue , comprising of two to-be-maintained grip-force levels presented in cyan 202 and red (illustrated in Figure 1A ). A 1 s Effector cue (cuing the right hand with a right pointer, and the left 203 hand) was presented at 6 s from the beginning of a 12 s delay period . The colour of each pointer (either 204 red or cyan) indicated which hand had to be prepared for executing a given grip-force level. The 205 combinations of displayed grip-force levels and the colour of the hand-cues were balanced so that all 206 combinations were presented equally often, and each hand was prepared for each level the same number 207 of times. Upon the display of a Go-cue , participants applied simultaneously the anticipated grip-forces with 208 the cued hands; importantly, in the absence of a grip-force or Effector cue . A pre-Go-cue was presented for 209 0.5 s before the response to allow optimal response timing during the 1.5 s response window. Finally, visual 210 

Feedback was provided by showing on a screen the applied forces as green radians if accurate or as red 211 radians if inaccurate, i.e. outside of the cued grip-force level (compare Figure 1A ). The mean grip-force 212 during the last 0.75 s of the response window was evaluated to assess accuracy of responses. For the 213 analysis, trials were considered accurate when the applied force was within the target range ± 15° (4.15% 214 of maximum grip force). 215 

Four experimental runs comprised of 48 experimental trials each. Trial order was fully randomized 216 within a run, where each experimental condition ( i.e. , the cueing of each of the four to-be anticipated grip-217 forces with a target hand) was presented equally often ( i.e. , 12 times per run). 218

2.5.1 Time-resolved searchlight decoding of abstract force intensities and effector-specific grip-force 231 

anticipation 232 

We employed an MVPA searchlight approach to identify brain regions that exhibited multivariate 233 parametric codes of force intensity before and after specification of the hands to-be-used. A finite impulse 234 response (FIR) model was used to obtain run-wise beta estimates for each 1 s time-bin of the trials. 20 235 consecutive time-bins were modelled (as illustrated in Figure 1C ), comprising 6 time-bins throughout each 236 delay period, 6 time-bins from the PreGo cue (i.e., execution period), and 2 time-bins of the ITI. High-pass 237 filtered data (cut-off 192 s) were included in the first-level general linear model (GLM), modelling each 238 combination of intensity and hand. Additionally, six movement parameters, the first five principal 239 components (explaining variance in the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals; Behzadi et al., 2007) 240 and a run constant, were added as nuisance regressors, for a total of 688 regressors (8 conditions × 20 241 time-bins × 4 runs complemented with the 6 realignment parameters and 5 principal components). 242 

To identify where and when information about the intended intensity was encoded in the brain, we 243 applied time-resolved MVPA using SVR (Kahnt et al. , 2011), which can be considered a multivariate 244 pendant of parametric coding, a method previously applied in a series of WM decoding experiments ( e.g. ,245 Christophel et al. , 2012; Schmidt et al. , 2017; Uluç et al. , 2020 Pennock et al. , 2021). Using a searchlight 246 approach (r=4 voxel) independently for every time-bin allows testing for local multivariate representations 247 (i.e. , activation patterns of voxels) that code parametric grip-force anticipations. Two SVR models were 248 trained to predict grip-force ( i.e. , the four grip-force intensities) based on a multivariate data vector ( i.e. ,249 multivoxel activation pattern) for each hand. To account for differences in the difficulty of grip-force 250 performance, the distances between the four SVR labels were adjusted using Fechner’s law (Fechner, 1860) 251 as previously done (see Caccialupi et al., 2025a), resulting in labels of -2.40, -1.48, 0.45, and 3.42. 252 

Beta estimates of each condition were first normalized ( i.e. , z-scaled) across the samples for each 253 voxel as implemented in the Decoding Toolbox (TDT; Hebart et al. , 2015) and forwarded to a four-fold 254 leave-one-run-out cross validation schema. To make our data comparable to previous reports, we used as 255 measure the prediction accuracy defined as the Fisher’s z-transformed correlation coefficient between the 256 predicted value levels and the actual value levels of the test dataset (in TDT: “zcorr”, see also Kahnt et al. ,257 2011). The centre of the searchlight was moved voxel wise through the brain, and prediction accuracy 258 values were saved as corresponding whole-brain accuracy maps. In this way, we obtained an accuracy map 259 for every subject and time-bin, reflecting local activation patterns that code abstract or effector-specific 260 grip-force intensity in a multivariate way. 261 

Prediction accuracy maps were entered into a second-level analysis to test for above-chance 262 decoding in terms of SPM’s flexible factorial design implementation of an ANOVA. We used a t-contrast to 263

2.5.2 Cross-decoding analysis: Cross-Hand cross-decoding 267 

We conducted a cross-decoding analysis, i.e. cross-hand cross-decoding ( X-Dec: Cross Hand ) to test 268 whether and where parametric representations of grip-force intensity can be found in an effector-269 independent format (i.e., information about the force intensity is retained in similar brain activation 270 patterns for the two hands). The cross-decoding analysis was based on the same FIR model as the main 271 decoding analysis. The logic underlying the use of the SVR in the cross-decoding, including the four-fold 272 leave-one-run-out cross-validation schema, is the same as in the main analysis, with the only difference 273 being that, in X-Dec: Cross Hand , training and testing of the SVR-model were conducted on beta estimates 274 corresponding to different hands. The SVR model was first trained on beta estimates reflecting preparation 275 with the right hand and tested on the left hand, then vice versa (see Caccialupi et al., 2025b). 276 

After normalization and smoothing, prediction accuracy maps were entered into a second-level 277 analysis to test for above-chance decoding using SPM's flexible factorial design implementation of an 278 ANOVA. This second level design included 1 factor and 20 levels for time-bins (t1–t20). T-contrasts were 279 used to assess above-chance decoding on maps of the second half of each period, as done in the main 280 analysis (accounting for temporal correlation between periods). 281 

An additional second-level analysis was conducted to identify brain regions representing force 282 intensities in similar, effector-independent codes across the first and second delays. For this analysis, we 283 included prediction accuracy maps from the main decoding analyses (i.e., one analysis for hand), which 284 reflect hand-unspecific decoding prior to the Effector cue (i.e., during the first delay), as well as from the X-285 

Dec: Cross-Hand (covering the first and second delays). The second-level design comprised 3 factors (i.e., 286 one for the right hand, one for the left hand, and one for the cross-decoding between them) and 20 levels 287 corresponding to the time-bins (t1–t20). T-contrasts were computed to assess above-chance decoding on 288 maps reflecting the first delay period (main analyses and cross-decoding), and the second delay ( X-Dec: 289 

Cross-Hand ). A conjunction analysis over t-contrasts was performed under the conjunction null hypothesis 290 (Nichols et al., 2005) to identify regions that similarly represent effector-independent information across 291 delays. All results are reported at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected at the voxel level. 292

2.5.4 Control analyses: label permutation control analysis 319 

To control whether prediction accuracies of the main analysis were based on the parametric coding of the 320 grip-force intensity, we performed a label-permutation test. Here, the labels of the conditions entered into 321 SVR-models were permuted. As previously applied (see Schmidt et al., 2017; Uluc et al., 2020; Caccialupi 322 et al., 2025), higher distance of the labelling from the original order should result in reduced decoding 323 accuracies (getting to zero for the highest distance i.e., completely unordered labelling). For all possible 324 permutations, the distance from the rank order was calculated as the sum of the absolute difference of 325 adjacent ranks (e.g., the linear order of grip-force level 1, 2, 3, 4 has a distance of ranks of sum (|1 – 2| + 326 |2 – 3| + |3 – 4|) = 3 and the permuted labelling 2, 1, 3, 4 corresponds to the sum (|2 – 1| + |1 – 3| + |3 – 327 4|) = 4, resulting in a difference of 1 from the linear order). Thereby, the permutation analysis congregated 328 permutations into four classes of distances from the linear order. 4 DISCUSSION 541 

In this fMRI study, we used a delayed grip-force task in combination with time-resolved SVR to identify 542 brain regions that parametrically code grip-force intensities. Specifically, we tested whether, during a 543 first delay period (i.e., prior to effector specification), brain activity patterns represent intended force 544 intensities in an abstract, effector-independent format, and how these representations are 545 subsequently transformed into effector- and movement-specific codes during a second delay. 546 

To this end, we adapted our previous delayed grip-force paradigm (Caccialupi et al., 2025a, 547 2025b) to include two consecutive delay periods: during the first, participants maintained two of four 548 possible force intensities for later execution; during the second, they were cued which hand to prepare 549 for each force. This design allowed us to test the abstract, effector-independent coding of force 550 intensity during the first delay and its subsequent transformation into effector-specific 551 representations during the second. During the first delay (lasting 6 s from trial onset), graded grip-552 force intensities could be decoded from activity patterns in the PCu. Meanwhile, during the second 553 delay (lasting 6 s from effector-cue onset), above-chance decoding was found in the contralateral aIPS 554 and PMd, localized to the right hemisphere for the left hand and to the left hemisphere for the right. 555 This systematic lateralization indicates that anticipated grip-force intensities are represented in 556 effector-specific brain regions during the second delay. Notably, the main decoding analysis also 557 revealed that graded grip-force intensities are parametrically represented in distinct areas across the 558 two delays. Together, these findings suggest that delay-specific activation patterns may reflect 559 successive stages of motor planning: the encoding of a motor goal (i.e., the intended force intensity) 560 in an abstract format during the first delay and its subsequent transformation into effector-specific 561 movement plans during the second. 562 

To further characterize the change in representational formats across delays, we applied cross-563 decoding and generalization tests to determine when and where force intensities were represented 564 in stable representational codes (i.e., same activity patterns which allow above-chance cross-decoding 565 or generalization) and where activation patterns are only exhibited in a condition-specific or time-566 specific manner. First, we performed cross-effector decoding ( Xdec: Hand ). Here, the SVR model was 567 trained and tested on different hands. This analysis revealed above-chance decoding in the PCu during 568 both delays. Coherently, a conjunction analysis of t-contrasts ( Xdec: Hand in D1 and D2, and main 569 decoding in D1) identified overlapping clusters in the PCu, confirming that this region encodes abstract 570 features of intended force intensities, even into the second delay. Consistently, cross-regression 571 decoding indicated that parametric codes in the PCu remained stable across delays, whereas 572 contralateral aIPS and PMd exhibited stable activation patterns from the second delay through 573 execution. Thereby, parametric codes in the aIPS and PMd are likely represented in a movement-574

specific format that emerges from the second delay onward. Together, these results indicate a 575 progressive transformation in representational format, from abstract coding in the PCu to effector-576 and movement-specific coding in aIPS and PMd. 577 

These results align well with the two-stage framework of action planning (Boettcher et al., 578 2021), which proposes that, following the initial encoding of a motor goal, the intended action is 579 specified into effector-specific movement plans. Accordingly, in our study, the PCu likely encoded grip-580 force intensities (i.e., the motor goal) in an abstract format, contributing to the early stage of motor 581 planning (Hummel et al., 2003; Astafiev et al., 2004). As planning progressed, contralateral aIPS and 582 PMd were likely engaged in effector selection and in anticipating the movements required to apply 583 the intended intensity, even before execution begins (Gale et al., 2021; Diedrichsen et al., 2022). 584 Within PMd, specific movement parameters, such as the muscle fibres recruited for later grip-force 585 execution, may have been represented and refined in preparation for movement onset (Mizuguchi et 586 al., 2013; Caccialupi et al., 2025a, 2025b). 587

4.1 The First Delay Period: Action Specific encoding of Graded Force Intensities in the Precuneus, and 591 

Modality-Independent Representation in the Right Intraparietal Sulcus 592 

During the first delay period, our main decoding analyses revealed two significant clusters, one in the PCu 593 and the other in the r-pIPS. 594 

The most pronounced cluster was located in the PCu, a region well known for its role in planning 595 motor actions (Gallivan et al., 2011, 2015; Wang et al., 2019). Previous time-resolved MVPA studies using 596 delayed-response paradigms have coherently shown that the PCu is involved in planning hand movements 597 during the delay period preceding the execution of goal-directed actions (Soon et al., 2008; Bode & Haynes, 598 2009; Gallivan et al., 2011). Crucially, above-chance decoding in the PCu has also been found during an 599 early phase of this delay, preceding effector specification (Soon et al., 2013). While this finding has been 600 interpreted as evidence that the PCu encodes abstract intentions independent of effector-specific plans 601 (Soon et al., 2013), such abstraction does not imply a functional detachment from motor control (Gallivan 602 et al., 2011). Rather, early increases in prediction accuracy in the PCu, together with task-dependent 603 activation patterns in delayed-response paradigms (when abstract information is maintained and 604 transformed for later execution of complex motor actions) more frequently reflect higher-order 605 representations of action features within the motor hierarchy (Hamilton & Grafton, 2007; Gertz et al., 606 2017; Ariani et al., 2018; Monaco et al., 2020). 607

Moreover, converging evidence indicates that a 612 dorsomedial parieto-frontal circuit linking the PCu with the aIPS and PMd is specialized for grasp planning 613 (Heed et al., 2013; Gallivan & Culham, 2015), and exhibits a posterior-to-frontal gradient of abstraction. 614 Within this circuit, posterior regions such as the PCu were found to represent the intended action (i.e., the 615 motor goal) regardless of movement details (Errante et al., 2021), whereas the PMd encodes detailed 616 motor parameters in preparation for execution (Caccialupi et al., 2025a). Recent fMRI MVPA studies also suggest that parametric modulation in the r-pIPS reflects the 628 encoding of graded abstract quantities (Pennock et al., 2021). Unlike the PCu, whose parametric codes 629 likely represents abstract action features, the r-pIPS may provide a modality-independent representation 630 of abstract parameters that can be flexibly accessed for both cognitive and motor functions. Accordingly, 631 during the first delay, parametric codes in the PCu and r-pIPS may respectively reflect modality-specific 632 (action-related) and modality-independent neural representations of graded intensities, highlighting the 633 simultaneous processing of motor-relevant information across PPC subregions. 634

4.3 Cross-Decoding Analyses: from Abstract to Effector- and Movement-Specific Force Intensity coding 713 

To investigate how intended force intensities were represented in the brain, and how representational 714 formats changed across delay periods, we performed two complementary cross-decoding analyses. The 715 first, cross-effector decoding ( Xdec: Hand ), assessed whether (in the brain) parametric activity patterns 716 encoded force intensities in an abstract, effector-independent format, with first and second delay 717 examined separately. The second, cross-regression decoding (used to test for temporal generalization), 718 evaluated the stability of these activity patterns, including their similarity between the execution period 719 and earlier delays. 720 

The Xdec: Hand was conducted using a searchlight approach and a time-resolved SVR-model, 721 trained on beta estimates from one hand and tested on the other. Consistent with the main decoding 722 results, it revealed a robust significant cluster in the PCu during both delay periods (at p < 0.05, FWE-723 corrected). During the first delay, above-chance decoding likely reflected abstract, effector-independent 724 coding of force intensity (as no information about the effector was available in this period), whereas during 725 the second delay, similar activation patterns for the two hands may indicate a shared representation (with 726 information about each hand stored in similar activation patterns). To further determine whether force 727 intensities were represented in an abstract format during the second delay, we performed a conjunction 728 analysis testing the overlap of activation patterns found in the main decoding ( Delay 1 ) and cross-effector 729 decoding (both delays) against a conjunction null hypothesis (Nichols et al., 2005). The resulting PCu cluster 730 (tested at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected) largely overlapped with those identified during the first delay, 731 confirming that parametric activation patterns in the PCu encode force intensities in a consistent, abstract 732 format across delays. This suggests that, while the second delay shows effector-specific codes in 733 contralateral aIPS and PMd, the PCu represents intended force independently of the selected hand. A small 734 cluster in the right pIPS also showed above-chance cross-hand decoding during the first delay, consistent 735 with its supporting role in abstract quantity representation. 736 

Cross-regression decoding further examined the generalization of parametric codes in the PCu 737 between the first and second delays. Specifically, independent tests were conducted for each hand, 738 followed by a conjunction analysis across hands to evaluate the similarity of abstract force-intensity codes 739 between delays. This procedure revealed a cluster consistent with those identified in the main and cross-740 effector decoding analyses (at p < 0.05, FWE-corrected), indicating that abstract representations of 741 intended force intensities are maintained in the PCu. 5 CONCLUSION 789 

This fMRI study provides new insights into how intended grip-force intensities are represented and 790 transformed across subsequent stages of motor planning. Using a delayed grip-force task and time-791 resolved SVR, we found that parametric codes of intended grip-force intensities undergo a reformatting 792 process, transitioning from abstract to effector- and movement-specific representations along the parieto-793 frontal axis. During the first delay, grip-force intensities were encoded in the PCu in an abstract, effector-794 independent format. In contrast, during the second delay, parametric activity patterns in the contralateral 795 aIPS and PMd reflected effector- and movement-specific coding. 796 

Furthermore, cross-effector and cross-regression decoding indicated that abstract representations 797 in the precuneus are maintained across delays but not during execution, whereas effector-specific patterns 798 in the aIPS and PMd closely resemble those observed during execution, reflecting movement-specific 799 coding during the second delay period. These findings support a two-stage model of motor planning, in 800 which abstract motor goals (i.e., intended force intensities) are progressively reformatted into effector-801 and movement-specific plans (i.e., grip-force anticipation) prior to execution. 802 

In sum, our results indicate that the human brain encodes grip-force intensity through a structured 803 sequence of transformations—from abstract codes to movement planning—across the parieto-frontal 804 circuit. This process aligns with current models of motor planning and provides new evidence for a detailed 805 and cohesive framework describing how abstract motor intentions are progressively transformed into 806 concrete, executable movement plans. Future studies may investigate whether similar transformations 807 occur across other effectors or motor domains. 808
