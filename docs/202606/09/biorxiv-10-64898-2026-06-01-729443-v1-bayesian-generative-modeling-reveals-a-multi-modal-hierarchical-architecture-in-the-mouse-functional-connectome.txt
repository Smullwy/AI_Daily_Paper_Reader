Abstract 12 

Understanding the principles governing large-scale functional organization of the brain remains 13 

a central challenge in systems neuroscience. Despite convergent findings, substantial variability 14 

across analytical approaches suggests that functional networks may not admit a unique partition-15 

ing. Here, we propose that this variability reflects an intrinsic property of the connectome itself: 16 

its organization may be fundamentally multi-modal rather than singular. To test this hypothesis, 17 

we employ a Bayesian generative modeling framework based on stochastic block models, enabling 18 

principled comparison of competing organizational principles and characterization of the full pos-19 

terior distribution over network partitions. Applying this framework to resting-state fMRI data 20 

in mice, we find that a non-degree-corrected hierarchical architecture provides the most parsimo-21 

nious description of the functional connectome. Importantly, the inferred posterior landscape is not 22 

dominated by a single configuration, but instead comprises multiple distinct and co-dominant or-23 

ganizational schemes. At the mesoscale, these hierarchical communities are anatomically grounded 24 

yet systematically reorganize canonical resting-state networks: primary sensory systems remain 25 

cohesive, whereas higher-order association networks are fractionated into multiple interacting sub-26 

circuits. This global structural variation is driven by structured variability at the community 27 

level, where integrative systems exhibit variable regional affiliations while sensory systems act as 28 

structurally stable anchors. Together, these findings suggest that the resting-state connectome is 29 

best described as a distribution over alternative, yet co-dominant, organizational configurations. 30 

This perspective reconciles inconsistencies across previous studies and supports a view of brain 31 

organization as inherently degenerate, providing a latent repertoire of network configurations that 32 

may underlie adaptive information routing and dynamic functional reconfiguration. 33

multiple analytical techniques have been employed to define these networks, including Independent 42 

Component Analysis (ICA), modularity optimization, and co-activation patterns (CAPs), among oth-43 

ers. Across studies, a few systems have been repeatedly observed, including visual, sensorimotor, and 44

We hypothesized that such variability reflects the fact that the organization of large-scale networks, 46 

as garnered via the correlation of time series data, is not necessarily unique (see Figure 1). In other 47 

words, whereas there may exist natural region clusters (e.g., sensorimotor regions), it is possible that 48 

the functional organization of the brain admits multiple credible (i.e., of similar “quality”) network-49 

level partitionings Meunier et al. (2009); Hilgetag and Goulas (2020); Palla et al. (2005); Vafaii et al. 50 

(2024). 51 

Addressing this question is challenging because the different methods employed to investigate func-52 

tional organization have distinct, and often implicit, technical properties. For example, a spatio-53 

temporal technique, ICA Hyv¨ arinen and Oja (2000), assumes that the underlying source signals (brain 54 

networks/components) are statistically independent of each other, such that activity in one network 55 

should not be predictable from the activity in another. Graph-based technique, modularity optimiza-56 

tion methods Newman (2006); Fortunato (2010), maximize a global network partition quality often 57 

maximizing, in the context of fMRI data, “assortativity”, namely the tendency for nodes to be grouped 58 

with nodes with similar characteristics (e.g., activity). Whereas ICA networks can overlap spatially, 59 

modularity optimization algorithms assume non-overlapping (disjoint) organization (i.e., a region be-60 

longs to one and only one network). Another spatio-temporal technique, CAPs Liu and Duyn (2013); 61 

Liu et al. (2018), assume that brain activity can be characterized by a finite set of discrete, recurring 62 

spatial activation patterns or “states”. Unlike ICA’s continuous mixing in time, CAPs assume that 63 

at any given time, only one (or few) patterns are “active”, and that brain activity switches between 64 

distinct configurations. 65 

It is not surprising, therefore, that many of the results obtained with these techniques differ in 66 

important ways, despite also capturing converging organization. Zerbi et al. (2015) applied group 67 

ICA Hyv¨ arinen and Oja (2000) and identified 23 functional circuits hierarchically clustered into six 68 

bilaterally symmetric networks: somatosensory, sensory processing (motor, visual, and auditory), olfac-69 

tory processing, limbic system (retrosplenial cortex, cingulate, hippocampus, thalamus, and amygdala), 70 

basal ganglia (caudate-putamen/dorsal-striatum, and pallidum), and cerebellar network. Grandjean 71 

et al. (2020) confirmed this organization across 17 independent center datasets using both group ICA 72 

and seed-based correlation analysis. Liska et al. (2015) used graph-theoretic modularity maximiza-73 

tion Newman and Girvan (2004) and observed six communities, including default-mode (retrosplenial 74 

cortex, anterior cingulate, temporal cortex), lateral sensory-motor cortex, basal forebrain, ventral mid-75 

brain (amygdala, hypothalamus), thalamus, and hippocampus networks. Coletta et al. (2020) using 76 

voxel-level modularity maximization, found four large-scale networks: default-mode plus limbic, lat-77 

eral sensory-motor, hippocampal, and olfactory/basal forebrain/ventral midbrain networks. Finally, 78 

Mandino et al. (2022) using co-activation pattern (CAP) analysis Liu and Duyn (2013); Liu et al. 79 

(2018), discovered three networks, including default-mode and lateral sensory-motor cortex. Gutierrez-80 

Barragan et al. (2019, 2022) investigated CAPs, and these preferentially highlight arousal-linked basal 81 

forebrain/thalamic engagement and anti-coordination between unimodal and polymodal cortices. In 82 

contrast, seed-based analysis in the same awake mice recovered default-mode, lateral cortical, and 83 

salience-like networks (similar to those found by Mandino et al. (2022)). For a detailed review of 84 

functional systems in rodents see Xu et al. (2022). 85 

To investigate the potential multiplicity of network organizations from the same dataset, what is 86 

needed is a principled framework to evaluate the extent to which different organizational principles 87 

explain the fMRI data. Here, we employ a Bayesian generative modeling approach to do precisely 88 

that Peel et al. (2022); Peixoto (2019, 2023). Essentially, different techniques implicitly assume how 89 

the identified communities of brain regions interact with each other: assortative (disjoint partition-90 

ing), overlapping, and/or hierarchical arrangements (Figure 1). We make the assumptions, i.e. the 91 

organizational principles, explicit so that we can evaluate their compatibility with the data, thereby 92 

testing the presence of multiple organizations in the same dataset. The Bayesian framework then 93 

quantitatively evaluates compatibility between the organizational principles with the data on a single, 94 

information-theoretic scale, going beyond the technical differences between current methods. We chose 95 

to evaluate these organizations given their prevalence in the network neuroscience literature Sporns 96 

and Betzel (2016); Gozzi and Schwarz (2016); Najafi et al. (2016); Vafaii et al. (2024); Meunier et al. 97 

(2009); Betzel et al. (2024). It should be stated, however, that other forms of organizations have been 98 

reported in the literature, and that our results do not preclude that they are noteworthy organizational 99 

principles that explain some of the functional organization of brain large-scale networks; for example 100

Beyond comparing these broad organizational principles, a second challenge emerges. Traditional 118 

approaches to characterizing brain organization from fMRI data often seek a single partition that 119 

best summarizes functional organization, assuming that the space of solutions, or network partitions, 120 

concentrates around a single division of nodes into communities (a partition), with individual parti-121 

tions being minor variations of their consensus at the center (Figure 3A; Lancichinetti and Fortunato 122 

(2012)). However, the pursuit of a consensus can be problematic when the underlying distribution 123 

of network partitions is inherently (or even moderately) heterogeneous Good et al. (2010); Fortunato 124 

(2010); that is, a single consensus can obscure the fact that the data may support multiple, distinct 125 

community arrangements. Figure 3B, for example, illustrates a potential case in which the landscape 126 

of solutions comprises of multiple concentrations of partitions, i.e. solution modes , such that partitions 127 

within a single mode are minor variations of their local consensus, but partitions in different modes 128 

are “structurally” distinct. In the example, we observe not a single dominant organization but rather 129 

multiple distinct modes that coexist (in this case, five), where a single consensus solution is unable 130 

to represent either of the distinct solutions. Existence of multiple modes in the solution landscape 131 

means that brain’s functional organization may not be a single, fixed map of community assignments, 132 

but rather be described as a landscape of coexisting distinct arrangements. We hypothesize, therefore, 133 

that the brain’s functional architecture may be partitioned in multiple distinct ways, even under the 134 

same organizational principle. Each mode represents a different possible arrangement of communities, 135 

potentially reflecting different complementary aspects (states, individual variations) of functional ar-136 

chitecture that cannot be summarized by a single partition alone Mattar et al. (2015); Betzel et al. 137

It begins with a prior probability ( P (b)) over possible community partitions. For a given partition, an organizational principle (e.g., assortative, hierarchical) defines a likelihood ( P (A | b)) which is the probability of observing the network A given that partition structure. The Bayesian methodology we employ is uniquely suited to discover multiplicity of arrangements 139 

within an organizational principle Peixoto (2021). By characterizing the full posterior distribution 140 

over all possible partitions, it allows us to identify multiple solution modes if the data support their 141 

existence. Furthermore, it assigns a relative weight, or probability ( ω), to each mode, quantifying its 142 

prevalence in the solution landscape (see Figure 3B). The fact that multiple modes can have substantial 143 

weight means the data support multiple, potentially complementary ways of understanding the brain’s 144 

functional architecture, rather than there being a single “correct” partition. 145 

# 2 Results 146 

To investigate the large-scale functional organization of the mouse brain, we analyzed resting-state 147 

BOLD-fMRI data from lightly anesthetized mice ( n = 9 , 0.5 − 0.75% isoflurane). Data were col-148 

lected across three longitudinal sessions, each comprising four runs that lasted 10 min long, yielding 149 

a total of 120 min of data per mouse (see Section 4.1 for details). Following standardized prepro-150 

cessing Desrosiers-Gr´ egoire et al. (2024) and co-registration to the Allen CCFv3 space Wang et al. 151 

(2020), we constructed group-level functional connectivity graphs comprising of N = 172 brain regions 152 

of interest (ROIs) (see Section 4.1.5 for details). We defined each ROI by merging the regions in the 153 

CCFv3 based on their functional roles and anatomical proximity. 154 

To construct a sparse, undirected graph suitable for network analysis, the group-level Pearson 155 

correlation matrices were binarized by retaining only the top d% strongest edges (main text reports 156 

d = 20%; see Supplement for d = 10%). This standard thresholding step is necessary to remove 157 

spurious, low-magnitude noise correlation, thereby reducing computational burden and helping the 158 

generative algorithms isolate the true topological structure of the network. 159 

To ensure our findings are robust to sampling variability, we embedded this network construction 160 

within a bootstrap resampling framework (see Section 4.1.6 for details). In addition to constructing 161 

a graph from the original cohort of mice (herein termed the reference sample ), we generated 500 162 

independent group-level graphs by sampling the subjects with replacement. This allowed us to provide 163 

confidence intervals and statistical testing for all subsequent metrics. 164

## 2.1 Hierarchical organization is more compatible with the resting-state 165 

## functional organization than purely assortative organization 166 

To evaluate the extent to which different organizational principles explain the resting-state functional 167 

organization in mice, we employed a Bayesian generative modeling framework Peel et al. (2022) (see 168 

Section 4.2 for details). This framework involves defining a “generative model” (that is, a probabilistic 169 

recipe) to construct the observed functional graph in terms of arrangements of latent communities 170 

(“partitions” of the graph). Crucially, these arrangements are dictated by a specific organizational 171 

principle (such as strict modularity or a nested hierarchy). A Bayesian inference procedure then 172 

inverts this generative process to infer the community structures responsible for the observed graph, 173 

calculating the posterior probability distribution over all possible community arrangements allowed by 174 

that specific rule. 175 

We compared seven generative hypotheses (i.e., organizational principles) implemented as stochastic 176 

block model (SBM) variants Peixoto (2019). To formalize the most prominent topological theories 177 

in network neuroscience into testable models, we evaluated a purely assortative architecture Sporns 178 

and Betzel (2016) alongside standard Betzel et al. (2018b), hierarchical Hilgetag and Goulas (2020), 179 

and overlapping Najafi et al. (2016); Vafaii et al. (2024) arrangements. Furthermore, the standard, 180 

hierarchical, and overlapping models were parameterized both with and without degree-correction (see 181 

Section 4.2.2 for details). 182 

This distinction between non-degree-corrected and degree-corrected models allowed us to test a 183 

fundamental assumption regarding how highly connected regions (“hubs”) are integrated within the 184 

network. Specifically, this distinction evaluates topologies often associated with “rich-clubs” — densely 185 

interconnected communities of hubs that form a centralized communication backbone Sporns and 186 

Betzel (2016); Sporns (2016); Bullmore and Sporns (2009). Non-degree-correction assumes that a 187 

region’s connectivity is a homogeneous feature of its community, thereby grouping highly connected 188 

nodes together in a manner that can capture rich-club-like topographies. Conversely, degree-correction 189 

allows nodes with widely varying connectivity profiles to be grouped together Karrer and Newman 190 

(2011), implying that hubs are distributed across heterogeneous functional systems rather than forming 191 

an exclusive core. Finally, because modularity maximization is commonly used to investigate large-192 

scale networks Liska et al. (2015); Coletta et al. (2020), we included the purely assortative SBM as its 193 

generative equivalent (see Section 4.2.3). 194 

For each generative model, we approximated the posterior distribution over all possible graph par-195 

titions using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations (see Section 4.2.4 for details). Briefly, 196 

the algorithm systematically navigates the vast landscape of possible community arrangements (parti-197 

tions) to isolate the specific ensemble of partitions most strongly supported by the data. To objectively 198 

adjudicate between these competing hypotheses, we quantified each model’s “explanatory power” us-199 

ing Total Description Length (TDL; see Section 4.3.1 for details). Grounded in information theory, 200 

TDL represents the total number of bits required to succinctly describe the network data under a 201 

given generative rule. TDL balances goodness-of-fit against the complexity of the solution landscape. 202 

Models with a lower TDL encode the data more efficiently, making them more compatible with the 203 

network’s underlying topology. 204 

Model comparison based on TDL revealed a clear and consistent ranking of organizational prin-205 

ciples (Figure 4). The non-degree-corrected hierarchical model (nd-h-SBM) yielded the lowest TDL, 206 

indicating that a degree-homogeneous nested (multi-scale) architecture is more compatible with the 207 

underlying functional connectome of the mouse brain than the other evaluated principles. Conversely, 208 

the purely assortative model (a-SBM) — the generative equivalent of strict modularity maximization 209 

— yielded the highest description length. This result suggests that characterizing the brain solely as 210 

a system of strictly segregated modules provides a less compatible description of the network’s archi-211 

tecture, demonstrating that pure assortativity alone may be insufficient to capture the full complexity 212 

of the functional connectome. 213 

Furthermore, across all evaluated hypotheses, non-degree-corrected models were consistently more 214 

compatible with the data than their degree-corrected variants. This structural preference indicates 215 

that, within this generative framework, high connectivity is modeled as a collective property shared 216 

within specific communities rather than an independent trait of individual regions. While this topolog-217 

ical pattern resembles “rich-club” organizations (densely interconnected groups of regions that jointly 218 

facilitate global communication Bullmore and Sporns (2009); Sporns and Betzel (2016)) this prefer-219 

ence may also be influenced by the inherent degree homogeneity of the thresholded BOLD network 220

## 2.2 The inferred communities are anatomically grounded 223 

Having identified the non-degree-corrected hierarchical principle as the most compatible generative 224 

rule, we first sought to contextualize its inferred communities before exploring the multiplicity of the 225 

solution landscape. As a reminder, while the network nodes were defined using the Allen CCFv3 226 

atlas, the Bayesian generative models were not provided with any explicit spatial priors regarding how 227 

these regions should be grouped into communities. It is therefore critical to determine whether these 228 

algorithmically derived communities correspond to anatomically meaningful systems or are merely 229 

mathematical artifacts. 230 

To extract these communities, we analyzed the full solution landscape of graph partitions generated 231 

by the MCMC simulations (see Section 4.2.4). The simulations approximate the posterior landscape 232 

by collecting a vast ensemble of valid partitions, where each partition represents a discrete assignment 233 

of ROIs into communities governed by the generative rule (here, hierarchical). To synthesize this 234 

massive ensemble and account for potential multi-modality in the landscape, we applied a mixture 235 

model Peixoto (2021) that grouped similar partitions into distinct clusters. Each cluster represents a 236 

distinct organizational scheme , and the relative size of the cluster reflects that scheme’s prevalence, or 237 

probability ( ω), within the landscape (see Section 4.3.2 for details). After computationally aligning the 238 

partitions to ensure consistent community identities across the ensemble (and bootstrap resamples), we 239 

summarized the ROI community assignments within each scheme as a probabilistic assignment matrix 240 

(π) by aggregating over the partitions within that scheme. In this matrix, each column represents a 241 

community, and the values quantify the probability that a given ROI is assigned to that community 242 

within that specific scheme. 243 

To identify the overall ROI assignments to the communities across this multi-modal landscape, we 244 

computed the expected assignment probability of each ROI by taking a weighted average (using ω)245 

of the π matrices across all schemes. To evaluate which ROIs are assigned to which communities, 246 

we used a statistical test across the 500 bootstrap resamples to separate robust topological structure 247 

from background noise. We retained only those ROIs whose expected assignment probability was 248

munity (C.04), anchored by anterior cingulate areas frequently implicated in executive control, and 269 

the more distributed Olfactory-Prefrontal-Thalamic community (C.01). This latter community links 270

pathways associated with odor-driven associative learning Zingg et al. (2014); Oh et al. (2014). 272

connected network classically associated with spatial navigation and memory. Affective, autonomic, 275 

and basal ganglia circuits were partitioned into two distinct cortico-subcortical loops: the Fronto-276 

Insular-Striatal community (C.03), anchoring the dorsal striatum and insula (regions linked via known 277 

striatal projection pathways and often coactivated during interoception), and the Striato-Amygdalo-278 

Hypothalamic community (C.07), capturing the amygdala, ventral striatum, and hypothalamus — a 279 

structurally coupled axis widely implicated in affective processing and survival drives Cardinal et al. 280 

(2002). 281 

Finally, deep brainstem and hindbrain regions were distinctly partitioned, reflecting their distinct 282 

projection targets and physiological roles. The model grouped the cerebellum, medulla, and sensorimo-283 

tor midbrain into the Cerebello-Brainstem community (C.08), reflecting pathways supporting motor 284 

coordination, while isolating the pons and behavioral state-related midbrain into the Ponto-Midbrain 285 

community (C.09), a structural hub associated with basic arousal and physiological state regulation 286 

Saper et al. (2001). 287 

Importantly, the emergence of these anatomically meaningful boundaries was highly robust to vari-288 

ations in edge density thresholding, yielding a consistent organization consisting of seven consolidated 289 

communities at a 10% threshold (see Supplement 5.5.2). The emergence of these anatomically coherent 290 

communities, derived entirely without explicit spatial priors during inference, lends strong credibility 291 

to the utility of our Bayesian generative modeling framework for mapping the functional connectome. 292 

## 2.3 The inferred communities refine canonical resting-state networks 293 

We next investigated whether the inferred communities directly align with canonical functional sys-294 

tems, or if they structurally refine them into distinct sub-circuits. To provide a reference frame for 295 

this comparison, we utilized the resting-state network (RSN) atlas from Zerbi et al. (2015), which was 296

in the field (Figure 6A). This atlas partitions the brain into six functional systems: the somatosensory 298 

(anchored by primary motor and somatosensory cortices), sensory (visual and auditory cortices), olfac-299 

tory (olfactory bulb and prefrontal areas), limbic (hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus, and cingulate), 300 

basal ganglia (striatum), and cerebellar networks. 301

systems, we performed a bidirectional geometric analysis (Figure 6B; see Section 4.3.4 for details). 303

# 3 Discussion 444 

A central goal of systems neuroscience is to understand the principles that govern large-scale functional 445 

organization in the brain. In this study, we addressed two related challenges that have complicated this 446 

goal: evaluating the extent to which different organizational principles are compatible with functional 447 

connectivity data, and determining whether the resulting network organization is best characterized 448 

by a single partition or by a more heterogeneous solution landscape. 449 

To address the first challenge, we employed a Bayesian generative modeling framework that makes 450 

explicit the assumptions underlying different organizational hypotheses. By comparing these models 451 

on a common information-theoretic scale (total description length), we found that within the set of 452 

models considered here, a non-degree-corrected hierarchical formulation provides the most parsimo-453 

nious (thus most compatible) description of the observed functional connectivity patterns, and the 454 

purely assortative formulation the least (see Section 2.1). This does not falsify the importance of 455 

assortativity — which is undeniably a fundamental principle of brain networks Betzel et al. (2018b) — 456 

but it demonstrates that enforcing pure assortativity alone may give an incomplete description of the 457 

system. We specifically focused our analysis on the middle level of this hierarchy because its spatial 458 

scale is highly comparable to canonical resting-state networks (RSNs). When examined in relation 459 

to these standard RSNs, the inferred communities showed a systematic reorganization: primary sen-460 

sory systems were largely preserved as cohesive units, whereas higher-order associative systems were 461 

distributed across multiple communities (see Section 2.3). These results suggest that canonical RSN 462 

definitions may aggregate distinct substructures that can be further resolved by generative modeling. 463 

To address the second challenge, we examined the assumption that the space of network partitions 464 

is well summarized by a single consensus solution. By characterizing the posterior distribution over 465 

partitions under the hierarchical model, we identified multiple distinct modes (organizational schemes 466 

— topological arrangements of nodes (ROIs) into communities) in the solution landscape (see Section 467 

2.4). Statistical testing did not reveal a significant difference in the prevalence of these modes across 468 

bootstrap resamples, indicating that no single organizational scheme dominates the landscape within 469 

the resolution of the present analysis. Further analysis showed that differences between modes arise 470 

from structured variability in the assignment of specific regions, with some communities exhibiting 471 

stable composition and others showing systematic variation across modes (see Section 2.5). 472 

Taken together, these results suggest that, within the present modeling framework, the functional 473 

connectome is not adequately described by a single partition. Instead, the data support a set of alterna-474 

tive partitions that differ in the assignment of specific regions while preserving broader organizational 475 

structure. This perspective provides a potential explanation for variability across previous studies, 476 

which may arise when a heterogeneous solution landscape is forced into a single consensus partition. 477 

## 3.1 Inferred communities refine canonical networks 478 

The communities inferred under the hierarchical model exhibit clear spatial organization, forming 479 

contiguous and bilaterally symmetric patterns despite the absence of explicit spatial priors. These 480 

features indicate that the generative model captures structured aspects of the functional connectivity 481 

data. However, when evaluated against a canonical resting-state network (RSN) atlas Zerbi et al. 482 

(2015), the inferred communities reveal systematic differences in how functional boundaries are drawn, 483 

particularly between sensory and associative cortices. 484 

Bidirectional geometric analysis demonstrated that primary sensory systems remain highly cohe-485 

sive. The canonical somatosensory network, for instance, mapped closely onto the inferred Somatomo-486 

tor community, indicating a strong correspondence between this RSN and the community identified by 487 

the model. In contrast, higher-order association networks were distributed across multiple inferred com-488 

munities. For example, the canonical limbic system was partitioned across the Olfactory-Prefrontal, 489 

Medial-Temporal, and Striato-Amygdalar communities, suggesting that this RSN encompasses multi-490 

ple distinct substructures under the present framework. 491 

Rather than invalidating canonical networks, these differences highlight a distinction in resolution 492 

and methodology. The RSN atlas was derived from post-hoc clustering of independent components 493 

Zerbi et al. (2015), providing a highly useful and broad heuristic for functional macro-regions. Con-494 

versely, our generative framework infers community structure directly from the data under an explicit 495 

probabilistic model. As a result, the generative model refines these broad territories, suggesting that 496 

some canonical RSNs, especially those associated with associative processing, may actually reflect 497

## 3.2 Multiplicity reveals structured variability across communities 502 

Mapping the posterior distribution of the optimal hierarchical model revealed that the functional con-503 

nectome is characterized by multiple distinct modes in the space of network partitions (the solution 504 

landscape). In contrast, evaluating the connectome using modularity maximization yielded a largely 505 

uni-modal landscape. Again, this does not falsify the importance of assortativity, but it demonstrates 506 

that enforcing a pure assortativity as the sole organizational rule provides an incomplete descrip-507 

tion that obscures underlying multiplicity. More flexible generative models admit a broader range of 508 

topological configurations that are equally consistent with the observed data. 509 

This contrast highlights an important theoretical nuance regarding Bayesian model selection Gel-510 

man et al. (2013); Murphy (2023). From an information-theoretic perspective, the total description 511 

length (TDL) inherently penalizes the entropy of the posterior distribution, favoring models with uni-512 

modal solution landscapes. Ideally, if a generative model perfectly captured the true underlying process 513 

of the data, it would yield a unique and unambiguous partition. The fact that our optimal model yields 514 

a multi-modal landscape indicates a degree of structural uncertainty, reflecting a mild “mis-fit” between 515 

the generative constraints of hierarchies and the profound complexity of the functional connectome 516 

Peixoto (2021); Grunwald (2004). However, as demonstrated by our analysis, possessing a uni-modal 517 

landscape is not a guarantee of a more compatible model. Modularity maximization forced an almost 518 

entirely uni-modal landscape, yet it provided the least compatible description of the data. Therefore, 519 

while this multiplicity serves as a strong motivation to explore even more advanced generative mod-520 

els in the future, it currently provides the most accurate and honest reflection of the connectome’s 521 

architecture (under our modeling choices). 522 

To quantify how there modes differ, we analyzed the variability or regional assignments across 523 

the identified modes. This analysis revealed that variability is not uniformly distributed across the 524 

connectome, but instead follows a structured anatomical pattern. Some communities exhibited highly 525 

consistent assignments across modes, indicating stable organization. In contrast, other communities 526 

displayed substantial variability in their regional composition. For example, the Somatomotor commu-527 

nity exhibited relatively constrained variability, while communities such as the Fronto-Insular-Striatal 528 

and Olfactory-Prefrontal-Thalamic systems showed broader variability, with systematic changes in 529 

cortical and subcortical affiliations. These patterns suggest that differences between modes arise from 530 

coordinated reassignments of subsets of regions, rather than from random fluctuations or global reor-531 

ganization. 532 

Importantly, these results must be interpreted within the scope of the statistical framework em-533 

ployed here. The identified modes reflect distinct high-probability partitions under the hierarchical 534 

generative model applied to a static functional connectivity network. As such, they characterize alter-535 

native ways of clustering the same time-averaged data from anesthetized animals, rather than directly 536 

corresponding to distinct functional states or dynamic information routing. Future work utilizing time-537 

varying functional connectivity Lurie et al. (2020), or temporal independent component analysis Xu 538 

et al. (2022) will be required to determine whether these alternative static partitions relate to transient 539 

temporal fluctuations. 540 

## 3.3 Methodological considerations 541 

While our Bayesian framework identifies a non-degree-corrected hierarchical architecture as the most 542 

compatible organizational principle, we do not claim this represents the absolute ground truth of the 543 

brain. In generative modeling, model selection is inherently relative to the specific hypothesis space 544 

evaluated Murphy (2022, 2023). It remains possible, and is encouraged, that future theoretical advances 545 

will propose even more sophisticated generative principles — ones that do not exist yet — that surpass 546 

discrete hierarchies, potentially including time-varying generative rules capable of modeling fMRI time 547 

series data directly (for example see Peixoto (2024)). 548 

A secondary consideration involves our specific model preferences. For instance, while the non-549 

degree-corrected models were preferred over degree-corrected variants — a result that could intuitively 550

suggest “rich-club” topographies where highly connected hubs group together — this preference must 551 

be interpreted with caution. Our network construction retained the top 20% of edges, a thresholding 552 

procedure that imposes strict constraints on the degree distribution. Furthermore, BOLD-derived 553 

correlation matrices possess inherent degree homogeneity Zalesky et al. (2012); Van Den Heuvel et al. 554 

(2017); Fornito et al. (2016). Without extensive sensitivity analyses, we cannot definitively conclude 555

of these methodological constraints. 557 

Finally, three additional methodological caveats contextualize our findings. First, functional MRI 558 

relies on the BOLD signal, which introduces spatial and temporal blurring that can alter the topology 559 

of functional boundaries Vafaii et al. (2024). Consequently, the exact spatial configurations of our 560 

inferred communities are inevitably influenced by vascular architecture. 561 

Second, the data were acquired from anesthetized mice. General anesthesia, and its specific dosage, 562 

globally depresses spontaneous cortical activity Bukhari et al. (2017); Barttfeld et al. (2015). Therefore, 563 

the structural multiplicity reported here strictly characterizes the anesthetized connectome. Future 564 

investigations in awake, behaving animals — and utilizing direct mesoscopic neural readouts like wide-565 

field calcium imaging Lake et al. (2020) — are required to fully characterize the topological multiplicity 566 

of the brain. 567 

Third, our results inherently depend on the choice of spatial parcellation. While our generative 568 

models received no spatial priors during community inference, the definition of the network nodes 569 

themselves relied on the Allen CCFv3 atlas. The size, shape, and anatomically predefined boundaries 570 

of these regions impose a foundational structural prior on the network topology. Future work should 571 

investigate whether deriving functionally-driven parcels directly from the time-series data (rather than 572 

relying on an anatomical reference atlas) alters the multiplicity of the solution landscape. 573 

## 3.4 Conclusion 574 

To summarize, our Bayesian generative modeling framework provides a principled re-evaluation of 575 

macroscopic functional organization in the mouse brain. By explicitly comparing competing topological 576 

hypotheses, we demonstrated that the functional connectome is optimally described by a hierarchical 577 

architecture, wherein broad associative networks are revealed to be composite structures composed of 578 

distinct sub-circuits. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the static functional connectome does not 579 

easily admit a singular consensus partition. Instead, the data support a multi-modal landscape of co-580 

dominant organizational schemes, driven by structured variability of specific regional affiliations around 581 

structurally stable sensory anchors. Ultimately, these findings underscore a critical methodological shift 582 

for systems neuroscience: to accurately map the functional connectome, analytical frameworks must 583 

move beyond enforcing singular, rigid solutions and embrace the brain’s inherent structural variation. 584

## 4.2 Framework of Statistical inference 707 

4.2.1 Bayesian generative modeling 708 

To infer the community arrangements underlying the observed functional networks, we adopted a 709 

Bayesian generative modeling approach. This statistical framework (Peel et al., 2022) allows us to 710 

formally test competing hypotheses about the principles of brain network organization, moving beyond 711 

heuristic pattern description to rigorous statistical inference. The core idea is to reframe the central 712 

question from “what communities exist?” to “how likely is it that a specific underlying community 713 

structure generated our observed network?” 714 

This framework relies on a generative model, which acts as a probabilistic recipe for how a network is 715 

constructed based on a latent community structure and a set of organizational rules. Using Bayesian 716 

inference, we invert this process to calculate the posterior probability of any proposed community 717 

partition. This calculation balances the prior (the assumptions of a given organizational hypothesis, 718 

such as assortativity) against the likelihood (the probability of observing the actual brain network if 719 

that hypothesized structure were true). 720 

Crucially, to identify the optimal community structure without arbitrarily fixing the number of 721 

communities in advance, we framed this probabilistic inference as an information-theoretic optimization 722 

problem. The posterior probability of a partition can be rewritten in terms of an information theoretic 723 

quantity called description length Σ, that quantifies the amount of bits required to describe the network 724 

in terms of the partition. Following the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle (Rissanen, 1998; 725 

Grunwald, 2004), the most probable explanation for a dataset is the one that provides the most efficient 726 

compression of that data. Therefore, our inference algorithm searches for the community partition that 727 

minimizes the description length (Σ) of the network. A shorter description length indicates a more 728 

parsimonious, and computationally more probable, model of the network’s true organization (see 5.1.2 729 

for the complete mathematical formulation). 730 

4.2.2 Generative models of brain organization 731 

We instantiated our Bayesian framework using several variants of the stochastic block model (SBM), 732 

specifically as formulated in (Peixoto, 2019). At its core, an SBM is a generative model for networks 733 

which posits that nodes are organized into groups, or “blocks”, and that the probability of a connection 734 

between any two nodes depends solely on the blocks to which they belong. All SBM variants and 735 

subsequent model fitting procedures were implemented using the graph-tool Python library (Peixoto, 736 

2014b). 737 

Each SBM variant represents a concrete, testable hypothesis about the organizational principles 738 

shaping brain networks. We evaluated three primary structural architectures (see 5.1.3 for complete 739 

mathematical formulations): 740 

• Standard SBM (s-SBM) (Peixoto, 2017): This model serves as a flexible foundation. It 741 

partitions nodes into groups but imposes no prior constraints on how these blocks connect. It 742 

can capture a wide range of topographies, from segregated assortative modules to integrative 743 

core-periphery or bipartite arrangements (Betzel et al., 2018a). 744 

• Hierarchical SBM (h-SBM) (Peixoto, 2014c): Brain networks are thought to be organized 745 

across multiple scales. The h-SBM explicitly models this by assuming communities are nested 746 

within larger, higher-order communities, recursively partitioning the network to reveal a multi-747 

scale hierarchy. 748

• Overlapping SBM (o-SBM) (Peixoto, 2015): To account for brain regions participating in 749 

multiple functional systems, the o-SBM partitions the connections (half-edges) rather than the 750

Furthermore, for each of these three architectures, we tested both non-degree-corrected (nd-) and 753

science (Karrer and Newman, 2011). Non-degree-corrected models assume all nodes within a commu-755 

nity have a statistically similar number of connections. In contrast, degree-corrected models decouple 756 

a node’s overall connection strength from its community assignment. 757 

This distinction pits two competing neurobiological hypotheses against each other. A degree-758 

corrected model treats a region’s “hubness” (Bullmore and Sporns, 2009) as an intrinsic local property 759 

separate from its community. Conversely, a non-degree-corrected model assumes connectivity is a 760 

feature of the community itself (e.g., all nodes in a “hub community” form a highly connected “rich-761 

club”). Comparing these variants reveals which organizational principle better explains the empirical 762 

functional architecture. 763 

4.2.3 The modularity benchmark and its generative counterpart 764 

A widely used method for brain community detection is modularity maximization (Newman, 2006; 765 

Sporns and Betzel, 2016), which identifies partitions with dense within-community connections and 766 

sparse between-community connections. While powerful, this is a descriptive heuristic that optimizes 767 

a single score (modularity), rather than a generative model explaining network formation. 768 

However, modularity maximization is mathematically equivalent to maximum likelihood estimation 769 

of a special case of the degree-corrected SBM, known as the degree-corrected Planted Partition model 770 

(which we refer to as the assortative SBM (a-SBM) ; see 5.1.4) (Newman, 2016; Zhang and Peixoto, 771 

2020). 772 

This equivalence implies both methods seek to explain the exact same underlying principle: strict 773 

assortativity. Yet, they do so fundamentally differently. Modularity maximization will forcefully return 774 

a high-scoring assortative solution regardless of whether that configuration is genuinely supported by 775 

the data. In contrast, the a-SBM assigns probabilities to configurations and penalizes models that lack 776 

statistical evidence. This allows us to directly test the validity of the assortative assumption: if modu-777 

larity maximization yields stable solutions, but the a-SBM fails to identify a coherent configuration, it 778 

indicates that modularity is overfitting to incidental noise rather than detecting true structural rules. 779 

4.2.4 Model fitting: MCMC simulations 780 

The posterior distribution over all possible network partitions—which we refer to as the “solution 781 

landscape”—is incredibly vast and high-dimensional, making it computationally impossible to solve 782 

analytically. Therefore, we approximated this posterior distribution using Markov chain Monte Carlo 783 

(MCMC) sampling (Gelman et al., 2013). 784 

At its core, MCMC is an algorithm that starts at a random partition and explores the landscape by 785 

iteratively proposing changes to the community assignments, preferentially accepting changes that im-786 

prove the model’s fit. We utilized a highly advanced MCMC algorithm implemented in the graph-tool 787 

Python library (Peixoto, 2014b) specifically curated for graph structures. This algorithm explores the 788 

landscape using two smart, topology-driven mechanisms: first, it uses neighborhood-guided proposals, 789 

as opposed to random proposals, relocating a node to a community where it already has existing con-790 

nections (Peixoto, 2014a). Second, to escape getting trapped in local minima, it utilizes macroscopic 791 

merge-split proposals, periodically merging two entire communities together or splitting one in half to 792 

make massive leaps across the solution landscape (Peixoto, 2020). 793 

This guided “random-walk” yields an ensemble of highly probable partitions that accurately repre-794 

sent the true posterior distribution (see 5.1.5 for exact acceptance criteria). For each generative model 795 

evaluated, we ran five independent MCMC chains, each for 100,000 steps. Running five chains is a 796 

standard, robust practice in Bayesian inference (Gelman et al., 2013); it provides a mathematically 797 

sufficient number of pairwise comparisons to ensure the sampler has fully explored the global landscape 798 

from different starting points, without incurring prohibitive computational costs. 799

4.2.5 Validating model faithfulness: MCMC convergence diagnostics 800 

Before comparing different models (that is, organizational principles), it is crucial to ensure that each 801 

model provides a “faithful” explanation of the data. This diagnostic step is grounded in a core principle 802 

of Bayesian inference: a mathematically well-specified model whose assumptions are compatible with 803 

the network’s underlying structure should produce a single, coherent posterior landscape (Peixoto, 804 

2023). While this landscape may be complex, it should be continuously connected and thoroughly 805 

explorable. 806 

The convergence of multiple, independent MCMC chains onto this exact same landscape serves 807 

as a direct test of the model’s coherence. If independent chains initialized from different starting 808 

points consistently find and explore the same high-probability regions, it indicates that the model’s 809 

assumptions align with the data. Conversely, if chains become permanently trapped in irreconcilable, 810 

disconnected regions, it strongly indicates that the model’s structural assumptions are incompatible 811 

with the data, causing the posterior landscape to pathologically fracture. 812 

To assess this, we monitored the description length (Σ) distributions sampled by each independent 813 

chain. Convergence was evaluated both qualitatively (via distribution overlap) and quantitatively 814 

using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) distance (see 5.1.6). 815 

As chains converge onto a unified landscape, their sampled Σ distributions should become nearly 816 

identical. To formalize this, we utilized the KS distance as a strict geometric tolerance limit. We 817 

required that the absolute maximum difference between the distributions of any two chains drop below 818 

a standard threshold of 0.2 (Gibbons and Chakraborti, 2011) (see 5.1.6 for details). Models that failed 819 

to achieve this strict spatial convergence across all independent chains were considered unfaithful 820 

representations of the data and were excluded from all subsequent topological analyses. 821 

## 4.3 Analysis Procedures 822 

4.3.1 Identifying the organizing principle: Model selection via Total Description Length 823 

After identifying the set of faithful models that successfully achieved MCMC convergence, we performed 824 

a formal model comparison to determine which organizational principle provided the most parsimonious 825 

explanation of the mouse functional connectome. We evaluated the competing models based on their 826 

model evidence—the probability of observing the network data integrated over the entire ensemble of 827 

possible partitions generated by the model. 828 

For practical computation and information-theoretic interpretation, we calculated the negative 829 

logarithm of this evidence, termed the Total Description Length (TDL). Following the Minimum 830 

Description Length (MDL) principle (Rissanen, 1998), TDL quantifies the total number of bits required 831 

to compactly represent the network data using the specific structural rules of the model. The model 832 

yielding the lowest TDL provides the most efficient data compression and is formally considered the 833 

optimal, most probable generative explanation. 834 

Intuitively, TDL acts as a rigorous mathematical formulation of Occam’s razor (see 5.1.7 for exact 835 

derivation). TDL integrates over the entire solution landscape (that is, the posterior distribution of 836 

partitions), rather than evaluating a model solely on a single “best-fit” partition. Crucially, while TDL 837 

penalizes unnecessary model complexity, it does not artificially punish a model for producing a multi-838 

modal solution landscape. It calculates a precise balance between the model’s average goodness-of-fit 839 

and the entropy (the statistical uncertainty, or “spread”) of its solution landscape. If the data inher-840 

ently contains multiple valid organizational schemes, TDL accommodates this multiplicity, effectively 841 

preferring a model that accurately captures the degenerate solution landscape over a simpler model 842 

that underfits the data by forcing a single, uni-modal consensus Peixoto (2021). 843 

For each network, TDL was calculated for each independent MCMC chain using the graph-tool 844 

Python library (Peixoto, 2014b) and averaged across chains to provide a single, stable estimate. By 845 

comparing these final TDL values, we objectively adjudicated between our competing structural hy-846 

potheses, testing whether the resting-state functional architecture is best governed by simple assor-847 

tative modules, overlapping communities, or a nested hierarchy, thereby selecting the single optimal 848 

generative framework for all subsequent topological analyses. 849

4.3.2 Characterizing global multiplicity: organizational schemes of the whole brain 850 

Partition alignment. A critical step before analyzing the partitions generated by MCMC sam-851 

pling is alignment. Because community labels are arbitrarily assigned during the sampling process, 852 

equivalent functional groups might receive different integer labels across different samples—a well-853 

known statistical issue termed “label switching” (Stephens, 2000). To resolve this, we utilized the 854 

random label model (Peixoto, 2021). Conceptually, this model posits that all sampled partitions are 855 

variations of a single, hidden “canonical” labeling template, but with their integer labels randomly per-856 

muted. By treating the observed assignments as random permutations, the algorithm mathematically 857 

“un-shuffles” the labels of each individual sample. It maximizes the node-for-node overlap between 858 

partitions, explicitly matching and re-numbering groups of nodes that consistently cluster together 859 

(see 5.1.8 for algorithmic details). This ensures that a given community label represents the exact 860 

same community across the entire ensemble. 861 

Multimodal mixture model. We hypothesized that the posterior distribution of the functional 862 

connectome might not form a single, narrow peak, but rather a complex, multi-modal landscape rep-863 

resenting multiple distinct topological configurations. To explicitly test this hypothesis and discover 864 

potential multiplicity, we clustered the vast ensemble of aligned MCMC samples into a discrete, in-865 

terpretable set of K distinct solution modes (Peixoto, 2021). Conceptually, this algorithm groups 866 

structurally similar network partitions together to identify the dominant “peaks” (or centers of mass) 867 

within the highly complex solution landscape. 868 

The mixture model yields two key outputs for each mode k:869 

1. The mode probability ( ωk): The relative mass, or fraction of the overall posterior landscape, 870 

occupied by mode k, representing its statistical likelihood (that is, P 

> k

ωk = 1). 871 

2. The node-membership probability matrix ( π(k)): A matrix defining the precise topological 872 

structure of the mode, where entries denote the probability of a specific ROI (a row of the matrix) 873 

belonging to a specific community (a column of the matrix). 874 

This two-tiered summary quantifies both the structural uncertainty within a given organizational mode 875 

(via π(k)) and the statistical uncertainty between competing modes (via ωk). 876 

Topological merging of redundant modes. The mixture model identifies statistically distinct 877 

peaks in the posterior landscape. However, two closely situated peaks might represent the exact 878 

same macroscopic topological state, differing only by minor noise or probability scaling localized to 879 

a few ROIs. To bridge the gap between strict statistical non-equivalence and true neurobiological 880 

redundancy, we performed a post-hoc structural merge of the identified modes. 881 

Procedurally, we compared the overall topological “shape” of every mode against every other mode 882 

using a geometric similarity metric (cosine similarity). To guarantee we only merged modes that 883 

were genuinely redundant (and not simply similar by chance) we generated a random baseline by 884 

repeatedly shuffling the ROI labels. Modes that were significantly more similar to each other than 885 

to this spatially shuffled baseline were mathematically consolidated (see 5.1.8 for exact permutation 886 

and FDR-correction details). The resulting merged clusters, with their probabilities ( ωk) summed 887 

together, represent the final, parsimonious set of macroscopic organizational schemes — the distinct 888 

ways in which the whole brain can functionally organize itself under a given organizational principle 889 

(e.g., hierarchical). 890

4.3.3 Defining the reliable shape of communities 908 

Before analyzing the fine-grained structural multiplicity of individual communities, we first rigorously 909 

define their topological shapes—that is, the set of ROIs that reliably constitute a community. 910

membership matrix. We computed the weighted average (proportional to ωk) of the node-membership 912 

matrices ( π(k)) across all schemes identified within that resample. This step provides a single expected 913 

node-membership matrix that accounts for the structural uncertainty across schemes (see 5.1.9 for 914 

exact formulations). 915 

With an expected node-membership matrix generated for each resample, our next step was to 916 

separate true member ROIs from high-variance background noise. We compiled the distribution of 917 

each ROI’s expected membership for a community across the 500 bootstrap resamples and tested it 918

(see 5.1.9)—“survived” this thresholding process. 921 

ROIs that failed to survive this test were classified as incidental noise for that specific community 922 

and masked out. The surviving ROIs formally defined the reliable spatial boundaries of the community. 923 

4.3.4 Biological contextualization of inferred communities: Resemblance to canonical 924 

resting-state networks 925 

Having established the strict spatial boundaries of our inferred communities, our next objective was to 926 

biologically contextualize these communities. We achieved this by evaluating their spatial alignment 927 

with a canonical set of functional networks. 928 

Canonical functional systems. We adopted the resting-state network (RSN) atlas defined by Zerbi 929 

et al. (Zerbi et al., 2015) as our reference atlas. This atlas provides a standardized parcellation of the 930 

mouse brain into six broadly recognized macroscopic functional systems that have been consistently 931 

found in multi-center study (Grandjean et al., 2020): somatosensory, sensorimotor (motor, visual, 932 

auditory), olfactory, limbic, basal ganglia, and cerebellum. 933 

Bidirectional spatial mapping: Specificity and Coverage. To quantify the structural corre-934 

spondence between our inferred communities and the canonical RSNs, we computed two complemen-935 

tary geometric metrics: Specificity and Coverage . Specificity measures the proportion of an inferred 936 

community’s spatial volume that falls strictly within a single canonical RSN. Reciprocally, Coverage 937 

measures the proportion of a canonical RSN’s spatial volume captured by an inferred community. These 938 

metrics were computed for each organizational scheme separately and averaged (weighted by ωk) to 939 

yield an expected spatial mapping per bootstrap resample (see 5.1.10 for mathematical formulations). 940 

Quantifying integration: Effective network mappings. To translate these spatial overlaps into 941 

interpretable topological principles (i.e., segregation versus integration), we utilized an information-942 

theoretic approach. By treating a community’s specificity profile across the six canonical systems 943 

as a probability distribution, we calculated its Shannon entropy to derive the effective number of 944 

spanned RSNs (see 5.1.10 for mathematical formulations). A value near 1 indicates a highly segregated 945 

community nested within a single canonical system, whereas a value greater than 1 quantitatively 946 

identifies an integrative community bridging multiple systems. We applied this identical entropy 947 

framework symmetrically to the coverage profiles to determine whether a canonical system remains 948 

cohesive or is functionally subdivided by our generative framework. 949

4.3.5 Characterizing structural variation within individual communities 950 

An organizational scheme represents a distinct macroscopic topological configuration of the functional 951 

connectome. Across the probability landscape of different schemes, a specific community may exhibit 952 

varied spatial boundaries, or merge with another community or split. Here we zoom in and characterize 953 

the topological multiplicity of the individual communities themselves. 954 

Discovering distinct topological patterns: generative clustering. For a given community, we 955 

extracted its representative membership probability vectors (the c-th column of the π matrix) from 956 

every organizational scheme across all 500 bootstrap resamples. Crucially, these vectors were strictly 957 

masked to include only the community’s surviving ROIs (as defined in 4.3.3). This masking is a 958 

necessary prerequisite: high-dimensional clustering algorithms are extremely sensitive to uninformative 959 

dimensions (Raftery and Dean, 2006; Bouveyron and Brunet-Saumard, 2014). Including regions that 960 

primarily contribute background noise obscures true spatial boundaries, causing the algorithm to overfit 961 

and artificially divide the data. 962 

We grouped these high-signal vectors using Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) (Murphy, 2012). 963 

We utilized GMMs because, similar to the multimodal mixture model used in Section 4.3.2, they are 964 

probabilistic generative models. By modeling the ensemble as a mixture of Gaussians, the algorithm 965 

identifies K dominant centers of mass within the solution space. For each cluster k, the GMM yields 966 

two key outputs: (1) The mixing weight ( ωk), representing the relative prominence of the cluster in 967 

the ensemble, and (2) The centroid ( v(k)), a node-membership vector defining the exact topological 968 

structure (i.e., the distinct spatial pattern) represented by the cluster. 969 

Unlike our previous non-parametric models, GMMs require the number of clusters to be provided a970 

priori . To objectively determine the optimal number of spatial patterns, we evaluated candidate GMMs 971 

using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Consistent with the information-theoretic principles 972 

used for our macroscopic model selection, the BIC can be interpreted as the total number of bits 973 

required to compactly encode the ensemble of membership vectors using k Gaussian clusters. It resolves 974 

the statistical tradeoff between accuracy and complexity by rewarding goodness-of-fit while strictly 975 

penalizing the addition of unnecessary parameters (see 5.1.11 for algorithmic details). The minimum 976 

BIC value thus identifies the “sweet spot” of the data, revealing the most parsimonious number of 977 

spatial patterns statistically supported by the ensemble. Crucially, this data-driven optimization does 978 

not force multiplicity; if a community exhibits no meaningful structural variation across the landscape, 979 

the minimum BIC will correspond to a single cluster ( k = 1). 980 

Topological merging of redundant patterns. While the minimum BIC identifies the statistically 981 

optimal number of Gaussian components, generative mixture models optimize strictly for probabilistic 982 

likelihood and do not inherently encode topological constraints (Theis et al., 2016). Consequently, a 983 

GMM may artificially split a single continuous spatial pattern into multiple mathematically distinct 984 

components simply due to magnitude differences (e.g., one cluster having uniformly stronger or weaker 985 

membership probabilities overall) (Hennig, 2010). 986 

Because resolving this redundancy cannot be done using likelihood alone, we consolidated clusters 987 

based on a neurobiological criterion: spatial collinearity. We first applied the community’s survival 988 

mask (defined in 4.3.3) to the GMM centroids, zeroing-out regions whose expected membership was 989 

statistically zero. We then measured the shape redundancy of these masked centroids using cosine 990 

distance, a geometric metric that evaluates spatial alignment while perfectly ignoring magnitude dif-991 

ferences. Clusters whose masked centroids had a cosine distance less than expected by chance (assessed 992 

via a spatially shuffled null model, FDR-corrected; see 5.1.11) were formally merged. The final merged 993 

clusters defined the distinct patterns in which the community manifests, and their weights ( ωk) were 994 

summed to reflect the total prominence of the distinct pattern. 995 

Isolating structurally stable and structurally variable ROIs. Having identified the distinct 996 

ROI assignment patterns a community exhibits across the landscape, our final objective was to pinpoint 997 

exactly which ROIs drive this structural variation. We evaluated the assignment behavior of individual 998 

ROIs by comparing their distributions of assignment probabilities across the distinct patterns using 999 

a Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis H-test (a non-parametric one-way ANOVA). We tested the 1000 

null hypothesis that an ROI’s assignment distribution is identical across all patterns. To ensure 1001 

statistical validity, we first corrected for intra-sample dependency—arising because a single bootstrap 1002

5.1.5 Markov chain Monte Carlo implementation details 1090 

To infer the community structure, we must find the partitions ( b) that have the highest posterior 1091 

probability, P (b | A), corresponding to the lowest description lengths (Σ). Because the space of all 1092 

possible partitions is too vast to search exhaustively, we approximate the posterior distribution using 1093 

the advanced Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms developed by Peixoto (Peixoto, 2014a, 1094 

2020). 1095 

Standard MCMC algorithms propose moving a single node to a new community chosen uniformly at 1096 

random. This results in exceedingly slow mixing times and trapping in local minima. The graph-tool 1097 

implementation overcomes this by utilizing two specialized move proposals: 1098 

1. Neighborhood-guided local moves: When proposing a new community assignment r for 1099 

node i, the target community is not chosen at random. Instead, it is proposed with a probability 1100 

proportional to the number of existing connections between the neighbors of node i and the 1101 

nodes already residing in community r (Peixoto, 2014a). This leverages local network topology 1102 

to guide the search aggressively toward more probable solutions. 1103 

2. Macroscopic merge-split moves: To escape deep local minima separated by valleys of low 1104 

probability, the algorithm periodically proposes merging two entire communities together, or 1105 

splitting a single community into two (Peixoto, 2020). This allows the Markov chain to make 1106 

macroscopic leaps across the posterior landscape that would be impossible via single-node up-1107 

dates. 1108 

Regardless of the proposal type, all moves are formally evaluated using the Metropolis-Hastings 1109 

criterion (Metropolis et al., 1953; Hastings, 1970). If a proposed move decreases the description 1110 

length, it is strictly accepted. If it increases the description length, it is accepted with a probability 1111 

proportional to the difference in description lengths, ensuring the algorithm maintains detailed balance 1112 

while exploring the global landscape. 1113

Dimensionality reduction for generative clustering: Prior to clustering, the membership vec-1261 

tors were strictly masked to include only the surviving ROIs of the community. This masking is a 1262

dimensionality, data points become sparse, and the distance (or density) contrast between true clusters 1266 

and noise diminishes—a phenomenon known as the curse of dimensionality (Bouveyron and Brunet-1267 

Saumard, 2014). By masking out non-surviving ROIs, we restrict the GMM to a high-signal subspace, 1268 

allowing it to accurately model the covariance structure and estimate the true likelihoods of the un-1269 

derlying topological patterns. 1270 

Model selection via Bayesian Information Criterion: All GMM fitting and evaluation proce-1271 

dures were implemented using the scikit-learn library in Python (Pedregosa et al., 2011). For a 1272 

given community, GMMs were fitted to the masked membership vectors across a range of candidate 1273 

cluster counts ( k = 1 , 2, · · · , 10) using maximum likelihood estimation. To objectively identify the 1274 

optimal number of spatial patterns, we selected the model that minimized the Bayesian Information 1275 

Criterion (BIC). The BIC formalizes the tradeoff between a model’s goodness-of-fit and its complexity: 1276 

BIC = −2 ln( ˆL) + p ln( n) (15) where ˆL is the maximized likelihood of the model given the data, p is the total number of estimated 1277 

parameters (which increases with k), and n is the number of membership vectors. The first term 1278 

rewards models that accurately capture the data’s structure, while the second term strictly penalizes 1279 

the addition of unnecessary Gaussian components, thereby preventing overfitting. 1280 

Topological merging of redundant patterns: To resolve structural redundancy among the op-1281 

timal GMM components, we evaluated the spatial collinearity of their centroids (mean vectors, v(k))1282 

using cosine distance. For two centroids i and j, the cosine distance is: 1283 

DC (v(i), v (j)) = 1 − v(i) · v(j)

∥v(i)∥∥ v(j)∥ (16) This metric isolates the relative distribution of membership strengths across ROIs (the shape) while 1284 

ignoring uniform differences in probability magnitude. 1285 

To determine statistical significance, we generated a spatial null distribution by randomly shuffling 1286 

the ROI indices of the centroids 1,000 times and recalculating the pairwise cosine distances. An 1287 

empirical p-value was calculated for each pair based on the proportion of null distances that were smaller 1288 

than the true observed distance. We pooled these p-values and applied Benjamini-Hochberg False 1289 

Discovery Rate (FDR) correction ( α = 0 .05) (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). Clusters exhibiting 1290 

statistically significant shape redundancy were merged via connected components. The centroid of 1291 

each newly merged entity served as the representative spatial pattern, and the mixing weights ( ωk) of 1292 

its constituent GMM clusters were summed. 1293

for individual ROIs across the distinct patterns, we first had to ensure the observations were statistically 1295 

independent. Because a single bootstrap resample can yield multiple organizational schemes, it can 1296

## 5.3 Multiplicity in organizational schemes for the modularity maximization 1307 

## method 1308 

To verify that the multiple organizational schemes observed in our results are a genuine property of the 1309 

functional connectome under a generative framework, rather than an algorithmic artifact of our multi-1310 

modal mixture model pipeline, we applied the exact same Bayesian inference procedure to partitions 1311 

generated via traditional modularity maximization Newman and Girvan (2004). 1312 

The modularity objective landscape is known to be a highly irregular “rugged plateau” of struc-1313 

turally similar partitions Good et al. (2010). If our clustering algorithm artificially forces continuous 1314 

variation into distinct macroscopic modes, we would expect to see false multiplicity here as well. 1315 

Instead, applying our clustering pipeline to the modularity maximization results yielded an effec-1316 

tively uni-modal landscape. While our procedure detected two schemes, their occurrence probabilities 1317 

were massively skewed (Figure 14). A single dominant organizational scheme occurred almost exclu-1318 

sively ( ω1 ≈ 1), while the alternative scheme was exceptionally rare ( ω2 ≈ 0). Non-parametric statisti-1319 

cal testing confirmed this profound asymmetry in prevalence (large effect size, p < 0.05). Furthermore, 1320 

the topological differences between this dominant scheme and the rare variant were exclusively localized 1321 

to minor reconfigurations in the hindbrain (not shown); the entire cortical and subcortical functional 1322 

architecture remained invariant across the landscape. Because the clustering algorithm successfully 1323 

collapsed the rugged modularity plateau into a single unified cortico-subcortical architecture, this 1324 

suggests that our procedure does not artificially hallucinate multiplicity. Consequently, the distinct, 1325 

co-dominant schemes identified by our generative framework may reflect genuinely distinct whole-brain 1326 

functional organizations. 1327
