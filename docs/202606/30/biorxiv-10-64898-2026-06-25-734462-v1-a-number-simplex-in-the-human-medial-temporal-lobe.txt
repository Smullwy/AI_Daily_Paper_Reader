ABSTRACT 37 

38 

Humans handle numbers nimbly, suggesting a richer neural manifold structure than the prevalent 39 

mental number line model. In populations of medial temporal lobe (MTL) neurons in humans performing 40 

two simple tasks (dot counting and arithmetic), we find robust neural coding of numerosity that results in 41 

high dimensional, simplex-shaped manifolds. This shape affords more flexibility than a linear manifold 42 

due to its high shattering dimensionality and expressibility. Dot arrays and Arabic numerals evoked 43 

distinct simplicial population codes, yet they were linked by a linearly transferable latent structure within 44 

the same task. We find similar simplicial geometry of number representations in large language models 45 

(LLMs). Moreover, subjects’ internally computed arithmetic results were decodable during the 46 

calculation period, with decoding accuracy correlating with individual mathematical capacity. Finally, 47 

linear transformations of simplicial operand representations modeled the brain's conversion of operands 48 

into decodable results, suggesting that the brain’s arithmetic procedures have some resemblance to the 49 

attention architecture of LLMs. Together, these findings establish a high dimensional representational 50 

foundation for numerical cognition in the brain. 51

INTRODUCTION 56 

Humans have the ability to process numbers flexibly. We can understand a wide range of 57 

numbers, even ones we have never seen before, and represent them in multiple ways (Amalric & 58 

Dehaene, 2016; Dehaene et al., 2025). We use diverse strategies even for simple arithmetic, shifting 59 

among estimation, memory retrieval, decomposition, and rule-based procedures depending on the context 60 

(Banerjee et al., 2025; Campbell & Xue, 2001; Delazer et al., 2005; LeFevre et al., 2013; Siegler, 1988; 61 

Siegler & Braithwaite, 2017). We transition seamlessly between different numerical modalities (Piazza et 62 

al., 2007; Eger et al., 2003; Jacob & Nieder, 2009), and instantly grasp abstract, non-linear relationships 63 

between disparate quantities (Foucault et al., 2025). Even at a young age, we can skip-count by twos or 64 

threes, distinguish even from odd, or hold several nonconsecutive numbers (Gathercole et al., 2004) in 65 

working memory (e.g. PIN number, ZIP code). 66 

Our mathematical flexibility implies the existence of a representational geometry capable of 67 

supporting the dynamic and combinatorial demands of symbolic human thought (Shepard et al., 1975; 68 

Dehaene et al., 2025). Indeed, classic multidimensional scaling behavioral studies suggest that the 69 

intrinsic representation of numbers may be high-dimensional (Shepard et al., 1975; Miller and Gelman, 70 

1983; Griffiths and Kalish, 2002). These studies showed that people represent similarities among single-71 

digit numerals not only by absolute magnitude difference but by multiple properties, including parity and 72 

arithmetic relatedness. At the neural level, this suggests a high-dimensional representational geometry. 73 

The computational capacity of a neural population is strongly constrained by the geometry of its 74 

population-code. In particular, it depends on how many dichotomies of task conditions can be separated 75 

by a linear readout (shattering dimensionality), and on how flexibly those conditions can be mapped into 76 

output variables by downstream readouts (expressivity). High dimensional representations have high 77 

shattering dimensionality, making them ideal for on-the-fly flexible dichotomization (such as even/odd or 78 

prime/composite, Bernardi et al., 2020; Cover, 1965; Rigotti et al., 2013). They also have high 79 

expressibility, meaning they can support flexible reorderings (such as even-first, then-odd; Cover, 1967; 80 

Rigotti et al., 2013). In high dimensions, distinct population activity patterns can be arranged as the 81 

vertices of a high-dimensional generalization of a triangle or tetrahedron; such a shape is known as a 82 

simplex (Boyd & Vandenberghe, 2004). Because these vertices are affinely independent, simplicial 83 

geometry maximizes linear shattering of the finite pattern set and permits maximal linear-readout 84 

expressivity under the affine-readout assumptions (Cover, 1965). These facts motivated us to hypothesize 85 

that the inherent neural geometry of numerosity would involve a simplicial manifold structure. 86 

Both human neuroimaging and non-human primate single-unit recordings have identified neural 87 

activity selective for specific numerosities (Nieder et al., 2002; Nieder et al., 2006; Harvey et al., 2013; 88 

Piazza et al., 2004). These classical “number neurons/voxels” typically exhibit Gaussian-like tuning 89 

curves centered on a preferred quantity, providing a direct biological substrate for the approximate 90 

number system and the behavioral distance effect. Neurophysiological investigations into numerical 91 

cognition have historically centered on the intraparietal sulcus and prefrontal cortex (Nieder & Dehaene, 92 

2009; Debray et al., 2026). More recently, human single-neuron recordings have revealed robust number 93 

coding in the human medial temporal lobe (MTL), which includes the hippocampus and adjacent 94 

structures (Kutter et al., 2018, 2022, 2023, and 2024). This coding is consistent with emerging evidence 95 

that the MTL represents structured numerical information (Valério et al., 2026). While these results 96 

linking neural activity to number processing have generally been interpreted in the number line 97 

framework, they are also consistent with alternative structures because of the expressibility of high-98 

dimensional manifolds. 99

The possibility of a high dimensional number manifold raises the question of how the brain 100 

implements arithmetic calculations. Pioneering studies using fMRI (Qin et al., 2014) and local field 101 

potentials (Pinheiro-Chagas et al., 2024) tell us where and when calculations occur, but not how . One 102 

important study identified abstract codes for arithmetic operation in the MTL (plus vs. minus, Kutter et 103 

al., 2022). Most recently, Czajko et al. (2024) investigated internally generated results during tasks 104 

involving the doubling or halving of dots on a screen with fMRI. However, this approach was limited to 105 

approximate, non-symbolic quantities and restricted the second operand to only two choices (two or four). 106 

Consequently, how the brain transforms two exact, symbolic operands into an exact result is unknown. 107 

This problem is, ostensibly, more difficult with high-dimensional representations than with number lines. 108 

A similar challenge arises in large language models (LLMs), which operate over high-dimensional hidden 109 

representations (Zhao et al., 2026; Cheng et al., 2025) yet can successfully perform arithmetic 110 

computations (Stolfo et al., 2023). We investigated the hidden states of LLMs, finding that their 111 

numerical representations are organized as simplicial geometries. Inspired by this, we show that the 112 

human MTL also supports arithmetic processing through simplex-like symbolic representations, which 113 

flexibly integrate operand information into decodable internal results via operation-dependent linear 114 

transformations. 115

RESULTS 119 

Eleven patients undergoing intracranial monitoring for epilepsy (1 male, 10 females; age 41 ± 120 

3.14 years old) participated in an arithmetic task. A subset (n = 10 patients) also performed a dot-121 

counting task ( Figure 1A–C ; Methods ). In the arithmetic task , participants viewed two operands 122 

(Figures 1-3 ) and an addition or subtraction operator, mentally computed the answer, and answered the 123 

question with a key press ( Methods ), followed by computer feedback ( Figure 1B ). In the dot-counting 124 

task , participants viewed an array of dots whose spatial layout varied across trials (Kutter et al., 2018), 125 

then reported the number of dots after a short delay ( Figure 1C ) with a key press, and then received 126 

feedback. Across both tasks, we recorded 554 isolated neurons from medial temporal lobe structures, 127 

including hippocampus (n = 389 neurons from 11 patients), entorhinal cortex (n = 70 neurons from 5 128 

patients), amygdala (n = 77 neurons from 4 patients), and parahippocampal cortex (n = 18 neurons from 1 129 

patient). 130 

Neural tuning for task variables is often quantified using a standard fixed-window firing-rate 131 

code. However, a good deal of previous research indicates that much of the informational capacity of 132 

neuronal firing could reside in the spiking temporal dynamics beyond their averaged firing rates (Cariani 133 

& Baker, 2025; Li et al., 2026; Panzeri et al., 2026; She et al., 2024; Smith et al., 2019; Xie et al., 2024; 134 

Zuo et al., 2015; Chabrol et al., 2015). Indeed, in an example neuron during the arithmetic task, firing-rate 135 

tuning curves showed overlapping responses across different numbers ( Figure 1D ). Yet, in the same 136 

example neuron, spike rasters and peristimulus time histograms revealed that numbers with overlapping 137 

mean firing rates nevertheless produced different response dynamics over time ( Figure 1E,F ), consistent 138 

with temporal pattern separation (Madar et al., 2019a, 2019b; Nigam et al., 2025). We therefore binned 139 

each trial’s spike train across time and used cross-validated linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to extract 140 

stimulus-coding temporal components, defined as linear combinations of time bins (Zhu et al., 2025, 141 

Figure 1G ). Projecting trials from the example neuron onto the first two temporal components revealed 142 

additional details of the number code ( Figure 1H ). Note that we specifically confirmed that our temporal 143 

component extraction procedure does not artifactually generate high dimensional representational 144 

geometry ( Supplementary Figure 1 ). 145 

This pattern generalized across neurons and tasks. In six example neurons during the arithmetic 146 

task, firing-rate tuning curves often showed overlapping responses across different numbers ( Figure 1I ), 147 

whereas the corresponding temporal-component projections separated number conditions more clearly at 148 

the single-neuron level ( Figure 1J ). We observed a similar pattern in the dot-counting task: example 149 

neurons showed partially overlapping firing-rate tuning, but temporally resolved projections separated 150 

dot-number responses along one or more temporal components ( Figure 1K, L ). 151 

To quantify the increase in sensitivity of incorporating temporal coding, we compared cross-152 

validated nine-way number decoding using both firing rate and temporal-component representation. The 153 

proportion of number-coding neurons in the arithmetic task increased from 3.36 ± 0.71% to 24.80 ± 154 

1.73% under the temporal code (paired t-test across n = 11 subjects, p < 0.0001; significant neurons 155 

defined from permutation test with label shuffling, Figure 1M ). Across all neurons, temporal components 156 

significantly improved single-neuron decoding accuracy relative to firing rate by 3.79% ( Figure 1N ). 157 

Temporal dynamics also produced a more sensitive measurement of neural coding in the dot-counting 158

We found no significant regional differences in the proportion of neurons coding numbers in each 166 

MTL region. Specifically, using the temporal-component tuning definition, we fit a binomial generalized 167 

linear mixed-effects model to explain the proportion of coding neurons with brain region as a fixed effect 168 

and subject as a random effect, allowing us to test regional differences while accounting for repeated 169 

neurons sampled from the same patient. The post-hoc ANOVA region effect was non-significant in both 170 

the arithmetic task (region main effect: F(3,550) = 0.43, p = 0.74; Figure 1Q ) and the dot-counting task 171 

(region main effect: F(3,507) = 1.1034, p = 0.35; Figure 1R ). These results are consistent with others 172 

showing that number-coding neurons are widely distributed across the recorded medial temporal lobe 173 

regions (Kutter et al., 2018; 2024). 174 

Finally, we tested whether the observed coding neurons were dominated by any particular 175 

especially well-represented numbers (cf. Kutter et al., 2024). For each coding neuron, we defined the 176 

most preferred number as the number with the highest decoding accuracy, then compared the distribution 177 

of preferred numbers across subjects. In the arithmetic task, we observed no evidence for favored 178 

numbers across 1–9 after accounting for subject-level variability (Friedman test, p = 0.0692; Figure 1S ). 179 

Thus, while there is a marginal trend suggesting a preference for numbers at the high and low end of the 180 

range tested (numbers 1 and 9) across neurons (Kutter et al., 2024), it is not significant in our current data. 181 

The dot-counting task showed the same pattern, with no significant overrepresentation of any individual 182 

preferred number (Friedman test, p = 0.139; Figure 1T ). 183

A simplicial number manifold in MTL 236 

We next investigated the shape of the neural population manifold of numbers in MTL. A number 237 

line, even when log-spaced, imposes a privileged ordering and supports a limited set of dichotomies and 238 

orderings. In contrast, a simplex-like (“ simplicial ”) geometry supports many dichotomies and orderings 239 

(Figure 2A,B ). In an example subject, z-scored temporal components pooled across coding neurons 240 

showed structured number tuning, with components preferring a given dot number often also responding 241 

to other dot numbers, not necessarily only to the immediate neighbouring numbers ( Figure 2C ). 242 

However, the centroids of numbers 1-9 did not fall along a line; non-adjacent numbers did occupy nearby 243 

positions, suggesting a simplicial population geometry ( Figure 2D ). 244 

To quantify this population geometry, we first measured transition angles between each pair of 245 

sequential numbers (Hénaff et al., 2019; 2021). A number line predicts transition angles near 0°, whereas 246 

a noiseless regular simplex predicts transition angles of 120° (e.g., the 120° turn required to navigate from 247 

vertices 1 to 2, and then 2 to 3, along the edges of an equilateral triangle, which is a regular 2-simplex; 248 

this also holds for regular simplices in higher dimensions due to the equidistance property). Because trial 249 

noise can inflate transition angles even for a line-like code, we constructed split-half distributions and 250 

compared them with two matched controls: (1) a log-number-line control in which number centers were 251 

shifted onto a fitted log line while preserving trial-to-trial noise, and (2) a matched simplex control in 252 

which centers were placed on an 8-dimensional simplex with distance from the center to vertices scaled to 253 

match the empirical distances. In the same example subject, the empirical transition-angle distribution 254 

was far from the number-line control and largely overlapped the matched simplex control ( Figure 2E ). 255 

Across subjects, the empirical mean transition angle was significantly greater than the 95th percentile of 256 

the number-line null distribution, indicating that the geometry was substantially more simplicial than 257 

expected from a noisy number line ( Figure 2F ). In contrast, the empirical transition angles were not 258 

significantly different from the lower bound of the matched simplex distribution, consistent with a 259 

simplex-like geometry rather than a noise-exaggerated one-dimensional line ( Figure 2F ). 260 

We next asked whether neural distances recapitulate numerical distances, as expected under a 261 

number-line representation. We fit a mixed-effects model predicting neural distance from numerical 262 

distance, with subject and number identities included as random effects to account for dependence among 263 

entries in the representational distance matrix. Numerical distance did not significantly predict neural 264 

distance in the dot-counting task across patients (slope β = 0.014735; p[slope = 0] = 0.68; Figure 2G ). 265 

Thus, although the population clearly distinguished number identities, neural distances did not reliably 266 

scale with numerical distance, providing no evidence for a smooth number-line-like metric. 267

High Shattering Dimensionality 268 

One benefit of a simplicial code is high shattering dimensionality, which refers to the number of 269 

classification dichotomies available (Bernardi et al., 2020; Posani et al., 2025). We quantified simplex 270 

shattering dimensionality (SSD) by decoding dichotomies across all possible number splits (rather than 271 

restricting the analysis to balanced half-splits). The full population produced high shattering performance 272 

(decoding accuracy or SSD = 0.7302 ± 0.0203; n = 10 subjects. Figure 2H ). Critically, all subjects’ SSD 273 

values were above chance, thus no single patient’s representation is purely one-dimensional (p = 0.0062, 274 

permutation test with 160 shuffles). Importantly, this dimensionality was not explained simply by residual 275 

magnitude or number-line information: shattering performance remained significant even after regressing 276 

out magnitude-related structure (p = 0.0062, Figure 2H ), despite being expectedly smaller than the full 277 

population (p = 0.000026 two-sided paired t-test). To estimate the minimum dimensionality required to 278 

support this performance, we projected the number manifolds into lower-dimensional PCA subspaces and 279 

recomputed the shattering score. One-, two-, and three-dimensional projections all produced significantly 280 

lower shattering performance than the full representation or high-dimensional reference (1D: p = 2.34e-5, 281 

q = 9.36e-5; 2D: p = 7.6e-4, q = 0.00152; 3D: p = 0.0134, q = 0.0178, q denotes the Benjamini-Hochberg-282 

corrected p-value), whereas the four-dimensional projection was no longer significantly different from the 283 

original, unreduced high dimension neural code (4D: p = 0.252, q = 0.252; Figure 2H ). Thus, while the 284 

true dimensionality is likely higher, four dimensions serve as a conservative lower bound necessary to 285 

account for the observed shattering dimensionality given our data. 286

Low CCGP 288 

We next asked whether the number manifold has specific abstract axes that are strong enough that 289 

they are recoverable across novel contexts (like a “baked-in” representation of even-odd), or whether 290 

these categories are flexibly created on the fly. We therefore selected 11 meaningful dichotomies of the 291 

nine numbers: (1) magnitude, (2) visual, (3) keypad, and verbal groupings, and measured cross-condition 292 

generalization performance (CCGP) across subjects ( Figure 2I ). For each dichotomy, we trained a linear 293 

classifier to separate the categories using a subset of the numbers, and tested its accuracy on the 294 

remaining, held-out numbers (e.g., training a "small vs. large" axis on {1, 3, 4} vs. {5, 6, 7, 9} and testing 295 

generalization on {2} vs. {8}). Consistent with a high-dimensional representation, CCGP was generally 296 

weak (Bernardi et al., 2020): except for the “small/large” split, which generalized in 8 of 10 subjects, 297 

most other dichotomies showed significant generalization in only a few (0–3) subjects. In other words, 298 

aside from the high-low magnitude split, these other dichotomies do not form highly abstract, reusable 299 

axes within the manifold that could be linearly read out across novel contexts. 300 

Mean CCGP across splits was 0.5096 ± 0.0076, with per-split values ranging from 0.3037 to 301 

0.6976, where a CCGP of 0.5 is chance level. Collectively, the pairing of high shattering dimensionality 302 

with low CCGP reflects a high-dimensional neural code optimized for memorization over rule-based 303 

abstraction (Bernardi et al., 2020). While the high shattering dimensionality reflects the capacity to 304 

separate highly familiar digits into many arbitrary groupings, the weak CCGP indicates that the manifold 305 

lacks the abstract axes required to rapidly categorize novel unseen numbers (e.g. a newly discovered 306 

(fictional) integer quantity between four and five) into these learned groupings. 307

No privileged representation of ordered number line 309 

Whereas the CCGP asks whether binary categorical axes generalize across held-out numbers, we 310 

next used a complementary variance-decomposition analysis to ask how much of the reliable number-311 

condition structure is carried by a continuous ordered number-line axis. To establish the amount of 312 

reliable condition-dependent structure available to be explained, we replaced each trial’s population 313 

response with the mean population response for its corresponding number condition. This condition-mean 314 

representation removes trial-by-trial variability while preserving stable differences among number 315 

conditions. Across subjects, this reliable condition-mean structure accounted for approximately 19% of 316 

the total trial-level variance. We then fitted a 1-9 number-line projection using the individual single-trial 317 

responses through linear ridge regression and evaluated how much of the condition-mean structure was 318 

captured by projecting the condition means onto this axis. If the number conditions were primarily 319 

arranged along an ordered one-dimensional continuum, this projection should capture most of the 320 

condition-mean variance, leaving little residual condition-mean structure. Instead, the number-line 321 

component accounted for only a small fraction of the reliable signal. The best-fitting linear number-line 322 

projection explained 0.5330 ± 0.1124% of total trial variance, whereas the residual condition-mean 323 

structure left outside the number-line component explained 18.4155 ± 4.1041% (p = 0.0018, two-sided 324 

paired t-test; Figure 2J) . A similar result was obtained removing a logarithmic version of the number-line 325 

(log number line: 0.5267 ± 0.1078% variance explained; residual condition mean: 18.4218 ± 4.0881%; p 326 

= 0.0017; Figure 2K ). Separately, at the single-trial level, we trained cross-validated regression models to 327 

project neural activity onto each of the 9! possible ordered lines and ranked the canonical 1–9 ordering by 328 

held-out decoding correlation. The canonical ordering did not rank significantly above chance (1-9 line vs 329 

50%, linear: p = 0.0527; log: p = 0.0967, one-sided Wilcoxon signed rank test) and remained significantly 330 

below the best possible ordering (1-9 line vs 100%: p = 9.77e-04; log vs 100%: p = 9.77e-04; Figure 2L ). 331 

No difference was found between the ranking of linear vs log number line (p = 0.19). Thus, although a 332 

number-line projection can always be extracted from a high-dimensional code, it is not a privileged axis 333 

of the neural representation. 334

A simplicial manifold for Arabic numerals 336 

We repeated the same analyses for Arabic numerals in the arithmetic task (example: Figure 2M-337 

P) . As in dot counting, we found that numerical distance did not predict neural distance across the 338 

representational distance matrix ( Figure 2Q ), and concomitant high shattering dimensionality ( Figure 339 

2R ) and a minimal dimensionality of three ( Figure 2R ). We speculate that the lowered bound on 340 

estimated dimensionality for Arabic numerals may reflect the reduced stimulus variability (geometry of 341 

the dot locations) of symbolic numerals compared with spatially variable dot arrays. CCGP across the 342 

same 11 dichotomies was limited ( Figure 2S ). The linear and log number-line projections remained weak 343 

relative to the full condition-mean structure ( Figure 2T-U ). The canonical 1–9 order was not uniquely 344 

privileged at the cross-validated single-trial decoding level. The canonical ordering ( Figure 2V ) did not 345 

rank significantly above chance (linear: p = 0.77; log: p = 0.91, one-sided Wilcoxon signed rank test) and 346 

remained significantly below the best possible ordering (linear vs 100%: p =4.88e-04; log vs 100%: p 347 

=4.88e-04). No difference was found between the ranking of linear and log number line (two-sided 348

Arabic and dot numbers have different but related codes 396 

We next asked whether MTL number representations are shared across nonsymbolic and 397 

symbolic formats. We took advantage of the Arabic-numeral feedback period in the dot-counting task. 398 

This allowed us to compare neural codes for nonsymbolic dot numerosities and symbolic Arabic numerals 399 

within the same counting-task context. 400 

At the single-neuron level, dot-stimulus and numeral-feedback showed limited preference 401 

consistency. The number of neurons with correlated cross-format number decoding accuracy was not 402 

more frequent than expected by chance (p = 0.77, two-sided binomial test at ɑ = 5% , Figure 3A ): only 403 

5.2% of neurons that significantly coded number in both epochs showed similar numerical preferences. 404 

Consistent with this, the correlation between most preferred dot number and most preferred feedback-405 

numeral number was not significant across neurons (r = 0.09, p = 0.501; Figure 3B ). Thus, individual 406 

MTL neurons generally did not express a strictly format-invariant number preference, even when dots and 407 

Arabic numerals appeared within the same task. 408 

However, unified cognitive variables need not be represented by identical tuning at the level of 409 

single neurons. They can instead be embedded within the shared low-dimensional geometry of a broader 410 

population response (Gallego et al., 2018; Fine et al., 2023; Safaie et al., 2023). We therefore asked 411 

whether dot stimuli and feedback numerals occupied similar number-related population subspaces. For 412 

each subject and format, we computed the principal-component subspace spanned by number-related 413 

population activity. We then quantified subspace overlap by asking how much variance from one format 414 

was captured by the principal components of the other format (Elsayed et al., 2016; Hu et al., 2026; Yan 415 

et al., 2026a). Within-format overlap was estimated using repeated split-half resampling, providing a 416 

reliability-matched upper bound for the overlap expected given finite trial counts. 417 

This analysis initially suggested strong format dependence at the population level. Cross-format 418 

overlap between dot stimuli and dot-feedback numerals was substantially lower than within-format 419 

overlap (p < 0.001, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Figure 3C ). Thus, dot and feedback-numeral 420 

representations did not simply occupy the same neural dimensions. 421 

Low subspace overlap, however, does not rule out the possibility that the two formats use 422 

different axes that are nevertheless linearly transformable through a shared latent code (Gallego et al., 423 

2020). We therefore applied singular vector canonical correlation analysis (SVCCA) to test whether dot 424 

and feedback-numeral representations could be aligned after allowing linear rotations and rescaling of 425 

their activity patterns (Raghu et al., 2017). In this analysis, a high SVCCA score would indicate that two 426 

formats express similar latent numerical structure, even if that structure is read out through different 427 

population axes. 428 

Unlike the low subspace overlap, SVCCA revealed significant alignment between dot-stimulus 429 

and feedback-numeral representations. The true dot–feedback alignment exceeded the 95th percentile of a 430 

label-shuffled control distribution (true median alignment: 0.5155 ± 0.0313; shuffle 95%: 0.5070 ± 431 

0.0261; median ± s.e.m.; p = 9.77e-4, one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test; Figure 3D ). Thus, within a 432

Implementation of arithmetic with simplicial numerical codes 520 

How do simplicial representations of numbers afford arithmetic computations? We hypothesized 521 

simple arithmetic may function the way it does in large language models (LLMs): routing operand- and 522 

operator-related information via attention to the answer position, where a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) 523 

transforms this integrated information into result-related representations (Stolfo et al., 2023). 524 

Under this general framework, we sought to determine the specific information integration 525 

strategy used to derive the result by comparing different model variants ( Figure 4A-F ). We define the 526 

neural result representation Y res as the serialized population firing rate across multiple time bins during 527 

the thinking period ( Figure 4A ). Furthermore, we hypothesize that Y res is linearly transformed from the 528 

two operand representations X op1 and X op2 , which we define as the pooled temporal components from 529 

tuned neurons. Importantly, the transformation should happen in a context-specific (plus vs. minus) 530 

manner ( Figure 4B-C ). Indeed, it can be seen that in both the flexible ( Figure 4B ) and commutative 531 

(Figure 4C ) model, X op2 are weighted differently depending on the sign of the operation. To test the 532 

possibility that the brain treats “3+4” equally as “4+3” by the commutative law, we shared weights 533 

between X op1 and X op2 during addition in the commutative model ( Figure 4C ). Two baseline models were 534 

developed where subjects simply memorized information from one of the operands, but incorrectly 535 

ignored information from the other operand, i.e., operand2 ( Figure 4D ) or operand1 ( Figure 4E ) to 536 

derive results. Finally, another control model simulated the case where the brain overlooks the mandate of 537 

specific arithmetic rules on how operand1 and operand2 should be integrated to derive result 538 

representations ( Figure 4F ). 539

upcoming internal computed results, we fit decoder models of different complexities to read out 541

DISCUSSION 619 

We find that coding for numbers 1-9 has a simplicial manifold structure in the human MTL. 620 

Whereas much of human neuroscience has focused on linear magnitude as the organizing principle for 621 

numbers, the neural geometry we observe is better aligned with the idea that mentally, numbers are 622 

organized along multiple dimensions (Shepard et al., 1975). Relative to a low-dimensional manifold, like 623 

a number line, a simplex offers several advantages. First, it has high shattering dimensionality , which 624 

permits zero-shot discrimination for any numerical subset (e.g., even/odd, prime/composite). Second, it 625 

has high expressivity , which permits zero-shot reordering of the digits. This ordering includes the 626 

standard 1-9 ordering; consequently, a simplicial manifold is a more general and more flexible 627 

representational scheme than a number line. Third, in the case of a regular simplex, its equidistance 628 

makes it more robust to noise and interference. A rich and influential body of literature has firmly 629 

established the existence of a mental number line (Dehaene et al., 1999; Dehaene et al., 2008; Knops et 630 

al., 2009; Rugani et al., 2015), including at the level of single neurons in the MTL (Kutter et al., 2018, 631 

2024). Our result extends this perspective to high dimensionality. 632 

In both artificial and biological networks, learning reshapes representational geometry by 633 

expanding the global dimensionality of the latent space to decorrelate heavily trained concepts (Flesch et 634 

al., 2022; Sun W. et al., 2025; Nguyen et al., 2024; Failor et al., 2025). From early childhood, individuals 635 

are exposed to both counting and Arabic numbers and a broad range of numerical manipulations 636 

(especially 1–9, Barner, 2017; Slusser, Santiago, & Barth, 2013; Carey, 2009; Gallistel & Gelman, 1992; 637 

Spelke, 2017). This intensive, lifelong overlearning of small numbers is therefore expected to drive the 638 

underlying neural manifolds to expand into a maximal-dimensional configuration (Nakai, et al., 2023; 639 

Chung et al., 2018). This idea is also consistent with our finding that LLM representations of numbers 640 

also have an inherently high dimensional structure. By directly quantifying this dimensionality, our 641 

results further extend prior LLM work that describes the geometry of numbers only in dimension-reduced 642 

spaces (Kantamneni & Tegmark, 2025; Sun Y. et al., 2025; Karkada et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026). 643 

Whether symbolic and nonsymbolic numbers share a common representation remains debated. 644 

Evidence for partially distinct systems comes from developmental and comparative work showing 645 

approximate numerosity representations appear before, or outside, formal symbolic math training (Starr et 646 

al., 2013; Nieder et al., 2002; Nieder et al., 2006; Rugani et al., 2015), whereas exact symbolic 647 

representations suitable for arithmetic appear later in life and are highly influenced by cultural, 648 

educational factors (Ferrigno et al., 2017; Pica et al., 2004; Dehaene et al., 2008; Dillon et al., 2017; 649 

Banerjee et al., 2025). However, behavioral links between approximate quantity and formal mathematics 650 

suggest that symbolic and nonsymbolic systems could have shared representation or are at least partially 651 

coupled (Gilmore et al., 2007; Halberda et al., 2008; Ferrigno et al., 2017). The neural basis of this 652 

relationship remains equally debated. Some work supports shared magnitude representations across 653 

nonsymbolic and symbolic formats, especially in intraparietal cortex, whereas other studies reveal 654 

notation-, hemisphere-, region-, and task-dependent numerical codes (Ansari, 2016; Aulet, Kaicher, & 655 

Cantlon, 2025; Cantlon et al., 2009; Kersey, Aulet, & Cantlon, 2026; Piazza et al., 2007; Cohen Kadosh 656 

et al., 2007; Bulthé et al., 2014; Sokolowski et al., 2017; Wilkey et al., 2020; Diester & Nieder, 2007; 657 

Seidler et al., 2026; Emerson & Cantlon, 2015). In human MTL, prior work established that nonsymbolic 658 

and symbolic numbers are represented differently by single neurons (Kutter et al., 2018). We similarly 659 

found that number codes were format dependent. Critically, however, we provide a new perspective to 660 

this question by showing that despite the apparent use of different codes, the nonsymbolic and symbolic 661 

number representations share a partially aligned latent representational subspace identifiable by SVCCA. 662 

> (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder. All rights reserved. No reuse allowed without permission. The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted June 28, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.25.734462 doi: bioRxiv preprint

This provides a potential computational mechanism that allows us to flexibly transfer between the 663 

nonsymbolic and symbolic numbers, e.g., buying 5 apples (non-symbolic quantities) with a bill that reads 664 

$5 (symbolic). Here we tested linear alignment; future work with larger within-task trial budgets and 665 

better matched tasks between formats could test whether nonlinear transformations support shared neural 666 

representation between formats (Andrew et al., 2013; Radford et al., 2021). 667 

How the brain transforms symbolic operands into arithmetic results remains unresolved (Nieder, 668 

2025; Qin et al., 2014; Pinheiro-Chagas et al., 2024), although one study did identify abstract operation 669 

rule-selective neurons in MTL (Kutter et al., 2022). One possible reason such calculation result 670 

information may have been missed is methodological choice: many prior analyses pooled 671 

neurons/channels across patients into pseudo-populations. This approach is well suited for detecting 672 

general coding principles, but it disrupts the link between an individual subject’s neural state and that 673 

subject’s own reported answer. This distinction is important for arithmetic, where subjects differ in 674 

strategy and math capacity. Indeed, we found that subjects with weaker result decodability also showed 675 

lower behavioral accuracy and larger errors. Motivated by recent work on arithmetic in large language 676 

models, we separated the computation into two stages. In transformer models, attention can route 677 

operand- and operator-related information to the answer position, while MLP modules transform that 678 

merged information into result-related representations (Stolfo et al., 2023). By analogy, we asked whether 679 

MTL activity during the thinking period reflects an operation-dependent transformation of operand 680 

representations, and whether this activity can then be decoded into the answer that subjects eventually 681 

report. 682 

Our findings support this computational decomposition. Analogous to an attention mechanism 683 

integrating contextual information, MTL activity cannot be explained by the mere working memory traces 684 

of isolated operands; it requires their operation-specific integration. Similar to an MLP module 685 

transforming these inputs into an output, MTL population activity can nonlinearly decode arithmetic 686 

results (which has never been shown to our knowledge), with decoding strength directly predicting the 687 

subject’s behavioral accuracy. These findings extend recent fMRI evidence showing neural encoding of 688 

internally produced quantities (Czajko et al., 2024). Crucially, our work advances this principle from 689 

approximate, single-operand transformations of nonsymbolic quantities to exact, multi-operand symbolic 690 

arithmetic. 691 

What neuron-level tuning functions produce simplicial manifolds? One possibility is a labeled-692 

line (i.e., one-hot) code, in which each numerosity is represented by neurons selective only for that 693 

number. However, another possibility is that individual neurons do not systematically encode specific 694 

numbers; indeed, they could have complex multi-peaked tuning curves (Foucault et al., 2025; Mainali et 695 

al., 2025). Such tuning curves are typically diagnostic of complex, warped, or folded manifold structures, 696 

and are often associated with function superposition, in which multiple dissimilar, non-adjacent concepts 697 

are overloaded onto single neurons (Elhage et al., 2022; Maimon et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026b). 698 

Inspection of our single-neuron tuning curves argues strongly against the preferred-number regime and in 699 

favor of the alternative, suggesting a mechanism by which the brain maximizes numerical capacity with a 700 

finite number of neurons. 701

manifold structure we identify here will extend to these regions. Second, it remains unclear whether the 705 

principles we identify for 1-9 will apply to larger numbers. We deliberately restricted our stimulus set to 706

Human intracranial neurophysiology 1143 

Intracranial electrophysiological recordings were obtained from adult patients undergoing clinical 1144 

intracranial monitoring for medically refractory epilepsy at Baylor St. Luke Hospital, following 1145 

established intracranial recording procedures during epilepsy monitoring (Xiao et al., 2024; Franch et al., 1146 

2026a). Electrode placement was determined exclusively by clinical criteria. All participants provided 1147 

informed consent under protocols approved by the Baylor College of Medicine institutional review board. 1148 

Single-neuron activity was recorded from stereotactic depth electrodes equipped for unit recordings, 1149 

including AdTech Medical Behnke–Fried–style probes with 8 channels per probe. Electrode locations 1150 

were verified by co-registering each participant’s pre-operative anatomical MRI with the post-operative 1151 

CT scan (Franch et al., 2026a). 1152 

Each depth probe contained eight microelectrode channels optimized for unit recording. Neural signals 1153 

were acquired using a 512-channel Blackrock Microsystems NeuroPort system at 30 kHz. Spikes were 1154 

detected and sorted offline with Wave_clus (Chaure et al., 2018), followed by manual curation. Putative 1155 

units were retained when their waveforms were stable over the recording session and showed consistent 1156 

morphology, including amplitude and trough-to-peak features, together with inter-spike-interval 1157 

distributions consistent with a refractory period. Units with inter-spike intervals violation of more than 1158 

three percent were defined as multi-unit. Unless otherwise stated, analyses included both single-unit and 1159 

multi-unit activity (Franch et al., 2026a). 1160 

Electrode localization 1161 

Electrode localization was performed using a standardized MRI/CT co-registration workflow (Franch et 1162 

al., 2026a). For each patient, pre-operative T1-weighted MRI and post-operative CT images were 1163 

converted to NIfTI format (Li et al., 2016). The post-operative CT was aligned to the pre-operative MRI 1164 

using FSL (Jenkinson & Smith, 2001; Jenkinson et al., 2002). Electrode contacts were then manually 1165 

identified in BioImage Suite (Joshi et al., 2011) and transformed into each participant’s native anatomical 1166 

space using iELVis MATLAB utilities (Groppe et al., 2017; Yang et al., 2012). 1167 

Cortical surfaces were reconstructed using FreeSurfer (Dale et al., 1999), and electrode contacts were 1168 

visualized on each participant’s native anatomy using iELVis (Groppe et al., 2017). Microelectrode 1169 

positions were defined relative to the deepest macro-contact of the Behnke–Fried depth electrode 1170 

assembly. For group-level visualization, participant anatomies and electrode coordinates were warped 1171 

into MNI152 space using RAVE (Magnotti et al., 2020). 1172

cm away. Stimuli were presented on a black background, and participants responded using a standard 1177 

numeric keypad with numbers arranged in four rows by three columns. The keypad contained additional 1178 

response keys (e.g., enter/delete). 1179

Single-neuron number decoding was performed separately for the arithmetic and dot-counting tasks. For 1214 

dot counting task, spike trains were aligned to stimulus onset (i.e. dot array), and all decoding analyses 1215 

used spikes occurring from 0.1 to 1.9 s after the alignment event. This yielded a 1.8-s analysis window. 1216 

Trials were assigned to number conditions using the ground truth number of dots for each trial. 1217 

For each subject, number labels were sorted in ascending order, and the first nine number conditions with 1218 

at least two trials were selected (for all other than 1 subject, this results in 1-9, where the remaining 1219 

subject included 10 and 11 but excluded 2 and 5). Trials whose labels fell outside this selected set were 1220 

excluded from analysis. The analyses reported here were therefore nine-way number decoding analyses, 1221 

with a chance level of 1/9. 1222 

Firing-rate representation 1223 

For the firing-rate code, each trial from a given neuron was represented by a single value: the total spike 1224 

count in the 0.1–1.9 s analysis window. For plotting firing-rate tuning curves, this spike count was 1225 

converted to firing rate by dividing by the 1.8-s window duration. This representation therefore discarded 1226 

within-window temporal structure and retained only the overall response magnitude for each trial. 1227 

Temporal-window representation 1228 

For the temporal code, the same 0.1–1.9 s analysis window was divided into non-overlapping time bins, 1229 

and spikes were summed within each bin. Each trial was therefore represented as a vector of spike counts 1230 

across time for one neuron. 1231

Response-adjusted operand labels for Figure 4 1381 

For Figure 4, we constructed a response-adjusted operand representation for error trials intended to 1382 

approximate the numerical structure used by the participant on each trial. This analysis was motivated by 1383 

the fact that some arithmetic errors appeared consistently within and across subjects in a predictable way, 1384 

such as using the wrong operation sign, potentially due to the short presentation period and randomized 1385 

location of the stimuli, leaving subject without enough time to locate and recognize the task stimuli. Three 1386 

response-adjustment rules were used. 1387 

First, if the response was consistent with using the opposite operation sign, the operation sign for that trial 1388 

was switched. Operand order was not changed under this rule. For example, if the displayed operands 1389 

were 5 and 3 on a subtraction trial, the task-defined answer was 5 − 3 = 2. If the participant responded 8, 1390 

this response was consistent with computing 5 + 3 rather than 5 − 3, so the trial was treated as an 1391 

operation-sign error and the operation label was switched from subtraction to addition. Similarly, a 1392 

response of 6, 7, 8, 9, or 10 would fall within the ±2 response-unit tolerance around the alternative answer 1393 

8. 1394 

Second, when the minus sign appeared before the operands and the response was consistent with 1395 

attaching the minus sign to the first operand, the first operand was relabeled as a signed negative value. In 1396 

this case, the expression was represented as −operand1 + operand2. For example, if the display sequence 1397 

was “− 5 3,” the task-defined answer was 5 − 3 = 2. If the participant responded −2, this response was 1398 

consistent with treating the expression as −5 + 3. For the response-adjusted representation, the first 1399 

operand was therefore relabeled from 5 to −5, the second operand remained 3, and the operation was 1400 

represented as addition. 1401
