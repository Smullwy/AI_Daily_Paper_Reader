Abstract 24 

Speech production requires the coordinated control of the supralaryngeal articulators (e.g., the 25 

lips, tongue , and soft palate ) together with laryngeal control of phonation . While cortical 26 

sensorimotor regions are known to encode phonetic features , how the cerebellum represents 27 

speech and how cerebellar representations integrate within cortico -cerebellar circuits remain 28 

poorly understood . Using 7T functional MRI and representational similarity analysis, we 29 

examined neural representations of speech production during overt syllable production varying 30 

in place of articulation and voice onset time. We found reliable, syllable -specific activity patterns 31 

across both cortical and cerebellar speech regions. Ventral primary sensorimotor cortex 32 

distinguished syllables by place of articulation, whereas dorsal sensorimotor cortex showed 33 

sensitiv ity to voice onset time, consistent with a functional dissociation between articulatory and 34 

phonatory control . We also found secondary speech areas that encoded phonetic features .35 

Specifically, the parietal operculum showed sensitivity to both place of articulation and voice 36 

onset time, positioning it as an integrative node within the speech motor network. In the 37 

cerebellum we found superior and inferior speech area s, which did not differ in their 38 

representational geometry . Surprisingly , the cerebellar syllable representations were most similar 39 

to those found in the parietal opercul um rather than in primary motor cortex , with both regions 40 

encoding both phonetic features . These findings indicate that cerebellar speech representations 41 

are not solely determined by a single primary sensorimotor area but instead align more closely 42 

with the representations in the parietal operculum , suggesting that cerebellar speech areas 43 

contribute to the coordination of phonation and articulation. 44

1.  Introduction 52 

Speech is among the most complex motor activities humans perform , requiring the 53 

coordination of approximately 100 muscles across laryngeal, respiratory and oral motor systems. 54 

This coordination integrates two fundamental speech features: articulation, which shapes the 55 

configuration of the vocal tract, and phonation, the generation of voiced sound . Such intricate 56 

orchestration depends on a distributed neural network spanning both cortical and cerebellar 57 

regions (Guenther & Hickok, 2016) . Prior imaging work has primarily characterized neocortical 58 

representations of speech features , yet how the cerebellum encodes these fundamental building 59 

blocks of speech remains poorly understood. 60 

Cortical representations of speech motor control have been most thoroughly characterized in 61 

the primary sensorimotor cortex. The ventral sensorimotor cortex (vSM) project s directly to 62 

brainstem nuclei that innervate the vocal tract via multiple cranial nerves (Jürgens, 2002; 63 

Penfield & Boldrey, 1937) . Neural populations in this region are selectively tuned to specific 64 

speech effectors, including the lips, tongue, jaw, and larynx, forming a somatotopic organization 65 

that mirrors the anatomy of the vocal tract (Bouchard et al., 2013; Carey et al., 2017) . In contrast, 66 

a more dorsal region of the sensorimotor cortex (dSM) has been traditionally associated with 67 

trunk movements involved in voluntary breathing (Foerster, 1931; Penfield & Boldrey, 1937; but 68 

see Gordon et al., 2023) . A recent study contrasting voiced and whispered speech reported 69 

greater activation in dSM during voiced speech, suggesting that this region in involved not only 70 

in respiration, but also in the control of phonation (Correia et al., 2020) .71 

In addition to the primary sensorimotor cortex, the cerebellum plays a critical role in speech 72 

production (Ackermann & Brendel, 2016) . Patients with cerebellar lesions often present with 73 

ataxic dysarthria, a speech motor control syndrome characterized by inaccurate articulation ,74 

harsh voice quality and disrupted speech timing , implicating the cerebellum in both aspects of 75 

speech motor control (Ackermann et al., 1997; Ackermann & Hertrich, 1997) . Accordingly, 76 

kinematic and acoustic studies in cerebellar patients reveal deficits across both domains, 77 

including abnormal articulatory movement dynamics (Ackermann et al., 1995) , as well as 78 

increased instability in pitch and voice quality during phonation (Ackermann & Ziegler, 1994; 79 

Ackermann, 1991). 80 

Imaging studies show that overt speech activates two separate regions in each cerebellar 81 

hemisphere : a superior region in lobules VI and an inferior in lobul es VIII (Ackermann, 2008; 82

supplies the superior cerebellum (Ackermann et al., 1992) . This raises the possibility that 87 

superior and inferior cerebellar speech regions support different aspects of speech processing. 88 

Both cerebellar speech regions overlap with cerebellar motor territories implicated in tongue 89 

movement (Nettekoven et al., 2024; Saadon -Grosman et al., 2022) . In the limb motor system, the 90 

cerebellar hand area is reciprocally connected with the hand representation in the sensorimotor 91 

cortex, forming a closed cortico -cerebellar loop (Kelly & Strick, 2003; Saadon -Grosman et al., 92 

2022) . By analogy , the cerebellar speech areas may be preferentially coupled with vSM , in which 93 

case they should exhibit articulatory representations similar to those observed in vSM .94 

Alternatively, they may be preferentially connected with the dorsal sensorimotor area (dSM) ,95 

predicting representations that primarily reflect phonatory control. A third possibility is that 96 

cerebellar speech areas resemble higher -order neocortical speech regions , such as the 97 

supplementary motor area (SMA) or speech -related regions in the tempor oparietal junction. 98 

To address this question , we used high -field (7T) fMRI while p articipants produced syllables 99 

that differed in place of articulation and voicing. We combined high -resolution imaging with 100 

representational similarity analysis (RSA) to characterize how specific speech features are 101 

encoded in cortical and cerebellar activity patterns. Consistent with previous work (Bouchard et 102 

al., 2013) , activity patterns in vSM primarily distinguished between places of articulation. In 103 

contrast, representational geometries in dSM differentiated between voiced and voiceless 104 

consonants, in line with the proposed role of this region in phonation (Correia et al., 2020) .105 

Secondary sensori motor speech areas outside primary motor cortex showed a mixture of these 106 

two representations . We then asked whether cerebellar speech representations resemble those 107 

observed in cortical speech regions. 108

Stimuli . Six different consonant -vowel (CV) syllables were visually presented to the 118 

participants at the center of the screen using PsychoPy (https://www.psychopy.org/). The 119 

syllables were composed of a plosive consonant (/p/, /b/, /t/, /d/, /k/, /g/) followed by the vowel 120 

/a/ ( Fig. 1A). Plosives are produced by blocking the airflow in the vocal tract and then releasing 121 

it, creating a burst of air. The plosive syllables in the current experiment varied along two axes: 122 

place of articulation (PoA) and voice onset time (VOT). PoA refers to the location in the vocal 123 

tract where the airflow is obstructed. In plosive sounds, the blockage can be formed with the lips 124 

(/p/, /b/), with the tongue tip against the alveolar ridge (/t/, /d/), or with the back of the tongue 125 

against the velum (/k/, /g/), corresponding to the bilabial , alveolar , and velar sounds ,126 

respectively. VOT refers to the interval between the release of the plosive closure and the onset 127 

of vocal fold vibration. In voiceless plosives (/p/, /t/, /k/), the vocal folds do not vibrate during 128 

the release, whereas in voiced plosives (/b/, /d/, /g/), the vocal folds do vibrate during the release. 129 

Experimental design . Participants underwent MRI scanning in a single session. We acquired an 130 

anatomical image and 10 functional runs. Right before the MRI scan, participants were 131 

familiarized with the behavioral task in a short behavioral session (2 runs, ~20 min). On each 132 

trial, a syllable was presented on the screen for 2 seconds, followed by a fixation cross for 2 133 

seconds ( Fig. 1B). Participants were instructed to repeat the syllable out load three times at a 134 

comfortable pace during the 2s presentation period, and to remain silent during fixation to 135 

prevent overlap across task phases. Each run lasted ~4 minutes and included four blocks of 48 s 136 

separated by 14 s rest . Within each block all six syllables were presented, with each syllable 137 

appearing twice on consecutive trials. The order of items pairs within a block was randomized ,138 

resulting in a total of eight presentations of each item per run. Item and trial repetitions were 139 

included to improve the contrast -to -noise ratio (CNR). A period of 10 s rest was added at the end 140 

of each functional run to allow for signal relaxation and provide a better estimate of baseline 141 

activation. The entire MRI session, including the anatomical scans and setup, lasted ~60 minutes. 142

Preprocessing . Functional data were preprocessed in native space for each participant separately 154 

using SPM12 (fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm) and c ust om MATLAB code. Our minimal preprocessing 155 

pipeline included the following steps: First, functional images were corrected for geometric 156 

distortions caused by magnetic field inhomogeneity using the gradient echo field map (Hutton et 157 

al., 2002) . Then, functional images were realigned to the first volume of the first run to correct 158 

for head motion (six parameters: translation x, y, and z, and rotation pitch, roll and yaw). Lastly, 159 

the biased -corrected functional data were co -registered to the anatomical T1 image , for which the 160 

(0,0,0) coordinate was moved to the anterior commissure (AC). No smoothing or normalization 161 

to a group template was performed at this stage. 162 

First level general linear model. The preprocessed functional images were analyzed with a 163 

general linear model (GLM), using a separate regressor for each syllable (/p/, /b/, /t/, /d/, /k/, /g/), 164 

for each run. The activation of each trial (consisting of three repetitions of the same syllable) was 165 

modeled using a boxcar function of length 2 sec convolved with a two -gamma canonical 166 

hemodynamic response function with a peak at 5 sec and a post -stimulus undershoot minimum at 167 

11 seconds. This analysis resulted in activation images (beta maps) for each condition per run, 168 

for each participant. Rest was not modeled explicitly but served as an implicit baseline. 169 

Neocortical surface reconstruction . Reconstruction of cortical surface from the anatomical 170 

image was carried out using Freesurfer (Fischl et al., 1999) . In this procedure, white -gray matter 171 

and pial surfaces were reconstructed for each participant . The surfaces were then inflated into a 172 

sphere, and aligned to the left -right symmetric template atlas (fs_LR.32k ; Van Essen et al., 2012) 173 

based on sulcal depth and curvature information . The functional data were projected from native 174 

space to the subject’s individual surface , by averaging beta values of voxels intersecting the line 175 

connecting corresponding vertices of the individual white matter and pial surfaces. 176 

Cerebellar normalization . Cerebellar isolation and segmentation into white and gray matter 177 

were performed using the Spatially Unbiased Infratentorial Template (SUIT) toolbox 178 

implemented in SPM12 (Diedrichsen, 2006) . For each subject, the automatic segmentation was 179 

carefully inspected and , when necessary, manually corrected by one of the authors (S.J.) to 180 

exclude voxels originating from non -cerebellar tissue (e.g., visual cortex) . Cerebellar gray and 181 

white matter maps were then normalized into SUIT space using a non -linear deformation 182 

algorithm (Ashburner, 2007) . The activation estimates (i.e., beta weights) and residual mean -183

syllables. For each voxel or vertex and each run, we first subtracted the mean response across all 203 

syllables . This step removes shared global activations that are not specific to individual syllables. 204

Group -level univariate analysis . To assess whether mean activation differed systematically as a 217 

function of articulatory features, we performed a repeated -measures ANOVA on percent signal 218 

change values extracted from each ROI. The model included within -subject factors of Place of 219 

Articulation ( PoA; bilabial, alveolar, velar), and Voic e Onset Time (VOT; voiced, voiceless), 220 

with subject treated as a random effect. Significant main effects were followed up with post -hoc 221

To assess the topological organization of different PoA , we computed subject -level contrasts 223 

for each articulatory category (bilabial, alveolar, velar) against the remaining syllables. Group -224

Multivariate pattern analysis of syllable -specific representations . To quantify how much 228 

activation patterns for each syllable differed from each other, we calculated cross -validated 229 

distance between pairs of activity patterns (Nili et al., 2014) , resulting in a syllable × syllable 230 

representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) . Prior to calculating the distances, beta weights for 231 

each voxel were divided by the estimated noise standard deviation from the GLM for this voxel .232 

This univariate prewhitening step has been shown to increase the reliability of the RDM (Walther 233 

et al., 2016) . To obtain a cross -validated estimat e of the distances, we multiplied the difference 234 

between the activity pattern of two syllables in one imaging run with the difference computed in 235 

any other imaging run. This procedure ensures that if two patterns differ only due to noise, then 236 

the expected distance estimate is zero (Diedrichsen et al., 2021) . For visualization purposes only ,237 

the RDM values were square -root transformed and then normalized by the ir Euclidian norm to 238 

remove overall scale differences across ROIs . Note that distances between activity patterns 239 

within each ROI are statistically independent of the data used for ROI definition, as ROIs were 240 

defined based on average activity across all syllables relative to rest .241

Leave -one -out non -negative regression . To identify which cortical areas contribute unique, 299

3. Results 307 

3.1. Identifying regions responsive to syllable production 308 

We identified six neocortical regions and two cerebellar regions activated during overt syllable 309 

repetition (Fig . 2A-C). These areas included a large region in the ventral primary motor (M1) and 310 

sensorimotor (S1) cortex (vSM ). A second , smaller region was identified more dorsal ly (dSM ), 311 

situated between the hand and foot representation. We also observed consistent activity in 312 

primary auditory cortex and the auditory belt (Aud) , the supplementary and pre -supplementary 313 

motor areas (SMA), as well as the sylvian parietal -temporal area (Spt ). Together, these regions 314 

correspond to the “minimal speech production network” described by Bohland & Guenther 315 

(2006) .316 

Additionally, we identified significant speech -related activity in the parietal operculum. This 317 

region was located immediately ventral to vSM (Fig . 2B ), yet could be clearly distinguished 318 

from it on the cortical flatmap (Fig. 2A). Based on the cytoarchitectonic atlas, the activation was 319 

centered primarily in OP4, a subregion of the parietal operculum considered homologous to the 320 

parietal ventral area (PV) in non -human primates (Eickhoff et al., 2006) .321

3. 3. Representational geometry in cortical speech regions 370 

Having established reliable syllable -specific information, we next examined its organization 371 

across regions using representational similarity analysis (Kriegeskorte & Diedrichsen, 2019) . For 372 

each ROI, we computed the c ross -validated Mahalanobis distance between each pair of syllables. 373 

A distance estimate greater than zero indicate s a reliable difference between the regional activity 374 

patterns for these two syllables. All pairwise distance estimates were then combined into a 375 

representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) per region (Fig. 4A). 376 

We first tested whether there were any significant differences in representational geometry 377 

between the left and right hemispheric compartments of each functional region. In our task, we 378 

found no significant hemispheric differences in the RDMs (see Methods , all ps > 0.1) . We 379 

therefore average d the data from left and right hemispheres to produce a single representational 380 

estimate per ROI. In addition to the pattern differences between syllables, we also quantified 381 

mean activation responses for each syllable within each ROI (Fig. 4B). 382 

We then examined how syllables are represented within each region , testing whether the 383 

place of articulation (PoA) or voice onset time (VOT) were represented independently from the 384 

other feature (Fig. 4D; see Methods ). This analysis revealed a strong encoding of PoA in the 385 

vSM ( t(11) =5. 26, p=6.6 6×10 -4), the pOP (t(11) =3.9 7, p=.00 2), and the auditory cortex (t(11) =4. 34 ,386 

p=.0 01) . Univariate analysis revealed significant main effects of PoA in vSM and auditory 387 

cortex , consistent with the RSA results . Interestingly, SMA also showed a significant univariate 388 

PoA effect, though this was not reflected in the representational geometry (Table 1). Post -hoc 389 

comparisons revealed distinct response profiles across these regions: in the vSM alveolar 390 

syllables elicited the highest activation, whereas in the SMA and auditory cortex velar syllables 391 

evoked the strongest responses (Fig. 4B). 392 

Encoding of VOT was observed in dSM ( t(11) =3.02 , p=.0 11 ), SMA ( t(11) =2.4 3, p=.03 3), Spt 393 

(t(11) =3. 34 , p=.0 06), pOP ( t(11) =2. 89 , p=.0 14 ), and vSM ( t(11) =2.82 , p=.01 6), indicating sensitivity 394 

to voicing structure in these regions. Consistent with these representational effects, univariate 395

4. Discussion 458 

Using 7T fMRI and multivariate representational analysis, we examined how the brain 459 

encodes different features of syllable production , place of articulation and voice onset time, 460 

across cortical and cerebellar regions. Within primary sensorimotor cortex, we found evidence of 461 

a functional dissociation: ventral regions encoded syllables primarily according to articulatory 462 

configuration, while dorsal regions showed a greater sensitivity to the temporal onset of voicing. 463 

In addition to other expected areas, we identified a region in the parietal operculum that encoded 464 

a combination of place of articulation and phonation. Addressing our main research question, we 465 

found that both the superior and inferior cerebellar speech regions exhibited a similar mixed 466 

representation of these two speech features . Rather than mirroring the representational structure 467 

of either the ventral or dorsal primary sensorimotor speech region, the cerebellar representations 468 

aligned most closely with those of the parietal operculum. Th ese findings suggest an opercular -469 

cerebellar circuit involved in speech motor control that operates beyond the level of primary 470 

motor execution. 471 

472 

4.1 . Speech representations in the primary motor cortex 473 

In this study, we found a functional contrast between vSM and dSM in their tuning to speech 474 

features . The tuning of the vSM for place of articulation , and its somatotopic organization, are 475 

consistent with its established role in the control of articulatory muscles (Bouchard et al., 2013; 476 

Carey et al., 2017) . While dorsal sensorimotor cortex has traditionally been associated with trunk 477 

movements involved in voluntary breathing (Foerster, 1931; Penfield & Boldrey, 1937) , our 478 

current findings add representation -level evidence that its role in speech motor control extends 479 

beyond respiration. Correia et al. (2020) first suggested this idea based on greater activation in 480 

dSM during voiced versus whispered speech that persisted even when respiratory demands were 481 

matched . Here, we extended this by showing that dSM represent syllable s according to their 482 

voice onset time, demonstrating structured encoding of voicing distinctions across multiple 483 

place -of -articulation contexts. Together, these findings support a functional dissociation within 484 

primary sensorimotor cortex: vSM encodes articulatory configuration organized somatotopically 485 

along the vocal tract, while dSM encodes characteristics of voicing. 486 

It should be noted that the VOT sensitivity in the dSM did not significantly dominate PoA in 487 

a non -negative linear regression model, suggesting that dSM is not exclusively tuned to voicing .488

respiratory demands across voiced and voiceless conditions, future studies will be needed to 497 

dissociate phonatory from respiratory contributions to dSM activity. 498

4.2. Parietal operculum 500 

Our data revealed a speech -related area in the parietal operculum that was anatomically 501 

separate from the adjacent vSM (Fig. 2A -B) and showed a distinct representational organization 502 

encoding both place of articulation and voice onset time (Fig. 4). The parietal operculum has 503 

been subdivided into four cytoarchitectonically and functionally defined areas (OP1 -4; Burton et 504 

al., 2008; Eickhoff, Schleicher, et al., 2006) , with OP1 and OP4 corres ponding to secondary 505 

somatosensory cortex (SII) parietal ventral area (PV) in non -human primates , respectively 506 

(Eickhoff, Amunts, et al., 2006). In humans, OP1 has been mainly implicated in light touch, pain, 507 

and tactile discrimination (Burton et al., 2008) . S peech related activity in the present study ,508 

however, was mostly observed in OP4, which is functionally interconnected at rest with auditory 509 

and motor speech -related areas (Sepulcre, 2015) . The structured encoding of place of articulation 510 

and voice onset time observe in OP4 further supports a role for this area in speech production .511 

Previous fMRI studies of speech production have reported speech -related activity in the 512 

approximate vicinity of OP4, but its interpretation has been inconsistent. This activity has been 513 

variously ignored or attributed to the Rolandic operculum (Bohland & Guenther, 2006; Tourville 514 

et al., 2008) , or parietal operculum (Bailey et al., 2021) , reflecting inconsistencies in anatomical 515 

nomenclature across the literature. Precise localization is further complicated by the proximity of 516 

OP4 to the posterior insula and superior temporal cortex, regions that can be difficult to 517 

distinguish in volume -based analyses. By defining ROIs on the cortical surface and anchoring 518

4. 3. Cerebellar speech representations 522 

Our main goal was to determine how cerebellar speech representations fit into the neocortical 523 

speech network. We found that the cerebellum encodes both place of articulation and voicing, 524 

with a stronger representation of place of articulation . This pattern is consistent with the 525 

proposed role of the cerebellum in coordinating articulatory movements during speech, as well as 526 

with clinical observations of articulatory impairments following cerebellar damage (Ackermann 527 

et al., 1992) . Representations of both articulatory and phonatory speech features have previously 528 

been reported in the cerebellum ( Correia et al. 2020) . The present findings extend this work by 529 

directly comparing the relative contribution of these dimensions, revealing that place of 530 

articulation contributes significantly more to the representational structure than voicing. 531 

However, articulatory feature dominance in the cerebellum was substantially weaker than in 532 

vSM. Accordingly, cerebellar syllable representations were not best explained by the 533 

representational structure observed in vSM. This finding is difficult to reconcile with a simple 534 

closed -loop cortico -cerebellar motor model, in which each cerebellar motor territory is 535 

reciprocally connected with a single corresponding sensorimotor cortical region (Kelly & Strick, 536 

2003; Saadon -Grosman et al., 2022) . While this framework appears to capture the relationship 537 

between cortical and cerebellar representations of hand movements (King et al., 2023; Wiestler 538 

et al., 2011) , our results suggest that it does not generalize to speech production. Instead, 539 

cerebellar speech representations were most similar to those observed in the parietal operculum .540 

We propose several possible explanations for this finding. First, because cerebellar BOLD 541 

signals are thought to predominantly reflect inputs to the cerebellar cortex rather than the output 542 

of Purkinje cells (Caesar et al., 2003; Thomsen et al., 2009) , the similarity between cerebellar 543 

and opercular representational geometries may indicate that the cerebell ar speech regions receive 544 

a substantial portion of its input from the parietal operculum . Alternatively, the cerebellum may 545 

integrate input s from multiple speech -related motor regions, including vSM, dSM and potentially 546 

other cortical areas. Either way, the integrated representation of speech features in the cerebellum 547 

could support the role of these areas in the coordination of articulation and phonation. 548

previous reports of dual representations of body parts within sensorimotor regions of the 553 

cerebellum, despite a lack of clear functional differentiation between these spatially segregated 554 

representations (Nettekoven et al., 2024; Wiestler et al., 2011) . While our current data suggest 555 

that there is no difference in the level of phonetic feature encoding between these regions, future 556 

studies are required to evalua te their functional profiles across a wider spectrum of conditions. 557 

558 

4.4. Temporal -parietal speech representations 559 

Speech production relies on interactions between the motor and auditory systems (Guenther, 560 

2006; Hickok et al., 2011) . Our results show that speech evokes robust activations in temporal 561 

and parietal regions, including the auditory cortex and the Sylvian parietal -temporal area (Spt), 562 

that are implicated in translating auditory feedback into motor commands during speech 563 

production (Buchsbaum et al., 2001) . Previous studies have shown that the auditory cortex 564 

encodes acoustic -phonetic features during speech perception (Binder et al., 2000; Mesgarani et 565 

al., 2014) . Our data extends these findings by demonstrating that the auditory cortex represents 566 

phonatory features not only during speech perception, but also during speech production. Spt , on 567 

the other hand, did not show a clear representational structure of syllables, despite showing 568 

stronger mean activation for voiceless than voiced sounds. What drives this univariate sensitivity 569 

without corresponding representational structure remains unclear. 570 

571 

4. 5. Lateralization of speech representations 572 

In line with the left -dominant organization of speech motor systems (Hickok & Poeppel, 573 

2007) , we observed left lateralization of both mean activation and syllable -specific 574 

representational information in vSM and SMA. This extends prior findings to the level of fine -575 

grained representational structure, suggesting that the left hemisphere not only activates more 576 

strongly during speech but also represents phonetic features more distinctly. It should be noted 577

4. 6. Summary and conclusions 588 

In summary, we found a functional dissociation within primary sensorimotor cortex, with 589 

ventral regions encoding place of articulation and dorsal regions encoding voice onset time. The 590 

parietal operculum, in contrast, encoded both phonetic dimensions, suggesting an integrative 591 

representation of speech features. Contrary to predictions derived from simple cortico -cerebellar 592 

motor circuit models, cerebellar speech representations did not mirror those of primary 593 

sensorimotor cortex. Instead, they were most closely aligned with representations in the parietal 594 

operculum, a higher -order somatosensory region situated at the interface between auditory and 595 

motor speech systems. These findings point to an opercular -cerebellar circuit as a key component 596 

of the speech network and suggest that the cerebellum contributes to higher -order sensorimotor 597 

computations that extend beyond the control of articulatory movements alone. 598
