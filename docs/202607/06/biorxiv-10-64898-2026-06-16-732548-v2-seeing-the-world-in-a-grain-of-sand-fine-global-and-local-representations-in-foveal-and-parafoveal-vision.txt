Summary 27 

Foveal vision enables primates to perceive small objects with remarkable precision by resolving both 28 

global form and fine local detail. Yet current coarse-to-fine and local-to-global frameworks, derived 29 

largely from parafoveal recordings and large-object representations, posit that V1 encodes local detail 30 

whereas IT represents global form, leaving the neuronal basis of foveal object representation 31

Introduction 44 

Visual perception has fascinated scientists and philosophers for centuries. What appears effortless to 45 

us is the perception of objects in terms of both their global shapes or forms and local parts, with 46 

remarkable precision in the fovea. The fovea is a small, capillary-free depression at the center of the 47 

macula, approximately 0.4–0.5 mm in diameter corresponding approximately to the central 2° of 48 

retinal eccentricity. It contains the highest concentration of cone photoreceptors with slow temporal 49 

responses specialized for high-acuity form vision 1,2 (Figure 1A ). This is reflected in visual acuity 50 

testing, in which both small and large optotypes (such as C or E) are seen equally clearly when 51 

presented in the fovea. However, the cortical mechanisms underlying the joint representation of the 52 

global form and local parts of a coherent object remain unclear, especially in central vision, where 53 

both global and local features are perceived with comparable sharpness. This question, foreshadowed 54 

by William Blake’s poetic musings, gained scientific substance with the work of the Gestalt 55 

psychologists. They studied the psychophysical laws of visual perception and proposed that the global 56 

percept of the whole is processed prior to the local parts of a stimulus 3,4 , emphasizing that the whole 57 

has emergent properties that are not the simple sum of its parts. This perspective laid the conceptual 58 

foundation for subsequent theoretical and physiological work on local–global representations in the 59 

visual brain. 60 

Although global and local features have distinct visual characteristics, both can be characterized 61 

by distance-dependent spatial-frequency (SF) composition, which can be quantified using classical 62

two-dimensional Fourier analysis of their spatial power spectra 5 . For example, Fourier analysis of the 63 

Portland Vase image revealed that local features are biased toward higher-SF components, whereas 64 

global structure is dominated by lower SFs ( Figure 1B ). Notably, the power spectrum shifts toward 65 

lower SFs as the aperture size increases. Thus, low-SF visual information is classically assumed to 66 

convey the global structure 6-8 , and to precede high-SF local information in the primate ventral visual 67 

pathway 8-12 . This is referred to as coarse-to-fine analysis based on SF information analysis 8-10,12,13 ,68 

and is supported by neural recordings of parafoveal regions in V1, V2, and V4 toward object-selective 69 

regions in inferotemporal (IT) cortex 5,14-18 , where preferred SFs generally decrease along the ventral 70 

visual hierarchy when tested with sinusoidal gratings. 71 

However, global shape is not necessarily defined only by low-SF information. For both small 72 

and large objects, their global shapes are defined mostly by sharp edges and lines, and natural objects 73 

and scenes often contain a broad range of low- and high-SF components 19,20 . Furthermore, neurons 74 

representing the foveal region have very small receptive fields (RFs) and support exceptionally high 75 

visual acuity 14-16 . This high acuity allows foveal vision to resolve not only local details but also 76 

emergent global forms defined by high-SF components ( Figure 1AB ). Accordingly, as visual acuity 77 

declines exponentially with increasing eccentricity, global form remains readily perceived but 78 

becomes blurred in the parafovea and peripheral vision (2–6° and beyond) 21 . Furthermore, cortical 79 

neuronal recordings from parafoveal regions have revealed that the sizes of neuronal RFs increase 80 

markedly along the primate ventral visual pathway 22-25 , allowing cortical neurons to integrate 81 

information over larger visual space and to represent large objects and scenes with increasing 82 

complexity 23-27 . These findings form the basis of a dominant hierarchical feature-integration 83 

framework, which posits that V1 primarily encodes local details whereas IT represents objects and 84 

scenes through progressive pooling of lower-level features. However, this framework, which 85 

emphasizes a transformation from local to global representations, does not specify how fine global 86 

form of small objects is jointly represented with fine local information within foveal representations. 87

Results 112 

The complexity of natural images, which contain multiple orientations and other visual attributes, 113 

limits their utility for isolating the neural mechanisms of global and local processing without applying 114 

oriented spatial filters 9,12,13,29 . To overcome these limitations, we constructed two-dimensional tire-115 

track-like global–local stimuli composed of aligned local line elements ( Figure 1D ), which can be 116 

reliably identified in neuronal responses at both single-cell and population levels. The SFs of these 117 

global–local stimuli were set at 0.5 cycles/° and 3 cycles/° representing coarse global and fine local 118 

information, respectively. Conceptually similar to the hierarchical stimuli introduced by Navon 6 ,119 

these stimuli enabled psychophysical validation showing that global form and local features with 120 

orthogonal orientations are perceived with comparable precision within the central 2° of the fovea 121 

(Figure 1E ). In contrast, global form becomes increasingly dominant in the parafoveal region and 122 

beyond. This eccentricity-dependent decline in acuity affects local feature perception more strongly 123 

than global structure, indicating that global and local processing vary across retinal eccentricity along 124 

cortical hierarchy. 125 

To address cortical cellular mechanisms underlying fine global and local representations in foveal 126 

and parafoveal vision, we performed two-photon imaging with single-cell resolution in four 127 

successive cortical areas along the ventral stream—V1, V2, V4, and IT—at eccentricities of 1–2° 128 

within the foveal region 30,31 in awake macaques ( Figure 1F ). In the parafoveal regions of these 129 

cortices (2–6° eccentricity), we combined large-window intrinsic-signal optical imaging of 130 

population responses with extracellular electrophysiological recordings 5,31,32 .131 

132 

Distinct spatial components of global form and local features 133 

In contrast to control stimuli of oriented sinusoidal gratings, whose spatial power is concentrated at a 134 

single SF, global–local stimuli contain multiple SF components ( Figure S1A ), consistent with the 135 

Fourier analysis results obtained from the image of the Portland Vase. Notably, in addition to spectral 136 

power being concentrated at the low SF that matches the designed global SF of 0.5 cycles/°, global 137 

forms also contain high-SF components, including those derived from the oriented line endpoints. 138 

Accordingly, the power spectra of global information spanned from coarse to fine (0.5 to 8 cycles/°). 139 

In contrast, fine local information remains sharp, as it is dominated by spectral power of high SFs, at 140 

the preset 3 cycles/° and above. 141

Importantly, as stimulus size increases progressively to match neuronal RFs across retinal 142 

eccentricity and cortices along the visual hierarchy 24,25 , the proportion of global SF power rises 143 

rapidly relative to local SF power ( Figure S1BC ), suggesting that neurons with larger RFs are 144 

exposed to proportionally much more global information containing both low- and high-SF 145 

components. These results indicate that global form and local parts are represented in parallel but not 146 

in fixed proportion within inherently overlapping spatial-frequency domains. As stimulus scale and 147 

RF size increase across retinal eccentricity and the visual hierarchy, low-SF components increasingly 148 

dominate global representations. Thus, global and local representations arise from scale-dependent 149 

mixtures of low- and high-frequency components rather than from strictly separable spatial-frequency 150 

bands. This spatial analysis challenges the common practice of using low-SF filtering as a proxy for 151 

global information 8 .152

Hierarchical population representations of global and local in the parafovea 154 

Based on the spatial analysis of global form and local features ( Figure S1 ), we first investigated how 155 

global and local information are represented at the population level in the parafoveal regions across 156 

the visual hierarchy. Cortical population responses in V1, V2, and V4 at eccentricities spanning from 157 

1° to 6° were simultaneously recorded with intrinsic-signal optical imaging in eight hemispheres from 158 

five macaques ( Figure 1F ). Differential orientation maps (dark–bright domains) in response to 159 

drifting global–local stimuli, with angular differences between global and local orientations of 90°, 160 

45°, and 0°, were obtained ( Figure 2A ). Oriented drifting sinusoidal gratings served as control stimuli. 161 

By analyzing orientation preference maps (colored contours) overlaid on the differential orientation 162 

maps for each area 5,32 (Figure S2 ), we found that the population response profiles to grating stimuli 163 

in all visual areas exhibited peaks and troughs at the expected orientations (0°, 90°, 45° and 135°). 164 

For global–local stimuli, population responses in V1 and V2 aligned consistently with the local 165 

orientations. In contrast, those in V4 tracked only the global orientation and were unaffected by the 166 

orientations of local elements. To further illustrate global and local representations across retinal 167 

eccentricity in V1, V2, and V4, we constructed global–local preference maps by extracting the peak 168 

orientation of the response profile at each pixel in response to the above global–local stimuli ( Figure 169 

2B ). In V1 and V2, peak orientations were consistently centered at around 90° or 45°, corresponding 170 

to the local orientations of the stimuli, whereas in V4 peak orientations only represented the global 171 

orientations. These hierarchical population responses—global-dominant in V4 and local-dominant in 172

However, we previously demonstrated that there exist high-SF functional domains in parafoveal 195 

V4 encoding local orientations of the global–local stimuli 5 , suggesting that both local and global 196 

features are represented in parallel in V4. To further investigate how global and local representations 197 

vary with retinal eccentricity, we focused on V4, a key intermediate stage linking early visual cortex 198 

with higher cortical areas. Because V4 has larger receptive fields and a lower cortical magnification 199 

factor than V1 and V2, a smaller cortical surface area in V4 can represent a relatively larger region 200 

of visual space. Within the global–local preference maps of peak responses (global: 0°, local: 45°), 201 

we observed red-hot and dark-blue regions encoding local and global orientations, respectively 202 

(Figure 2C ). Importantly, local orientations dominated V4 in its foveal regions within 2° eccentricity, 203 

whereas at more peripheral regions peak orientations shifted toward global encoding. These results 204 

indicate that V4 retains fine local information from foveal V1 and V2 inputs, while increasingly 205 

representing global information across retinal eccentricity within its larger RFs. Due to the limited 206 

spatial resolution of intrinsic-signal optical imaging, however, it remains unclear how global form 207 

and local information are encoded neuronally at each cortex of the visual hierarchy. 208

Neuronal encoding of global and local information within the parafovea 210 

To address the above question at the neuronal level, we extracellularly recorded 155, 90, and 189 211 

orientation-selective neurons in V1, V2, and V4, respectively, at eccentricities between 2° and 6° in 212 

three awake macaques ( Figure 1F ). We characterized the response tuning of individual neurons to 213 

global–local stimuli, in which global and local orientations differed by 90°. Oriented sinusoidal 214 

gratings were used as control stimuli to identify each neuron’s preferred orientation. Based on feature 215 

selectivity quantified by a global–local index (GLI) 5,32 , neurons were then classified as local-216 

orientation-selective, dual-orientation-selective or global-orientation-selective (Figure 3A and 217 

Figure S5A ). Note that a subset of neurons displayed dual tuning, with two response peaks 218 

corresponding to local and global orientations of global–local stimuli, respectively. These three types 219 

of neurons were found across all cortical areas, indicating that both global and local information from 220 

the same stimuli emerge at the neuronal level as early as V1 and propagate in parallel along the ventral 221 

visual hierarchy. 222

Temporal processing of global and local information in the parafovea 280 

The perceptual coarse (global) to fine (local) framework, in which global information is proposed to 281 

be processed before local detail, is supported by observations that neurons preferring low SFs exhibit 282 

shorter response latencies than those preferring higher SFs when tested with sinusoidal gratings 8,34 .283 

These slower high-SF responses likely originate in the primate fovea, which is characterized by a 284 

high density of cone photoreceptors and midget ganglion cells with relatively slower temporal 285 

responses than those in the perifovea 2,35 . We confirmed the latency differences between low and high 286 

SF by using sinusoidal gratings in V1, V2 and V4 ( Figure 4AB ) and V4 in previous study 5 . In addition, 287 

these response latencies of either onsets or peaks exhibited monotonical increases for all SFs across 288 

the visual hierarchy. However, whether neuronal responses to coarse and fine global forms defined 289 

by low and high SFs, respectively, together with fine local detail within global–local stimuli are 290 

themselves temporally distinguishable has not been examined directly. Given the band-pass nature of 291 

SF tunings of neuronal responses to sinusoidal gratings 14-16 and the broadband SF power spectrum of 292 

global information in the global–local stimuli ( Figure S1 ), neurons preferring intermediate SFs (1–3 293 

cycles/°) could not be assigned to either low- or high-SF global groups. Thus, we only compared 294 

response latencies of global neurons in response to global–local stimuli, but based on their preferred 295 

SFs were either below 1 cycle/° (low-SF global) or above 3 cycles/° (high-SF global) measured with 296 

sinusoidal gratings. As the SF components of local features in the global–local stimuli were 3 cycles/° 297 

and above ( Figure S1 ), neurons in responses to local orientation were classified as high-SF local 298 

orientation selective. Therefore, responses latencies were compared only among low-SF global, high-299 

SF global and high-SF local groups at each cortical area along the visual hierarchy ( Figure 4CD ). 300

Discussion 469 

In our daily life, we can effortlessly resolve both fine global shapes or forms and fine local details of 470 

small objects in central vision, while also grasping the gist of multiple items in the surrounding scene. 471 

However, a century-old question, dating back to the Gestalt psychologists and their proposal of 472 

“global precedence” 3,4 , remains unresolved: how are global form and local features represented at the 473 

neuronal level to generate coherent visual perception across the visual field? Current hierarchical 474 

theories of visual feature integration, including local-to-global and coarse-to-fine frameworks 8-

475  

> 10,12,13,22-25

, have not specifically explained how global forms and local details are jointly represented 476 

by cortical neurons in primate foveal vision. 477 

478 

Multi-scale global and local processing across eccentricity and cortical hierarchy 479 

The Gestalt notion of “global precedence” 3,4 gave rise to classical models of visual processing in 480 

which global form perception is associated with low-SF information and local feature analysis with 481 

high-SF components 7,41 . This view has shaped dominant frameworks in which coarse global structure 482 

is extracted early and subsequently refined by local analysis, for example through “hierarchy and 483 

reverse hierarchy” loops in which IT represents the global whole before fine local details are 484 

recovered from V1 inputs 8,10 . However, both natural image statistics 19,20 (Figure 1B ) and the global– 485 

local stimuli used here ( Figure S1A ) indicate that global structure is not confined to low SFs, but 486 

instead spans a broad range of spatial scales, including high-SF components. Consistent with this, our 487 

neuronal recordings from foveal and parafoveal regions showed that high-SF-preferring neurons were 488 

not limited to their expected role in local processing; a substantial high-SF population also encoded 489 

fine global information across ventral cortical areas. In foveal vision, neurons with small RFs and 490 

high-SF sensitivity support not only fine local analysis but also the encoding of fine-scale global form, 491 

enabling global and local information to coexist in high-resolution representations. Thus, these 492 

findings revise conventional coarse-to-fine accounts by showing that global perception cannot be 493 

reduced to coarse spatial processing in the foveal vision. They provide a neuronal basis for the 494

Our results also refine classical hierarchical accounts of global and local processing, which have 497

complex representations in higher visual areas 10,22,23,25 ,26 . Although this feature integration and 499 

assembly theory suggest that object recognition may culminate in IT and other higher cognitive areas, 500

parallel across all stages of the ventral visual pathway, with a gradual shift towards global dominance 502

1A ). In other words, IT neurons with large RFs can represent not only coarse global form, but also 506 

fine global form together with fine local detail within the foveal portion of their RFs. 507 

Beyond the expected high-SF local-selective neurons, the presence of both fine- and coarse-scale 508

Closely spaced temporal processing of global form and local features 526 

In primate retina, the fovea cones exhibited slower light responses up to 30 milliseconds than 527 

peripheral cones 2,39 , supporting high acuity but slow visual perception 38,40 . Regardless, our neuronal 528 

latency results from parafovea recordings accord with classical accounts of coarse-to-fine perception 8 ,529 

which propose that rapid processing of low-SF global information provides a causal basis for the 530 

perceptual precedence of global form 8,10,13 . We refined this framework by distinguishing fine global 531 

processing from coarse global processing: high-SF global-selective neurons exhibited the longest 532 

response latencies across parafoveal representations (2°–6° eccentricity) in V1, V2 and V4. The 533 

latencies of low-SF global, high-SF local, and high-SF global responses all increased monotonically 534 

along the visual hierarchy, consistent with feedforward propagation and progressive integration 535 

across successive cortical stages 25-27 . However, these response latencies were far below ~100ms, the 536 

timescale required for object identification and recognition 8,50,51 . The response delays among these 537 

groups were tightly clustered within only a few milliseconds ( Figure 4 and Figure S6 ), and how 538 

such small temporal differences contribute to rapid categorization and identification of small objects 539 

remains unclear. Regardless, these distinct yet closely spaced responses to global form and local detail 540 

suggest that meaningful visual representations emerge early within the ventral visual hierarchy prior 541 

to extensive engagement of higher cognitive cortices 8,11 . Furthermore, our finding that the temporal 542 

processing of fine and coarse global forms by high- and low-SF-preferring neurons emerges as early 543 

as V1 and persists across successive cortical stages does not support reverse-hierarchy models in 544 

which coarse-to-fine processing is proposed to proceed from IT to V1 8,10 . Although the precise role 545 

of top-down feedback from IT and other higher cortical areas to earlier visual cortices remain unclear, 546 

it may contribute to higher-level functions such as attentional modulation and size- and position-547 

invariant object representation 48,52,53 . Importantly, SF tuning to drifting gratings is itself dynamically 548 

time-dependent 54,55 , raising the possibility that coarse-global, fine-local and fine-global 549 

representations may likewise evolve over time. Consistent with this possibility, recent evidence 550 

suggests that early IT responses encode relatively simple features, whereas later responses encode 551 

more complex features 56 . Psychophysical studies have shown that removing low-SF information 552 

diminishes the temporal advantage of global processing 57,58 , consistent with our observation that low-553 

SF global neuronal responses occurred before high-SF fine-local and fine-global responses. Together, 554 

these parafoveal response latency findings reveal a tightly spaced temporal sequence of cortical 555

The advantage of fine global representation for small objects in foveal V1 559 

V1 neurons are not merely simple orientation and spatial-frequency filters, as suggested by responses 560 

to sinusoidal grating stimuli, but also have the capacity to integrate local information into precise 561 

global representations of small objects. The simplest example of visual feature construction is the de 562 

novo generation of orientation and direction selectivity through spatiotemporal integration of 563 

collinear organized lateral geniculate nucleus inputs by V1 layer 4 neurons with simple RFs 59-63 .564 

Many V1 neurons also exhibit complex RF properties that encode line ends, angles and other local 565 

configurations 22,30,42 .566 

Recent findings further demonstrate that this integrative capability critically depends on cross-567 

laminar interactions across V1 sublayers. For example, cascaded normalization mechanisms across 568 

V1 sublayers substantially transform spatial integration from input to output layers 64 , whereas high-569 

SF information in primate V1 is not merely inherited from feedforward P-pathway inputs, but can be 570 

selectively amplified by local recurrent circuits in superficial output layers 65 . More recently, studies 571 

using natural images and complex visual stimuli have shown that V1 neurons respond robustly to 572 

richer visual structures than simple Gaussian–Gabor filter models would predict 30,33,42-44,66 . Together, 573 

these findings indicate that V1 provides a more flexible substrate for spatial integration than classical 574 

filter-based descriptions alone would suggest. 575 

This computational capacity for global information processing is especially important for small-576 

object representation in foveal vision. Small objects fall within the high-acuity foveal map, where V1 577 

neurons have small RFs and preserve precise retinotopic relationships. Thus, retinotopic information 578 

about fine global form and local detail can be represented together before spatial information is 579 

degraded by pooling in higher cortical areas. This view is consistent with our finding that V1 and V2 580 

neurons encoding fine global and local features exhibit more linear responses when tested with 581 

sinusoidal gratings, whereas V4 responses are more nonlinear, suggesting progressively greater 582 

integration across visual space at higher cortical stages. 583 

By contrast, large objects extend across foveal, parafoveal and peripheral regions and therefore 584 

require progressively broader, coherent integration over larger RFs in higher visual cortices, drawing 585 

on distributed inputs from early visual areas. Such integration is essential for representing large-scale 586

Several limitations of the study 592 

First, selective attention can bias perception toward either global structure or local detail 67,68 . Because 593 

our recordings were performed during passive fixation in awake monkeys, without task demands that 594 

selectively emphasized local or global information, we could not determine how attention modulates 595 

global–local processing. Thus, the present study addresses feedforward and intrinsic global–local 596 

representations rather than selective visual attention. Second, because we used two-dimensional 597 

global–local stimuli rather than natural three-dimensional (3-D) objects, our findings should be 598 

interpreted as evidence that V1 responses most likely represent two-dimensional global 599 

contours/outlines, including fine-scale high-SF global structure such as aligned line endpoints in the 600 

stimuli, rather than as evidence that 3-D global objects are represented in V1. In fact, 3-D objects are 601 

represented in IT through hierarchical cortical integration 25 . Third, the cortical mechanisms 602 

underlying global and local processing in the foveola, corresponding approximately to the central 0– 603 

1° of visual space, remain unresolved. This limitation reflects not only the technical challenges of 604 

neuronal recording or imaging in the primate visual system 46,69 , but also a more fundamental 605 

constraint: the spatial resolution of the foveola exceeds that of standard visual display systems. 606 

Consequently, stimuli with sufficiently well-defined global and local features cannot yet be presented 607 

at a scale matched to the extremely small RFs of foveolar neurons. Although our recordings did not 608 

directly sample the foveola, our near-foveolar dominance of high-SF representations of both global 609 

form and local features suggests that fine-scale global structure may extend toward the center of gaze, 610 

where very small objects can be resolved with superhigh acuity. 611

Psychophysics on human 653 

Psychophysical experiments were conducted in 8 participants (5 males and 3 females; 23–38 years 654 

old) with normal or corrected-to-normal vision ( Figure 1E ). All participants provided written 655 

informed consent prior to participation, and all procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee 656 

of the Institute of Neuroscience, Chinese Academy of Sciences (No. CEBSIT-2023032). 657 

Visual stimuli were presented on a gamma-corrected monitor (Display++, Cambridge Research 658 

Systems; 1920 × 1080 pixels, 120 Hz) positioned 67 cm from the eyes, subtending 60.8° × 34.2° of 659 

visual angle. Luminance calibration was performed using a SpectroCAL MKII spectroradiometer 660 

(Cambridge Research Systems Ltd., Rochester, Kent, UK). Stimuli were generated using 661 

Psychtoolbox-3 in MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Eye position of the right eye was 662 

monitored using an EyeLink 1000 system (SR Research Ltd., Ottawa, Ontario, Canada) at a sampling 663 

rate of 1000 Hz, and fixation was required within a 0.5° window. 664 

Global–local stimuli were conceptually similar to the seminal hierarchical stimuli introduced by 665 

Navon 6 and consisted of a global band constructed from short white line elements on a black 666 

background. Stimuli were presented within an edge-blurred circular aperture (radius, 1.2°), either at 667 

fixation (0°) or along the right horizontal meridian at eccentricities of 2°, 4°, 8°, or 12°. The SFs of 668 

the global and local components were fixed at 0.5 and 3 cycles/°, respectively. The global orientation 669 

was either near-horizontal (nominal orientations: 3° or 177°) or near-vertical (nominal orientations: 670 

87° or 93°), with a random trial-by-trial jitter of ±0.5°. Local line orientations were selected from the 671 

same set of possible orientations, with the constraint that they were near-orthogonal to the global 672 

orientation. A two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) task was used. Each trial began with a text cue 673 

(“global” or “local”) instructing participants to report the orientation of the corresponding stimulus 674 

component. After a 500 ms fixation period, the stimulus was presented for 200 ms. Participants were 675 

instructed to respond as quickly and accurately as possible after stimulus onset by reporting whether 676 

the cued orientation was tilted left or right relative to the vertical axis. Reaction time was measured 677
