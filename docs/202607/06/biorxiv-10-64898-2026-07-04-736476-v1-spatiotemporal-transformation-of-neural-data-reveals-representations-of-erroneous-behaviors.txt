## Abstract 13 

Abnormal states such as erroneous be haviors  are generally difficult to represent from neural data. 14 

However, such states are also known to have spec ific spa tiotemporal features , indic ating a 15 

feasibility of developing a method to focus on them . If a method can highlight these 16 

spatiotemporal features, it may effectively represe nt such abn ormal states,  helping  evaluate 17 

abnormal brain functions . In the present study , we proposed  the hierarchy of supported modules 18 

(HSM)  to highlight  spatiotemporal features  that can  represent  abnormal states.  HSM 19 

spatiotempo rally tr ansforms multidimensional neural  time -series based on their spatiote mporal 20 

context . We evaluated HSM through  decoding and similarity analyses  using  multiple  publicly 21 

available datas ets.  In the HSM results, de cod ing accuracies were higher for erroneous behaviors 22 

than for  normal  behaviors, and similarities were lower between erroneous behaviors and  normal 23 

behaviors than between  normal  behaviors,  demonstrating the ability of HSM to capture  the 24 

spatiotemporal features of erroneous behaviors. Su rprisingly, m any parts of these results were 25 

also pre sent  even before HSM learning, showing the virtue of HSM as a simple -to -use meth od. 26 

The  proposed  HSM method  may help elucidate t he mechani sms underly ing erroneous behaviors. 27

## 1. Introduction 33 

The human brain  has evolved  to  optimiz e human perfor mance.  As such , the  human  brain  is 34 

unlikely  to  represent  suboptimal states such as erroneous behavior . This may lead to a lack of 35 

neural representation s for err oneous behaviors in the  brain.  However, th e spatiotemporal 36 

structure s of neural a ctivity dur ing errone ous beha viors are  not merely random, but  kno wn to 37 

contain representations of  these states. For example, ne ural travelin g waves in the brain, which 38 

consist  of sequences of neural act ivities, propagate in  opposite directions during erroneous 39 

be haviors ( Alamia et a l. 202 3) . When a human commits errors, there are  characteristic  event -40 

related potentials in mid -frontal area ( Holmes and Pizzagalli, 2008 ).  Furthermore,  prolonged 41 

blood -oxygen -level -dependent  (BOLD) responses  are observed  during  erroneous  behaviors ( Janik 42 

et al., 2022 ). To  explore  neural represe ntations durin g abnorm al stat es, it is therefore important to 43 

consider the spatiotemporal context of neural activity,  beyond a single pattern of neural activity. 44 

A single pattern of neural activity does not  have one -to -one correspondence  to a single piece of 45 

neural information. This is well demonstrated by the phen omenon  of trial -to -tr ial neural variability 46 

in which neural activity patterns vary over trials in response to the same neural information 47 

(Nogueira et al., 2 020; Zhang et al., 202 2;  Nakuci et al., 202 3). Th is v ariability emerges as specific 48 

spati otempo ral structures  that are d istinct from noise  (Ni et al., 2022; Rib ei ro et al., 2024; Ake lla  et 49 

al., 2025). Previous  model  studies ha ve shown tha t recurrently -networked s tructures can  yield 50 

structured  variability (Kotekal and Ma cLean, 2020; Negr ón e t al., 2024),  which  has  also  been 51 

verified  experimentally (Xi ao et a l.,  2024). In networked neural systems, it is  more  important to 52 

focus on  the spatiotemporal context of n eural  activity than inspecting instantaneous spatial neural 53 

activity  patterns  alone  (for a r eview, see Ju and Bas sett, 2020). If  an identical  pattern of neural 54 

activity in  different  spatiotemporal context s can b e disentangl ed , a more accurate assessment of 55 

neural information dynamics  can be  made . T herefore, it is important to dis entangle, or  more 56 

genera lly transfor m, neural data by taking spatiotemporal context into accoun t. Thi s may improve 57 

our understanding of representations of  abnormal  states. 58 

In relation to neural variability, a method fo r disentangli ng neural data to reduce independent 59 

noise has been int roduced (Lecoq et  al., 2021). However, this method did not a ddress the 60 

spa tiotemporal context of ne ura l dat a. Because the  recurrently networked structures of the brain 61 

generate the spatiotemporal context of neural data, recurrent neural networks can be used to 62 

measure this spatiotemporal context ( Du rstewitz et al., 2023).  On e such example is the estimation 63 

of the dynamical system of neural data through recurrent neural networks (K oppe et al., 2019). 64 

However,  recurrent neural networks in such studies are trained solely to describe dynamical 65

## 2.5.  EEG processing 312 

We preprocessed  EE G data usin g the MATLAB toolbox  EEGLAB  (https://sccn.ucsd.edu/eeglab/ ).  To 313 

remove artifacts from scalp EEG, we first applied a ban dpass f ilter at 1 -50 Hz to the raw data 314 

using a finite impulse response (FIR) filter. Eye -blink artifacts were then removed using 315 

independent component analysis (ICA). The remaining artifacts were removed using the artifact 316 

subspace reconstruction (ASR) method. 317 

To mitigate the volume -conductio n effects on scalp EE G, we applie d surface Laplacian filtering t o318 

the artifact -free EEG da ta  using the CSD toolbo x (Kayser and Tenke, 2006a; Kayser and Tenke, 319 

2006b; Kayser, 20 09).  We used  the s tandard  CSD parameters, m  = 4 and  λ = 10 ⁻⁵ , as i n previous 320 

st udies o n EEG oscillations ( Tenke et al., 2011; Sihn et al., 2023; Sihn and Kim, 2024) .321 

To isolate theta, alpha, and beta oscillations, we  applie d FIR bandpass filters to the surface -322 

Laplacian -filtered data. The passband ranges of the FIR filters were 4 -7, 8 -12, and 13 -30 Hz for 323 

theta, alpha, and beta oscillations, respectively. 324 

To obtain amplitude envelopes of oscillations, we applied the Hilbert transform to the bandpass -325 

filtered theta and beta oscillations. The absolute values of the Hilbert -transformed data were 326 

taken as the amplitude envelopes. 327 

To obtain traveling waves of oscillations, we first extracted the oscillatory phases of the 328 

oscillations. To this end, we applied the Hilbert transform to the bandpass -filtered theta and beta 329 

oscillations. The angles of the Hilbert -transformed data were taken as the oscillatory phases. 330 

Based on these phases, we measured traveling waves using the local phase gradient (LPG) 331 

method developed in our previous study (Sihn and Kim, 2024). These traveling waves were spatial 332 

three -dimensional vectors and thus circular rather than linear values. Because HSM requires linear 333

## 4. Discussion 566 

In the present study, the HSM method was devised to transform multidimensional neural time -567 

ser ies da ta ba sed on their spatiotemporal context so as to represent abnor mal stat es such as 568 

erron eous beha viors and brain diseases. HSM sp atiotemporally transforms multidimensional 569 

neural time -series data b ased o n their spatiotemporal context, and this was confirmed through 570 

examinations of synthetic data using decoding and similarity analyses. In the HSM results, 571 

decoding accuracies were higher for errone ous behavi ors than for other behaviors, and similarities 572 

were lower between erroneous behaviors and other behaviors than between other behaviors, 573 

highlighting the spatiotemporal features of erroneous behaviors. Moreover, in the HSM results, 574 

the contrast of similarities between behaviors dif fered be tween healthy controls and patients with 575 

various brain diseases, highlighting the spatiotemporal features of brain diseas es. Surprisi ngly, 576 

many parts of these results we re al so p resent even before HSM learning, showing the virtue of 577 

HSM as a simple -to -use  method. 578
