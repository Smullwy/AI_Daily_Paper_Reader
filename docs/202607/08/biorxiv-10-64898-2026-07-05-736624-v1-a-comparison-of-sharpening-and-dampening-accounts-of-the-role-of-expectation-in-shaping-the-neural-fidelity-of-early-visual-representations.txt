ABSTRACT 1

The natural environment is spatiotemporally structured, and the brain exploits this 2

regularity to predict and prepare for upcoming sensory stimuli. Such predictive 3

processing is thought to increase neural e ffi ciency by reducing metabolic expenditure 4

and altering the fidelity with which newly encountered stimuli are encoded. 5

Competing theoretical frameworks propose this is achieved either through 6

sharpening, whereby expected events are encoded more precisely, or dampening, 7

whereby expected events are suppressed and encoded less precisely. Despite clear, 8

opposing predictions, evidence in humans for each account remains mixed due to 9

methodological and analytical inconsistencies. Here we addressed these issues using 10 

probabilistic visual paradigm combined with functional magnetic resonance imaging 11 

(fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG). We used population receptive field (pRF) 12 

mapping of fMRI data and inverted encoding of EEG data to compare the fidelity and 13 

timecourse of activity in visual areas in response to expected, unexpected, and 14 

random stimuli. Both methods produced a consistent pattern of results. Post hoc 15 

analysis of EEG data revealed that the apparent e ff ect of expectancy was better 16 

explained by local spatiotemporal stimulus properties than the global expectancy 17 

manipulation. Although this pattern resembled sensory adaptation, it was more 18 

consistent with an expectation of temporal stability combined with dampening, in 19 

which both the aggregate response to expected features and their representational 20 

fidelity are suppressed. Taken together, our findings suggest that predictive 21 

processing may operate through dampening, with ecological advantages for high-22 

fidelity encoding of unexpected sensory events. 23

INTRODUCTION 24 

The brain is sensitive to the spatial and temporal structure inherent in the natural 25 

environment, and can use this structure to predict and prepare for upcoming sensory 26 

inputs. For example, moving objects typically follow predictable trajectories that can 27 

be anticipated to support the e ff ectiveness of motor behaviours ranging from smooth 28 

pursuit eye movements and saccades to collision avoidance and interception 29 

(Wolpert & Flanagan, 2001). Anticipation of sensory events may also be used to 30 

support neural e ffi ciency, by reducing metabolic expenditure and increasing the 31 

fidelity with which sensory events are encoded (Barlow, 1961; Friston, 2005; Rao & 32 

Ballard, 1999). 33 

Two popular frameworks - sharpening and dampening – have been proposed 34 

to explain the mechanism by which expectation shapes sensory processing. While 35 

the two explanations agree that metabolic expenditure is reduced in response to 36 

expected sensory events, they di ff er in the means through which this is achieved, and 37 

off er opposing predictions for the fidelity of expected and unexpected stimulus 38 

encoding. The sharpening account claims that neurons tuned to near-to-expected 39 

features are suppressed, resulting in expected events being encoded more precisely 40 

(Kok et al., 2012; Teufel et al., 2018; Yon et al., 2018). By contrast, the dampening 41 

account asserts that expected features are suppressed, resulting in expected events 42 

being encoded less precisely (Lee & Rideaux, 2025; Rideaux, Dang, et al., 2025; Tang 43 

et al., 2018, 2023). A common approach to experimentally testing the e ff ects of 44 

expectancy has been to use probabilistic cues and compare behavioural or neural 45 

responses to target stimuli that are either likely or unlikely based on the preceding 46 

cue. The sharpening account predicts that probabilistically likely (expected) stimuli 47 

will be encoded more sharply than unlikely (unexpected) stimuli, and the dampening 48 

account predicts the opposite pattern. 49 

Despite the clear and falsifiable predictions made by the sharpening and 50 

dampening frameworks, there is still no consensus as to which account better 51 

describes the influence of expectation on the fidelity of perceptual representations. 52 

Compelling evidence for both accounts has been provided across a range of 53 

experimental designs and neuroimaging methods (Kok et al., 2012; Rideaux, Dang, 54 

et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2023, 2023; Teufel et al., 2018; Yon et al., 2018). There are 55

several possible explanations for these seemingly discrepant findings. The e ff ects of 56 

expectancy can be challenging to separate from other phenomena, such as sensory 57 

adaptation and attention, which can confound results and lead to misattribution of 58 

altered sensory processing (den Ouden et al., 2023; Feuerriegel, 2024). 59 

Methodologically, biophysical di ff erences in the signals recorded by non-invasive 60 

neuroimaging methods, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and 61 

electroencephalography (EEG), may play a role in determining which framework is 62 

supported, even within the same experimental design. For example, if sharpening and 63 

dampening both occur, but at distinct temporal delays or cortical locations (Press et 64 

al., 2020; Teufel & Fletcher, 2020), these methods, which vary in their spatiotemporal 65 

resolution, may produce conflicting results. 66 

While phenomenological and methodological factors are strong candidates for 67 

contributing to empirical discrepancies, another factor that may further obscure 68 

consensus are analytical di ff erences, particularly in human studies where 69 

representational fidelity is inferred from aggregate or indirect metrics of neural 70 

activity. For instance, while the sharpening and dampening accounts make opposing 71 

predictions about the sharpness with which expected events are encoded, 72 

multivariate decoding accuracy (i.e., comparisons between local patterns of neural 73 

activity) is typically used as a proxy of this precision, with higher values interpreted 74 

as evidence for more precise representations (Kok et al., 2012; Tang et al., 2018; Yon 75 

et al., 2018). Indeed, decoding accuracy is assumed to reflect the discriminability of 76 

stimuli, such that if a stimulus is represented more sharply it will be more 77 

distinguishable from other stimuli and thus lead to more accurate decoding. However, 78 

decoding accuracy also reflects the reliability of signals over multiple measurements, 79 

which may change independently of encoding precision. For example, a sensory 80 

event may elicit a neural response that is similar to other sensory events within a given 81 

feature-space (e.g., orientation), but if the reliability of the response across repeated 82 

presentations is increased, then decoding accuracy will improve, despite the 83 

sharpness of the representation remaining unchanged (Stehr et al., 2023). Thus, using 84 

decoding accuracy to test whether expected events are encoded more or less sharply 85 

has limitations that may contribute to the current lack of clarity around the influence 86 

of expectation on sensory encoding. 87

An alternative analytic approach, which is commonly used to characterize the 88 

spatial selectivity of neuronal populations in visual cortex, and which may better 89 

distinguish between response reliability and tuning width, is population receptive field 90 

(pRF) mapping (Dumoulin & Wandell, 2008). This approach extends the classical 91 

notion of a single neuron’s receptive field—the region of sensory space where stimuli 92 

elicit responses—to the voxel level measurable with fMRI. In this framework, the 93 

response of a voxel is modelled as arising from the aggregate activity of many 94 

neurons with overlapping receptive fields. During pRF mapping, participants view 95 

stimuli (e.g., drifting bars, wedges, or expanding/contracting rings) that systematically 96 

traverse the visual field while blood-oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) responses are 97 

recorded. A computational model is then fit to the voxel time series, typically 98 

assuming a parametric receptive field shape (often a 2D Gaussian defined by position 99 

and size). By estimating the model parameters that best explain the observed 100 

responses, the visual field location and spatial extent over which each voxel responds 101 

can be inferred. Thus, in contrast to decoding accuracy, pRF mapping provides a 102 

more direct measure of representational sharpness (specifically, pRF width), which 103 

can be used to adjudicate between sharpening and dampening accounts of 104 

predictive processing. 105 

Here we used pRF mapping to compare the tuning width of receptive fields in 106 

visual cortex in response to otherwise identical visual stimuli expected, unexpected, 107 

or random. Data from ten participants were collected over three scanning sessions 108 

per individual (30 scanning sessions in total) using ultra-high field (7T) fMRI. Data from 109 

a further 24 participants were collected using EEG, using the same experimental 110 

paradigm, to exploit its superior temporal resolution. EEG data were analyzed using 111 

inverted encoding, which reconstructs stimulus features by first modeling how neural 112 

responses encode those features and then mathematically inverting that encoding 113 

model to estimate the feature representation from population activity patterns. Stimuli 114 

comprised checkerboard wedges presented at di ff erent polar angles, and their 115 

expectancy (expected, unexpected, no expectation) was manipulated through 116 

probabilistic cueing (high, low, and equal probability). The aim of the study was to 117 

compare responses to stimuli in the three expectancy conditions in both fMRI and 118

METHODS 121 

Participants. Ten human adults were recruited from The University of Queensland 122 

for the fMRI experiment (6 females; average age, 27.1±5.0 years), and 24 were 123 

recruited from the University of Sydney for the EEG experiment (18 females; average 124 

age, 23.4±6.0 years). Sample sizes were based on previous related fMRI (Infanti & 125 

Schwarzkopf, 2020) and EEG experiments (Rideaux, 2024). Participants were 126 

neurotypical (by self-report), had normal or corrected-to-normal vision (assessed 127 

using a standard Snellen eye chart), and naïve to the aims of the study. The fMRI and 128 

EEG experiment protocols were approved by Human Ethics Committees at The 129 

University of Queensland (2020/HE003101) and The University of Sydney 130 

(2023/HE000072). 131 

Stimuli and procedure. Stimuli comprised monochrome checkerboard wedges, 132 

presented on a mid-grey background, which subtended a visual angle of 12º from the 133 

central fixation point, with an arc of 22.5º ( Fig. 1a ). Wedges were presented for 750 134 

ms at one of 16 possible linearly spaced polar angles between 0-360º, and alternated 135 

polarity at ~6.6 Hz. A black dot (radius, 0.1º) was presented centrally throughout the 136 

experiment, to support stable fixation. In each trial, a pair of wedges was presented 137 

sequentially, with pairs separated by a blank inter-pair-interval of 1500 ms. On 80% 138 

of trials, the second wedge in each pair was presented at the polar opposite location 139 

to the first (high probability, “expected” stimuli); in the remaining trials, the second 140 

wedge was presented at any of the remaining 16 locations, excluding the location of 141 

the first wedge and its polar opposite (low probability, “unexpected” stimuli; Fig. 1b ). 142 

To establish expectations in participants, the first eight trials of each block were 143 

always high probability stimuli. Trials were presented in blocks of 240 (~8 min), and 144 

each experimental session comprised five blocks. In the fMRI experiment, each 145 

participant completed two experimental sessions and one retinotopic mapping 146 

session, each ~1 h in duration. In the EEG experiment, each participant completed a 147 

single experimental session (~1 h). In the fMRI experiment, participants additionally 148 

completed an initial familiarization session, which was the same as the experimental 149 

sessions, but was undertaken in a behavioural lab without neural recordings. The data 150 

from the familiarization session were not included in any of the analyses. 151

Population receptive field mapping. The fMRI data from the experimental blocks 193 

were used to obtain independent estimates of the pRFs using a custom MATLAB 194 

toolbox for pRF analysis (SamSrf v9.9, https://github.com/samsrf). We predicted the 195 

neural response of each vertex in the cortical surface mesh from the overlap of a 196 

binary aperture describing the position of the wedge stimuli within each scanning 197 

volume with a model of the underlying pRF. We then convolved this with a canonical 198 

haemodynamic response function (HRF) to predict the BOLD signal in the 199 

experimental sessions. The binary aperture was a two-dimensional mask (100 × 100) 200 

corresponding to the stimulus location on the screen at each time point. We 201 

estimated a unidimensional pRF model of polar angle using wedge apertures 202 

(Dumoulin & Wandell, 2008), constrained to locations on a circle around fixation with 203 

radius 6º (half the outer eccentricity of the wedge stimulus). This e ff ectively produced 204 

a one-dimensional tuning model defined by two parameters: μ indicated the preferred 205 

polar angle of the voxel and σ corresponded to the tuning width. To compare the 206 

width of pRFs in response to the di ff erent stimuli (first stimulus and high/low/equal 207 

probability second stimulus), which were interleaved throughout the experimental 208 

blocks, we modified the standard pRF model to include three separate σ parameters 209 

to describe tuning width, σ1st for the initial stimulus in each pair, σLow for the stimuli 210 

that violated expectations, and σHigh for expected stimuli (i.e., occurring with di ff erent 211 

probabilities). This modification, combined with the large data volume produced by 212 

each fMRI block, increased the computing power required to fit parameters. This 213

described the measured activity in the EEG sensors given the location of the 287 

presented wedge. The forward model was then used to obtain the inverse model that 288 

described the transformation from EEG sensor activity to stimulus location (Lee & 289 

Rideaux, 2025; Rideaux, 2024). The forward and inverse models were based on 290 

neural responses to the first wedge within each pair to obtain the inverse model on 291 

which responses to the second wedge were decoded. 292

DISCUSSION 525 

The temporal context in which stimuli are embedded can drive expectations 526 

that support adaptive behaviours and effi cient sensory processing. Here we 527 

combined pRF mapping of fMRI BOLD responses with inverted encoding of EEG 528 

recordings to adjudicate between sharpening and dampening accounts of how 529 

expectation influences representational fidelity of simple visual stimuli. The fMRI 530 

results appeared to support a sharpening account of expectation (Kok et al., 2012; 531 

Teufel et al., 2018; Yon et al., 2018); however, analysis of EEG data revealed that 532 

di ff erences observed between processing of high and low probability stimuli were 533 

unlikely to reflect expectation established by our probability manipulation. Instead, 534 

the findings were more parsimoniously explained by local spatiotemporal properties 535 

of the stimuli. 536 

We manipulated expectancy by presenting stimuli at locations with either high 537 

(80%), low (20%), or equal probability. The results from both the fMRI and EEG 538 

experiments provided converging evidence that high probability stimuli elicited 539 

sharper spatial representations. However, the univariate responses to these stimuli 540 

were also higher, directly contradicting expectation suppression (Summerfield et al., 541 

2008), which is thought to conserve metabolic expenditure in response to expected 542 

stimuli. If the di ff erences in spatial tuning were caused by the probability 543 

manipulation, two patterns of results should be hypothesised for low and equal 544 

probability stimuli as a function of angular distance from the high probability location. 545 

First, if expectation tuning is highly precise, high probability stimuli should be more 546 

accurately decoded, and there should be no e ff ect of angular distance for either low 547 

or equal probability stimuli, because only stimuli presented exactly at the expected 548 

location should elicit an anticipatory e ff ect. Alternatively, if expectation tuning is not 549 

highly precise, then low probability stimuli (but not equal probability stimuli) presented 550 

near the expected location should be decoded more accurately, because stimuli 551 

presented at near-to-expected locations should also elicit an anticipatory e ff ect. 552 

However, neither of these hypotheses was supported. Instead, decoding accuracy 553 

and precision for low and equal probability stimuli were the same as for high 554 

probability stimuli up to o ff sets of ~45°, at which point they both declined as a 555 

function of angular distance. 556

These results suggest that di ff erences in spatial tuning were caused by local 557 

spatiotemporal stimulus properties, specifically, the angular distance between the 558 

first and second wedges in each pair. This would explain why there was no di ff erence 559 

between low and equal probability stimuli, and why di ff erential decoding accuracy 560 

and precision were highest around the location of the first wedge and lowest around 561 

the (high probability) opposite location. The changes in decoding accuracy and 562 

precision were accompanied by a repulsive bias away from the initial wedge location, 563 

which is consistent with a sensory adaptation e ff ect (Whitaker et al., 1997). However, 564 

the presentation duration of the first stimulus (750 ms) was considerably shorter than 565 

the standard duration used to induce sensory adaptation (30 s; Cli ff ord, 2002), and 566 

other aspects of the phenomenon we observed here seem inconsistent with such a 567 

process. For instance, previous EEG decoding work has shown that adaptation to 568 

orientation induces both increased univariate responses and decoding precision 569 

around the adapted location (Rideaux et al., 2023), whereas here we observed the 570 

opposite. 571 

Recent work on the influence of temporal context on sensory processing has 572 

shown that repeated events are remembered less precisely than those that change 573 

(Lee & Rideaux, 2025). This could be explained by an underlying expectation that 574 

sensory events will stay the same, or repeat, rather than change. The notion of an 575 

expectation for visual stability may seem counterintuitive given that retinal input is 576 

often changing, e.g., due to saccades, self-motion, and world-motion. However, for 577 

a prediction to be useful (e.g., to reduce metabolic expenditure or speed up 578 

responses) it must be somewhat precise. A prediction that retinal input will change is 579 

not necessarily useful unless it can specify what it will change to. If change is 580 

predicted, given retinal input of 𝑥 ! at time t, the range of possible values of 𝑥 !3. is 581 

infinite. By contrast, if no change is predicted, such that 𝑥 !3.=𝑥 !, then a precise 582 

prediction can be established and utilized. There is, of course, other information that 583 

can be used to reduce the parameter space of predicted change from 𝑥 !, for example, 584 

the value of 𝑥 !-.. However, these sources of expectation are not mutually exclusive. 585 

Indeed, across the infinite distribution of changes from 𝑥 !, zero change will occur 586 

more frequently than any other value, so it seems plausible that the brain would be 587 

sensitive to this pattern. 588

If there is indeed an expectation of temporal stability, the current findings 589 

would support the dampening account of expectation. Expected stimuli (those 590 

nearby to the repeat location) were suppressed both at the univariate level, due to 591 

expectation suppression, and their fidelity was reduced, as indicated by both broader 592 

pRFs and reduced decoding accuracy and precision. Moreover, the shape of the 593 

di ff erential decoding as a function of angular distance suggests suppression around 594 

the expected (repeat) location, rather than enhancement around the least expected 595 

(opposite) location. Ecologically, suppressing expected sensory inputs may be 596 

adaptive because these events are already captured by the system’s predictive 597 

model, whereas unexpected inputs must be encoded precisely in order to e ff ectively 598 

update the model (Soltani & Izquierdo, 2019). 599 

Taken together, the current findings are consistent with previous work on 600 

predictive processing, suggesting that expected events are suppressed, and that 601 

unexpected events are more reliably decoded from neural activity (Richter et al., 602 

2022; Tang et al., 2018) and behaviourally reproduced more precisely (Rideaux, Dang, 603 

et al., 2025). In contrast, they seem to contradict studies that have shown expected 604 

events are decoded more reliably (Kok et al., 2012) and produce more precise 605 

behavioural responses (Rohenkohl et al., 2014). The interaction between global 606 

probability and local spatiotemporal properties observed in the current study 607 

provides an example of the challenging nature of isolating the influence of expectation 608 

and may o ff er insight into the discrepant findings associated with this phenomenon. 609 

The fact that the probability manipulation did not induce detectable neural e ff ects is 610 

also consistent with a growing body of evidence indicating that while probabilistic 611 

cuing paradigms produce robust behavioural e ff ects, they do not always elicit neural 612 

responses that can be reliably detected with human neuroimaging methods (den 613 

Ouden et al., 2023, 2025; Hu et al., 2025). 614 

Decoding accuracy is frequently used as a proxy for tuning width, as more 615 

sharply tuned responses are more distinguishable from responses to other points 616 

along a feature dimension and will thus result in more accurate decoding. However, 617 

the same outcome could also be produced, without changing tuning width, by 618 

increasing the temporal reliability of the response (Stehr et al., 2023). By applying pRF 619 

mapping and inverted decoding to neural recordings made during the same 620

A clear limitation of the current study is that the global expectancy 628 

manipulation did not appear to be e ff ective. While we found evidence of dampening, 629 

given the partial alignment of the pattern of results with sensory adaptation, further 630 

work is needed to determine whether the neural phenomena observed here were due 631 

to expectation of stability or adaptation. Another limitation of the study is that we did 632

cross-validate the effi cacy of the global spatiotemporal manipulation. Such a634

expectation of stability. 641

work is needed to rule out adaptation. Viewed through the lens of predictive 647

whereby expected inputs are suppressed and unexpected inputs are represented 649
