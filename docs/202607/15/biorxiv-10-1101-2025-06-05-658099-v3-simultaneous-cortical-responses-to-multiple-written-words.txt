ABSTRACT 16 

17 

We have learned much about the brain regions that support reading by measuring 18 neuronal responses to single words, but we know little about how the brain processes multiple 19 words simultaneously. This fMRI study fills that gap by varying the number of English words 20 presented while holding the amount of visual stimulation constant. We adapted the “simultaneous 21 suppression” paradigm, which has demonstrated that the response to multiple stimuli presented 22 simultaneously is typically smaller than the sum of responses to the same stimuli presented 23 sequentially. On each trial, participants viewed a rapid sequence of three frames. Each frame 24 contained two character strings, most of which were pseudo-letters with visual features matched 25 to familiar letters. The experimental conditions differed in the number of words in the sequence: 26 zero words; one word; two words sequentially; or two words simultaneously. Behavioral task 27 accuracy was worse for detecting two words presented simultaneously than sequentially. BOLD 28 responses increased linearly with the number of words presented in several reading-related 29 regions of the left hemisphere: text-selective occipito-temporal regions, the superior temporal 30 sulcus, the intraparietal sulcus, and the inferior frontal sulcus. In all of those regions, responses 31 did not differ between sequential and simultaneous presentation of two words. Nonetheless, the 32 sensitivity of ventral temporal text-selective regions to the lexical frequencies of two words was 33 attenuated by simultaneous presentation. To account for these patterns of activity and task 34 performance, we suggest that the reading network can detect two strings of letters 35 simultaneously, but there is interference during lexical access. 36 37 

Key words: visual word recognition; visual word form area; VWFA; simultaneous suppression; 38 parallel processing 39 40

INTRODUCTION 1

2To read this page, you must integrate information across many hierarchically arranged 3visual elements, from letters to words to sentences. Fluent reading requires a balance between 4holistic parallel processing of multiple elements and serial processing of chunks of text to avoid 5interference. Here, we investigate how reading-related brain areas process multiple words 6simultaneously. 7Most fMRI studies of visual word recognition have presented only one word at a time (e.g., 8Caffarra et al., 2021; Dehaene & Cohen, 2011; Yeatman & White, 2021). Others analyzed activity 9during sentence reading (e.g., Carter et al., 2019; Henderson et al., 2015; Schuster et al., 2015) 10 but were designed for other questions. EEG and MEG studies have provided important insights 11 into the dynamics of multi-word processing (e.g., Dunagan et al., 2025; Fallon & Pylkkänen, 2024; 12 Flick et al., 2021; Schotter et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), but fMRI offers superior spatial 13 resolution for specific reading-related regions. 14 Behavioral studies have raised deep questions about how efficiently readers can process 15 multiple words in parallel. The parafoveal preview benefit , for example, demonstrates that while 16 readers fixate on word N, they begin processing word N+1 (Schotter et al., 2012; Vasilev & 17 Angele, 2017). Some models of reading account for such effects by allowing parallel processing 18 of multiple words with divided attention (Engbert et al., 2005; Reilly & Radach, 2006; Snell et al., 19 2018; Snell & Grainger, 2019). Other models assume that readers finish processing the fixated 20 word before shifting attention covertly to the next word(s) (Pollatsek et al., 2006; Schotter et al., 21 2014). Several experimental tasks have also provided support for serial models (White et al., 22 2018, 2020; Brothers et al., 2022; Campbell et al., 2024; Johnson et al., 2022). 23 Behavioral deficits might arise from limited processing capacity in the “visual word form 24 area” (VWFA), a left ventral occipito-temporal region that links the visual system and the language 25 system (Dehaene & Cohen 2011; Yeatman & White, 2021). White et al. (2019) argued that a 26 posterior VWFA sub-region can process two words at once in separable “channels”. In contrast, 27 activity in the anterior sub-region was affected by the lexical frequency of only one of two words 28 per trial, consistent with a processing ‘bottleneck’ during lexical access (see also White et al. 29 2020). However, that study did not include a comparison condition with words presented one at 30 a time. 31 Here we leverage another neural correlate of processing capacity limits in vision: 32 

simultaneous suppression : a lower BOLD response to a set of stimuli flashed simultaneously 33 compared to the response to the same stimuli flashed in rapid sequence (Kastner et al., 1998; 34

Ours is the first study to draw on this literature to investigate how 3the responses in text- and language-selective regions vary as a function of the number of words 4being read. 5Previously reported simultaneous suppression effects could be due to more stimulus onset 6and offsets in the sequential condition, each of which causes a transient neural response, rather 7than the competitive interactions between stimuli (Kupers et al., 2024). ## Participants 19 

We recruited 22 participants (5 self-identified as male) from the Barnard College and 20 Columbia University student body, and general New York City Community (N = 4). As described 21 below, one participant had to be excluded from the analysis due to excessive head motion, leaving 22 a final sample of 21 participants. Their ages ranged from 19 to 40 years (mean = 24.4土6.3 years). 23 

All but one were right-handed. All participants had learned English before the age of five, and 24 provided written informed consent. They were monetarily compensated for their participation. The 25 study was approved by the Internal Review Board at Barnard College. 26 The sample size was chosen in advance of data collection. In our two previous fMRI 27 studies of BOLD responses to written words with similar protocols (Chauhan et al., 2024; White 28 et al., 2023), we included 15 and 17 participants, respectively. In this study, we explore a novel 29 effect (simultaneous suppression for words) which we expected might be smaller than the task 30 effects detected in those prior studies. We therefore set out to collect as much data as we had 31 funding for, which allowed full data sets for 22 participants. 32

## Equipment 20 

We acquired MRI data at the Zuckerman Institute, Columbia University. We used a 3T 21 Siemens Prisma scanner and a 64-channel head coil. At the start of each scanning session, we 22 acquired a T1-weighted structural scan, with 1 mm isometric voxels. We collected functional data 23 with T2* echo planar imaging sequences with multiband echo sequencing (SMS3) for whole brain 24 coverage without compromising temporal resolution. The TR was 1.5 s, TE was 30 ms, the flip 25 angle was 62° and the voxel size was 2 mm isotropic. 26 We presented the stimuli using custom code written in MATLAB and the Psychophysics 27 Toolbox (Brainard, 1997). We projected stimuli onto a screen that the participants viewed through 28 a mirror, with a viewing distance of 142 cm. The resolution of the display was 1920 by 1080 pixels, 29 and the refresh rate was 60 Hz. We also collected eye-tracking data with an SR Research Eyelink 30 1000 tracker. To respond to the tasks, the participant used their right hand to press one of three 31 buttons on a handheld MR-safe response pad. 32

## Stimuli 1

The stimuli and task for the main experiment are depicted in Figure 1A . A small black 2fixation dot was continuously present at the center of the screen against a light background (95% 3of screen maximum). The dots diameter was 0.11 degrees of visual angle (dva). The stimuli were 4five-letter English words. We selected 768 such words with no constraints on parts of speech. On 5the Zipf scale, the lexical frequencies ranged from 2.2 to 6.1, with a median of 4.1. Zipf is a 6standardized measure of word frequency calculated through an adjustment of the log frequency 7per million words in the SUBTLEX-US corpus (Brysbaert & New, 2009; van Heuven et al., 2014). 8Real words were presented in the Courier New, a standard monospaced serif font. The 9other “filler” strings were presented in an unfamiliar, illegible false font called BACS-2 (Vidal et al., 10 2017). These false font characters are designed to match the size, the symmetry, and the 11 numbers of strokes, junctions and terminations of Courier New. Both these fonts were scaled so 12 that the height of the letter ‘x’ was 0.41 dva. On average, the width of each character string was 13 2.65 dva, and the height was between 0.41 and 0.79 dva. The distance between centers of 14 neighboring letters was 0.55 dva. The stimuli were rendered in the same black color as the fixation 15 dot. 16 17 

## Trial sequence and task 18 

The stimulus presentation on each trial consisted of three successive frames. Two 19 character strings were presented in each frame, one above and one below the fixation dot. The 20 centers of the character strings were 1.5 dva from the fixation dot. This display arrangement differs 21 from the conditions of natural English reading, but we chose it for two reasons: because 22 behavioral evidence for serial processing of words is strong with that arrangement (White et al., 23 2020), and because both words are equally near to fixation and clearly legible. Arranging the 24 words horizontally would better match the conditions of natural reading, but there is a strong 25 right>left hemifield asymmetry (Yeatman & White, 2021). Indeed, a strong bias to process words 26 on the right complicated the results of White et al (2019). Crucially, the VWFA is known to respond 27 strongly to words in a region spanning roughly the central 5 degrees of the visual field (R. K. Le 28 et al., 2017; White et al., 2023), including just above and below fixation (Rauschecker et al., 2012). 29 Thus, the population receptive fields (PRFs) of the voxels we analyze are known to cover both of 30 the stimulus positions. 31 Each frame lasted for 183 ms, and there was a 50 ms inter-stimulus interval between 32 frames (with only the fixation dot presented). 33

## Functional Localizers 10 

Each participant completed two functional localizers: one to define category-selective 11 ventral temporal visual regions, and one to localize core language regions. The visual category 12 localizer is described in White et al. (2023) and Chauhan et al. (2024) (see also 13 https://github.com/Barnard-Vision-Lab/catLoc). Briefly, participants viewed images of four 14 categories of objects: text (including English words and pseudowords), false font strings, faces, 15 and limbs. See examples in Figure 1B . These categories were chosen to maximize contrast 16 between activity in the VWFA and surrounding face- and body-selective regions. Each frame 17 included three examples of each category, one at fixation and two larger ones, one to either side. 18 We used two fonts for the text, Courier New and Sloan, and two false fonts with matched visual 19 features, BACS-2 (Vidal et al., 2017) and PseudoSloan (Vildavski et al., 2021). Each 4-second 20 trial consisted of four frames with images of all the same category. Participants performed a one-21 back repetition detection task, pushing a button with their index finger whenever they saw the 22 same exact image repeated two times in a row. Repetitions occurred on 33% of trials. Each 23 participant completed four runs, each of which consisted of 54 trials and lasted for about six 24 minutes. ## Analysis of Behavior 14 

We defined accuracy as the proportion of trials with correct responses made within the 15 response period, which lasted 3.65 s after the offset of the final stimulus frame. On average, 16 participants failed to respond on time on 1.3% of trials (SEM=0.6%). Those trials were excluded 17 from the analysis of accuracy. 18 

## Eye-tracking data analysis 19 

We used an Eyelink 1000 eye-tracker (SR Research) to record gaze position data during 20 each scan. However, because of a suboptimal placement of the camera and infrared illuminator, 21 the data quality varied across participants. In some cases, it was not possible to calibrate the 22 tracker, or the tracker periodically failed to locate the participant’s pupil. For two participants, we 23 could not collect any gaze data. 24 The first step in our analysis of the eye-tracking data was to determine which trials had 25 data of acceptable quality. A whole run was excluded from the analysis of gaze positions if the 26 median standard deviation of gaze positions 250 ms before stimulus presentation was greater 27 than 1º. We chose that interval because participants were usually fixating stably just before the 28 stimuli arrived. Rapidly changing position estimates during that interval are a sign of a poor data 29 quality. 5 runs were excluded from the eye-tracking analysis for this reason (from a total of 3 30 participants). 31

## Preprocessing 3

We pre-processed our MRI data with fMRIPrep 21.0.1 (Esteban et al., 2019), which is 4based on Nipype 1.6.1 (Gorgolewski et al., 2011). Depending on the number of sessions, we 5obtained one or two T1-weighted structural scans for each individual. Cortical surfaces were constructed using the 8boundaries between gray matter and white matter using Freesurfer (Fischl, 2012). 9The functional MRI scans were preprocessed as follows: Magnetic field inhomogeneities 10 in each EPI sequence were corrected using a B 0 nonuniformity map collected during the same 11 session. All functional runs were co-registered to the native anatomical volume ( fsnative ) We then 12 slice-time corrected the multiband sequences and resampled them onto each participant’s cortical 13 surface. 14 We also used fMRIPrep to perform motion correction. Moreover, in order to ensure that 15 our data would not be contaminated by head motion, we used the following exclusion criteria. A 16 run with movement greater than 2 voxels (4 mm) was excluded from analysis. Additionally, if any 17 participant had such excessive head motion in more than half of the runs, that participant was 18 dropped from the analysis. Using these criteria, we excluded one participant from the analysis 19 entirely, leaving a final N of 21. A total of three runs were excluded for head motion from 2 of the 20 included participants. 21 22 

## BOLD Response Estimation 23 

We estimated BOLD responses (beta weights) to each single trial with GLMSingle in 24 Python (Prince et al., 2022). The result of this analysis is a beta weight for each trial, for each 25 cortical surface node in each participant. We first resampled the BOLD time-series from 1.5s to 26 1s. GLMSingle optimizes the hemodynamic response function for each node in the surface space 27 and performs denoising using fractional ridge regression to remove correlated noise. For the main 28 experiment, the design matrix consisted of a unique column for each stimulus condition (zero, 29 one, two-sequential or two-simultaneous words), uniquely coding trials in which the top word 30 appeared in the first or second frame, and uniquely coding the frame in which the words in the 1-31 word or 2-simultaneous conditions appeared. ## ROI Definition 4

We sought to define nine regions of interest (ROIs) in each hemisphere in each participant. 5See Figure 1D for these ROIs on an example participant’s inflated cortical surfaces. Details about 6the number of participants in which we were able to localize each ROI are provided in the tables 7that report statistics on the stimulus effects in each ROI. 17 The experiment included four main conditions: zero-word trials, one-word trials, two-word 18 sequential trials and two-word simultaneous trials ( Figure 1A ). The design allowed us to test two 19 hypotheses: 1) That the response magnitude in reading-related regions increases with the 20 number of items of their preferred stimulus category (i.e., words), while the amount of visual 21 stimulation is held constant. 2) That the response to two simultaneously presented words is lower 22 than the total response to two sequentially presented words, due to processing capacity limits. 23 This second hypothesis is based on earlier studies of simultaneous suppression in visual cortex 24 (e.g., Kastner, Weerd, et al., 2001; Kupers et al., 2024), and on prior behavioral studies that show 25 limited capacity for processing two words at once (e.g. White et al. 2020, White et al. 2026). ## Evidence of a capacity limit from lexical frequency effects 4

The fact that the mean BOLD responses were equivalent for simultaneous and sequential 5pairs of words could mean that reading-related brain regions can detect two strings of letters at 6once amidst the sequence of false font strings. However, that does not necessarily mean that two 7legible letter strings presented simultaneously were both processed lexically in the same way as 8two words presented in rapid succession were processed. The following exploratory analysis uses 9a signature of lexical processing to dig deeper into the comparison of the sequential and 10 simultaneous trials, focusing on the ventral temporal text-selective areas. 11 

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license available under a was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint (which this version posted July 13, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.06.05.658099 doi: bioRxiv preprint

29 Text-selective areas (often referred to as the VWFA) are known to respond more strongly 1to less common words (e.g., Chauhan et al., 2024; Kronbichler et al., 2004; Woolnough et al., 22021). There are a range of explanations for this effect, but it is generally agreed that low-3frequency words require more time and/or effort for the word recognition system (Taylor et al. 42013; Gagl et al. 2022). If the VWFA acts as the “orthographic lexicon,” high-frequency words 5evoke lower responses there because of more rapid lexical access (Kronbichler et al., 2004; 6Wimmer & Ludersdorfer, 2018). Another explanation proposes that low-frequency words evoke 7larger VWFA responses because they are more difficult to distinguish from non-words, and again, 8that difficulty increases the total response (Gagl et al. 2022). Alternatively, low-frequency words 9elicit stronger “prediction errors” from higher-level language areas that feed back to the VWFA 10 (Price & Devlin, 2011). All of these models attribute the lexical frequency effect to a component 11 of the word recognition process that goes beyond simply detecting letters strings. We therefore 12 analyze the magnitude of the lexical frequency effect to compare the degree to which such lexical 13 processes were engaged in the simultaneous and sequential presentation conditions. This mirrors 14 an approach taken by White et al. (2019), who reported that VWFA-2 (i.e., mOTS-words) was 15 sensitive to the lexical frequency of only one attended word at a time. 16 We separately analyzed two sets of two-word trials: those in which both words were of low 17 frequency (below the median of 4.1) and those in which both words were of high frequency (above 18 the median). The results are plotted in Figure 5 , for behavioral accuracy, response times and 19 BOLD responses in left ventral text-selective areas. ## Serial and limited-capacity parallel models inspired by the lexical frequency effects 7

We now propose two simple models of how activity in the ventral text-selective regions 8could produce the pattern of lexical frequency effects. One model assumes rapid serial processing 9of words, and the other assumes limited-capacity parallel processing. 10 These models predict the magnitude of BOLD responses to pairs of words in the mFus-11 words region, where we find the strongest lexical frequency effects. See Figure 6. The core idea 12 is that each word is processed until the accumulated “evidence” or “activation” reaches a 13 threshold, or until the word(s) is replaced by another stimulus frame that interrupts processing. 14 The total BOLD response is the integral of the activation over time for all words processed in that 15 trial. This approach was inspired by Taylor, Rastle & Davis (2013). To account for the overall 16 higher response to low- than high-frequency words, we assume that the evidence threshold is 17 higher for low-frequency words. Thus, it takes more time to finish processing a low-frequency 18 word, and the summed amount of activation is higher. This is roughly consistent with Bayesian 19 accounts of word recognition (e.g., Norris, 2013) and previous proposals that low-frequency words 20 are processed for more time or with more effort in the VWFA (Gagl et al. 2022; Kronbichler et al. 21 2004; Taylor et al. 2013). The free parameters in this model are: the slope of evidence 22 accumulation over time, the low-frequency word threshold, and the high-frequency word 23 threshold. The Python code that generates the model predictions is available along with our 24 analysis code in the public repository for this study: https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/VXD9Z. 25 

The serial model ( Figure 6A) assumes that mFus-words processes one word at a time. If 26 one word’s activation reaches its threshold before the next stimulus frame arrives, then 27 processing of the second word begins immediately. In sequential trials ( Figure 6A , upper two 28 panels), one word is presented per frame and is fully processed (activation reaches the threshold). 29 The area under the activation curve is larger for low- than high-frequency words (in Figure 6A ,30 bottom right panel, compare the empty blue bar with the filled blue bar). In simultaneous trials 31 (Figure 6A , bottom left panel), we assume that after processing one word, there is still time to 32 switch to begin processing the other word, but only partially. When both words are low frequency, 33

DISCUSSION 7

We first established that in ventral temporal text-selective regions, and in frontal and 8temporal language-processing regions, BOLD response magnitudes steadily increased with the 9number of words presented, from 0 to 1 to 2. Behaviorally, participants were less likely to detect 10 both of two words that were presented simultaneously than sequentially, suggesting a processing 11 capacity limit (Scharf et al., 2011; White et al., 2020; White et al., 2026). The neural correlate of 12 that capacity limit could have presented as a weaker BOLD response to two words presented 13 simultaneously as compared to sequentially, as occurs for other types of stimuli in visual cortex 14 (Kaiser et al., 2014; Kastner, Weerd, et al., 2001; Miller et al., 1993; Shim et al., 2013). However, 15 we did not observe statistically significant simultaneous suppression in any of our regions of 16 interest nor in a whole-cortex analysis. These data demonstrate that text-selective regions can 17 respond to two written words simultaneously. 18 However, there is a distinction between simply detecting two letter strings and identifying 19 them as specific words in the mental lexicon. We also found that in ventral temporal text-selective 20 areas, the response was higher for two low-frequency words than two high-frequency words, but 21 less so in the simultaneous condition. The statistical evidence in this analysis was modest and 22 must be followed up with further research. Our preliminary interpretation is that lexical access was 23 impaired by the simultaneous presentation, resulting in less sensitivity to the words’ frequencies. 24 We proposed two simple models to account for those effects, either assuming serial processing 25 or parallel processing with interference ( Figure 6 ). 26 We now consider four factors of our empirical design that may explain the absence of a 27 neural simultaneous suppression effect: 28 1. Visual Stimulation: We equated the numbers of characters and stimulus onsets and 29 offsets across conditions. Had we not done so, but simply presented a variable number of words 30 without the ‘filler’ false font strings, we may have found a simultaneous suppression effect. 31

36 However, such an effect could be due to nonlinear temporal integration of responses to stimulus 1transients, rather than competition for limited resources (Kupers et al. 2024). 22. Stimulus Duration: Each word was presented for 183 ms, which is short enough to 3minimize goal-directed eye movements within a frame. The interstimulus interval was 233 ms, 4roughly the average fixation duration during reading (Rayner et al., 2016). This interval is also not 5far from the 250 ms used in classic simultaneous suppression studies (e.g., Kastner et al., 1998; 6Kastner & Ungerleider, 2001; McMains & Kastner, 2011). However, there is some evidence that 7the effect increases as stimulus durations increase (Kupers et al., 2024). We chose not to make 8the time between frames any longer because that could invite eye movements. But we cannot 9definitively rule out shifts of covert attention between words within a single frame (see Figure 6A ). 10 If subjects attempted that strategy, however, they were not entirely successful, because task 11 accuracy was impaired on simultaneous trials. Nonetheless, we might have found stronger 12 simultaneous suppression had the presentation durations been even shorter, to prevent (partial) 13 sequential processing of simultaneously presented words. 14 3. Stimulus Location: Unlike many past studies (e.g., Kastner et al. 1999; Kupers et al. 15 2024), we presented just two stimuli, each 1.5º from fixation. Both were inside the large population 16 receptive fields of voxels in the ventral temporal cortex that cover roughly the central 5º of the 17 visual field (Le et al., 2017). Therefore, we expected a strong effect in ventral temporal cortex 18 (Kastner, De Weerd, et al., 2001; Kupers et al., 2024). To minimize the distance between words, 19 we presented them above and below fixation. If anything, we would be less likely to find 20 simultaneous suppression with the words presented to the left and right of fixation, which would 21 be more familiar to English readers. 22 4. Top-down attention: While early studies found the strongest simultaneous suppression 23 effect when the stimuli are ignored (Kastner et al., 1998; S. McMains & Kastner, 2011), it is also 24 present when the stimuli are attended (Scalf et al., 2011). As our question is whether two words 25 can be read simultaneously, we made our stimuli task-relevant. Voluntary effort to read amplifies 26 activity in the VWFA, possibly via top-down signals from language regions (Chauhan et al., 2024; 27 Kay & Yeatman, 2017; Mano et al., 2013; White et al., 2023). Thus, it is possible top-down signals 28 counteracted a local simultaneous suppression effect that we might have observed if the stimuli 29 were ignored. Conversely, an effect might have emerged had the task required further semantic 30 processing. This concern is mitigated by two findings: there was a behavioral deficit in the 31 simultaneous condition, and higher-level language regions were engaged and sensitive to the 32 number of words. 33

Comparison to a prior report of a serial bottleneck in the VWFA 1

White et al. (2019) measured BOLD responses to two words while participants performed 2a semantic categorization task and either focused attention on one word or divided attention 3between both. They concluded that the “serial bottleneck” apparent in task performance arises in 4mOTS-words (i.e., VWFA-2). 5On the one hand, these findings led us to predict a simultaneous suppression effect in 6text-selective areas, which we did not find. Note that White et al. (2019) displayed words to the 7left and right of fixation and found a bias: the word on the right was easier to recognize and 8dominated VWFA activity. Our display arrangement avoided that complication, perhaps revealing 9more balanced processing of two words at once. On the other hand, our analyses of lexical 10 frequency are consistent with White et al. (2019) in suggesting that the VWFA cannot process 11 two words at once as well as it can process one word at a time. 12 

A hybrid parallel-then-serial model to account for behavioral and neural effects 13 

To account for all the fMRI and behavioral data in this study, and to be consistent with 14 several previous lines of research, we propose a hybrid model: sublexical information from two 15 words is processed in parallel in word-selective cortex, followed by a serial process of identifying 16 each word as a lexical item ( Figure 7 ). The parallel stage is consistent with an existing model of 17 reading that proposes that multiple words are processed in parallel at the stage of activating letter 18 or bigram detectors (Grainger et al., 2014, 2016; Snell et al., 2018; Snell & Grainger, 2019). 19 Orthographic information from different spatial locations is pooled into a single “channel” that 20 activates multiple candidate word representations, which inhibit each other. 21 Building on those ideas, Anupindi et al. (2026) proposed a hybrid parallel-serial interactive 22 activation model to account for behavioral data in tasks with attention divided between two words. 23 They found large deficits for recognizing two unrelated words at once (one above and one below 24 fixation), consistent with the serial model. But when two words formed a compound word, 25 performance exceeded the serial model. This suggests some degree of parallel processing that 26 can maintain information about one word while another is identified. 27

However, 7further lexical processing occurs serially, perhaps relying on communication with higher-level 8language regions. The fact that processing of one 12 word often does not complete causes the task performance deficits on simultaneous trials. 13 However, both words contribute equal amounts of activation (the areas under the red and blue 14 curves are equal), meaning that the total BOLD response is equivalent to when two words are 15 presented sequentially. 16 

Future directions 17 

An important question for future research is about when BOLD responses in the text-18 selective regions asymptote as the number of words increase. And is that threshold the same for 19 all regions? The hybrid model we present raises the question of which brain regions participate in 20 the transition from parallel sublexical processing to the selection of one word to be identified 21 serially. It will be important to build upon this study with tasks that are more similar to natural 22 reading in a variety of languages. For instance, the capacity for parallel processing might vary as 23 a function of where the words are positioned, that that might differ across readers of scripts that 24 are arranged vertically or horizontally. Building towards natural reading, our results also present 25 avenues for exploring how responses to multiple words depend on their linguistic relationships: 26 such as pairs of words that are semantically related or form a compound or a syntactic phrase. 27
