Abstract 12As is true in humans, no two rodents prefer precisely the same tastes. Furthermore, no 3one rodent keeps precisely the same taste preferences forever. Here, we have made 4use of this between- and within-animal variability to generate and test novel hypotheses 5about the stability and malleability of neural perceptual coding. We used a brief-access 6task (BAT) to reveal both individual differences in taste preferences and shifting 7preferences within individual rats (quantified in terms of lick bout lengths). We moved on 8to show that these phenomena are not simply random variation: first, by demonstrating 9that gustatory cortical (GC) taste response dynamics, the late part of which reflect 10 palatability, match that individual rat’s BAT preferences (evaluated almost 2 weeks 11 prior) better than canonical preference patterns; that is, individual differences in 12 preferences reflect differences in neural taste processing. This match, however, links 13 neural taste processing only to the most recent BAT session—GC palatability 14 processing does not reflect performance in earlier BAT sessions. An implication of the 15 fact that rats’ perceptual preferences shift across BAT sessions is that any tasting 16 experience might impact taste processing, an implication that we tested by performing a 17 second session of GC taste-response recordings—sessions in which tastes were 18 delivered by intra-oral cannula to passive rats—and found that palatability-epoch taste 19 responses in this later session no longer matched the most recent pre-recording BAT 20 session. Together, these data demonstrate that every tasting experience (regardless of 21 the method of taste delivery) changes the rat’s processing of those tastes. 22

Introduction 23 At the most basic level, the task facing an animal’s perceptual systems is to 24 process input such that the animal can generate appropriate behavioral output. This 25 task is of particular importance when said input is gustatory, because taste-guided 26 consumption decisions are made when a potentially dangerous substance is already in 27 the mouth. Palatability (the hedonic value associated with a taste) is the primary 28 variable informing the selection of responses (swallowing or rejection) to such stimuli, 29 and it is therefore unsurprising that a sweet (caloric) taste would be ubiquitously 30 preferred, and a bitter (potentially toxic) taste ubiquitously shunned. 31 But despite the inescapability of the above logic, individual differences in 32 palatability judgments abound. It is a given, in fact, that different human animals have 33 different specific likes and dislikes when it comes to food; for any particular taste and 34 flavor, there can be found people who love it and people who loathe it. And this is not 35 just true for humans: rat taste preferences vary from strain to strain (Bachmanov et al., 36 1998; Grill & Bernstein, 1988) and even from individual to individual (Bacharach & Calu, 37 2019; Inui-Yamamoto et al., 2017; Kotlus & Blizard, 1998; Loney et al., 2012). While 38 part of the explanation for these individual differences is no doubt genetic (Armitage et 39 al., 2025; Bachmanov et al., 2016; Blizard, 1999; Keskitalo et al., 2007), a good deal of 40 evidence supports the hypothesis that eating history controls individual taste 41 preferences. Culture, for instance, drives some of the specifics of food preferences 42 (Jamel, 1996), and simple experience—both social and non-social—is known to impact 43 taste likes and dislikes (Herman & Higgs, 2015; Robinson & Higgs, 2012; Story et al., 44 2002). Social interactions can change taste palatability for rodents as well (Galef & 45 Wigmore, 1983; Posadas-Andrews & Roper, 1983), and even innocuous experience 46 has an impact on rat taste coding that appears to change preferences (Flores et al., 47 2016; Flores et al., 2018). 48 The above work motivates the suggestion that neural taste processing should 49 differ from animal to animal, and even from day to day within single animals, in concert 50 with differences in preference patterns. But there has been very little work done on the 51 relationship between individual differences and neural perceptual responses, and 52 essentially none looking at the stability of this relationship through time. Here we 53 perform this work, repeatedly evaluating rats’ taste preferences using a brief-access 54 task (BAT) that allows rigorous estimates of between-taste preference patterns in a 55 single session, and comparing these behavioral data to assays of the rats’ gustatory 56 cortical (GC) responses to the same tastes—responses that have been reliably and 57 repeatedly shown to reflect palatability in the response epoch spanning the period 58 between ~0.5s and ~1.5s following taste delivery (Katz et al., 2001; Mukherjee et al., 59 2019; Sadacca et al., 2016). 60 Individual rats’ BAT preferences proved to be a specific match for those rats’ 61 “Late-epoch” GC responses, despite: 1) 2-week “empty stretches” between collection of 62 behavioral and electrophysiological data; 2) a range of sampled taste batteries; and 3) 63 vast differences in taste administration methods used in the two types of sessions 64 (licking in the BAT rig; passive intra-oral cannula delivery in electrophysiology sessions). 65 But this match was delicate, such that even single tasting experiences destroyed it— 66 palatability-related GC responses matched only the most recent BAT preference 67 assessment, not assessments from earlier days, and a second test of the rats’ GC taste 68

Experimental Overview 79 To measure each rat’s taste preferences, we analyzed licking patterns from Brief-80 Access Task (BAT) data. The BAT provides a rapid and reliable assessment of 81 preferences across an entire taste battery; in particular, analysis of lick microstructure— 82 most notably, calculation of the average size of lick clusters in 10-sec trials—has been 83 shown to be an accurate assessment of the palatability of a proffered taste (Dwyer, 84 2009; Lin et al., 2012; Lin et al., 2026; Strickland et al., 2018), with smaller average 85 cluster sizes reliably (but see below) equating to lower palatability. Comparison of 86 cluster sizes therefore provided a rigorous, accurate picture of which tastes an animal 87 likes more, and which tastes it likes less ( Figure 1A ). After 3–5 sessions of BAT testing, 88 rats were prepared for subsequent electrophysiology recordings through the 89 implantation of an intra-oral cannula (IOC) and a 32-channel electrode into the primary 90 gustatory cortex (GC; Figure 1B ). Following recovery, rats received passive taste 91 deliveries via the IOC while GC activity was recorded over two sessions. Following 92 histological examination (Figure 1C ), only data from rats with accurate electrode 93 placements were included in the final analysis. Figure 1D summarizes when each 94 phase of the experiment occurred with a period of approximately 11 days 95 (encompassing surgical recovery and rig habituation) elapsing between the final BAT 96 session and the first electrophysiology recording. 97

Discussion 315 The fact that our BAT data revealed that individual rats have distinct taste 316 preference patterns (i.e., their own ideas of what tastes better and worse) is perhaps not 317 surprising, although the magnitude of those individual differences is almost certainly 318 larger than many would have predicted (particularly given the homogeneity of our rats’ 319 genetics and environment). More surprising is our finding that, even with a surgery, 320 almost 2 weeks of recovery, and a major change in stimulus delivery method separating 321 the BAT and electrophysiological assays, individual behavioral differences are reflected 322 in (“late-epoch”) GC palatability-related spiking. Most surprising, however, is the fact 323 that the stability of perception is upset by taste experience—whether lick-acquired or 324 IOC-delivered, taste delivery had an impact on taste processing. Our results therefore 325 suggest that every new tasting experience changes what a rat thinks of a taste in its 326 mouth; at least as far as gustation is concerned, Heraclitus was correct that “no [animal] 327 ever steps in the same river twice.” 328 These results validate the use of a brief-access task (BAT), and more specifically 329 lick cluster size analysis, to measure a rodent’s palatability judgments, suggesting that 330 the apparent individual and between-session variability that these tests reveal is bona 331 

fide . The significantly enhanced match between a specific rat’s measured preferences 332 and their GC late-epoch neural responses (compared to canonical average preference 333 rankings) makes it clear that between-rat variability in BAT performance is not noise but 334 signal—each animal is a genuine individual. An implication of this fact is that any 335 publication of consumption averages calculated from BAT data should always have 336 substantial confidence intervals around them. Furthermore, this brain-behavior match 337 provides convergent evidence that the late-epoch GC response, which both predicts 338 and drives the onset of consumption and rejection (Baas-Thomas et al., 2025; Li et al., 339 2016; Mukherjee et al., 2019; Sadacca et al., 2016), is deeply relevant to behavior; the 340 transition to palatability-related ensemble firing reflects the reaching of a consumption 341 decision, made on the basis of neural processing that is to some degree specific to the 342 animal and session. 343 This processing turns out to be remarkably stable (note that this means that the 344 relationships between responses are stable—it does not necessarily mean that the 345 precise responses are themselves unchanging) across days and weeks in which no 346 specific experience with the basic tastes is provided. We performed electrode 347 implantation surgeries after the final BAT preference testing session and gave each rat 348 approximately 11 days to recover prior to the beginning of adaptation to the 349 electrophysiology acquisition setup, such that 2 weeks separated behavioral and neural 350 evaluations of taste palatability. Despite this separation, brain-behavior correlations 351 reflected the individual rat’s BAT performance, a relationship that persisted despite the 352 fact that BAT and electrophysiology sessions differed with regard to: 1) whether the rat 353 was engaged in active sensing (BAT) or passive acceptance (electrophysiology); 2) 354 whether the taste was licked (BAT) or delivered directly into the mouth via IOC 355 (electrophysiology); and 3) whether the tastant accumulated in the mouth bit by bit 356 (BAT) or in a single aliquot (electrophysiology). In essence, our experiments constituted 357 a highly conservative test, in that each of the variables discussed above (including the 358 2-week between-session interval) would be expected to reduce the brain-behavior 359

Rats showed robust changes in their preferences across multiple BAT 366 sessions, and only the preference pattern from the final BAT session matched the 367 palatability-related GC firing recorded during the subsequent IOC session. These 368 results suggest that taste preferences are not fixed properties but are instead updated 369 following each taste experience. 370 Furthermore, it is clear that even delivering tastes via IOC to passively tasting 371 rats has an impact on taste processing, in that the alignment between recorded GC 372 activity and previously estimated preferences is lost in a 2 nd passive tasting session. We 373 would argue that the most parsimonious interpretation of these results is that even 374 passive taste exposure via IOC is sufficient to modify a rat’s preference structure, 375 although this was not directly tested here. Fully supporting this conclusion will require a 376 future study in which we incorporate an additional BAT session immediately after the 377 first recording session to test whether this most recent estimate of taste preference best 378 predicts subsequently recorded GC activity. 379 It is already well known that a first experience with a particular taste can increase 380 the palatability of that taste—a phenomenon known as the attenuation of neophobia (Lin 381 et al., 2012; Menchén-Márquez et al., 2026)—and change the processing of other 382 tastes (Flores et al., 2016, 2022). However, the phenomenon characterized in this 383 current dataset is almost certainly not related to neophobia, in that: 1) the changes 384 observed here occur with each new session, even when the tastes can no longer be 385 thought of as novel; and 2) our analyses demonstrate that these session-to-session 386 changes cannot be described simply as an increase in each taste’s palatability (also, 387 had the palatabilities of all tastes increased together, preference ranks would have 388 remained unchanged). Rather, our data suggest that aspects of taste processing that 389 can otherwise remain stable across at least a 2-week period are altered by each and 390 any taste experience, even after many taste experiences have already taken place. 391 Clearly, the specifics of taste processing are sensitive to external factors—tastes 392 must be presented to change taste preference structure—but it seems that a range of 393 types of experiences, in which the rat is offered any of a number of different possible 394 taste batteries, are sufficient to evince such changes. It’s worth noting that our rats were 395 housed and tested in a controlled environment (home cages and behavioral rigs); this 396 fact, and the seeming randomness of changes occurring with tasting experience (see 397 Figure 3D), makes it reasonable to conclude that the individual differences observed 398 here are likely driven primarily, if not entirely, by idiosyncratic internal factors such as 399 genetic and/or developmental variability, or perhaps lasting impacts of early-life taste 400 experience (see Schiff et al., 2023). Materials and Methods 416 

Subjects 417 Adult female Long Evans rats (N = 9; 250-375g at time of surgery) were acquired 418 from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA), singly-housed in independently-419 ventilated cages on a 12h/12h light/dark schedule, and acclimated to the facility and 420 handling for 1 week before the start of experimental procedures. Unless otherwise 421 specified, animals in home cages had ad libitum access to lab chow and water except 422 where noted. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines 423 established by the Brandeis University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. 424 425 

Brief Access Task (BAT) Preference Testing 426 The BAT rig (Davis MS-160 Lickometer apparatus, Med-Associates Instruments) 427 assessed individual preferences for each taste in the battery. Before preference testing, 428 rats underwent three days of 45-minute habituation to the experimental chamber, after 429 which they were placed on water restriction and given two days of licking training—60 430 10s-trials in which water was available via a metal lick spout attached to glass bottles. 431 For the preference test assessment, rats were presented with a battery of tastes 432 (in each rat, one of 3 different possible batteries of tastes were used to ensure that 433 results were generalizable—see Table 1 ; as no differences in effects were observed 434 between taste batteries, data was collapsed in our analyses) that spanned a putative 435 range of palatabilities. One taste was offered per trial, in which rats were given 60s 436 maximum to instigate licking, a maximum access time of 10s following the first lick, and 437 a 30s inter-trial interval between trials. Tastes were presented in a pseudo-randomized 438 order (i.e., blocking design), such that a single tastant cannot be offered more than two 439 successive trials (which would adversely impact willingness to participate). Each tastant 440 was presented 8-10 times, depending on the specific taste battery. BAT testing 441 persisted for 3 (taste set 1 & 2) or 5 (set 3) consecutive days. 442 Tastes, which were dissolved in deionized water (Millipore Milli-Q water 443 purification system), were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific at ACS Grade (with 444 the exception of saccharin, which was FCC/USP Grade). A fan above the rig served to 445 reduce any potential odor cues that might have accompanied taste presentation. The 446 time of each lick was recorded for off-line preference analysis. All experiments were run 447 between 10:00-16:00. 448 449 

Surgical implantation of GC electrode and Intra-Oral Cannula 450 After the final BAT session, animals were removed from water deprivation and 451 allowed to recover to full, pre-restriction weight for implantation surgery. Rats were 452 anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 453 mg/kg), and craniotomies were made to expose GC. The drivable electrode, consisting 454 of a bundle of 32 nichrome wires (25 μm in diameter), was implanted just above GC 455 (A/P = +1.4, M/L = ±5.0, D/V = -4.4), and secured in place with dental acrylic. Rats also 456 received an intra-oral cannula (IOC)—a polyethylene cannula inserted behind the 457 maxillary molars, through the left masseter muscle and through the opening of the 458 scalp. 459 Rats recovered for 7 days; during the first day, meloxicam (Alloxate, 5mg/ml, 460 Patterson) (1mg/kg) was injected for pain management. 461

Electrophysiological recording with passive tastant delivery 463 After recovery from surgery, rats habituated for 2 days (30-minute sessions) to 464 the electrophysiological recording rig (plugged into their electrode harness in a465 plexiglass experimental chamber measuring 8.5 x 9.5 x 11.5 in), after which they were 466 placed on water restriction in preparation for recording experiments. Experiments began 467 with two days of habituation to liquids delivered through the IOC, during which 60 (day 468 1) and 120 (day 2) trials of water were delivered with inter-trial intervals of 22s. Twenty-469 four hours after, each rat received two consecutive recording days where 30 470 randomized presentations of each tastant (a total of four tastes selected from the same 471 taste set received during the BAT preference testing; Table 1 ) were delivered. 472 Delivery of each taste was individually controlled by solenoid valves, pressurized 473 by inert nitrogen. To ensure our ability to deliver precise amounts of fluid onto the 474 tongue of each IOC delivery, we calibrated the rig to deliver 30 microliters in each 475 aliquot at the beginning of each recording session, controlling the open time of valves 476 

via a Raspberry-Pi microcomputer. 477 Spiking data were collected via Intan RHD2132 analog-to-digital chip amplifier. 478 Recordings were taken at a sampling rate of 30 kHz. Electrodes were driven down 479 (~0.25mm) 24hr prior to each recording session to capture different populations of GC 480 neurons for each session. The entire recording rig was enclosed in a faraday cage to 481 reduce latent electromagnetic interference during recordings. 482 483 

Electrophysiology data processing 484 Bandpass filtering and common average referencing were used to clean data 485 and increase signal quality. Discriminable action potentials of no less than 3:1 signal-to-486 noise ratio were isolated online from each signal and saved digitally. Prior to analysis of 487 taste-related activity, cells were clustered using 3-D cutting techniques alongside 488 supervised verification of inter-spike interval plots. All spikes included in analysis had at 489 least 2000 waveforms and met our criteria of < 0.5% 1ms violations and <2% 2ms inter-490 spike interval violations. For a comprehensive explanation of the protocol used for spike 491 sorting, refer to Mukherjee et al. (2017). By driving down the mini-microdrive between 492 sessions, new cells were captured each day, which was verified by comparing 493 waveform shapes. 494 Semi-processed spiking arrays were then exported to an analysis framework built 495 in python. After processing, each 7000ms (2s pre-stimulus, 5s post-stimulus) recording 496 window was binned using a sliding window average with a window size of 250ms and a 497 step size of 25ms. 498 499

Libraries 502 integral to the analysis included: pandas, numpy, pingouin, matplotlib, and seaborn. 503 Several lick parameters were measured for each tastant presentation; we used the 504 inter-lick interval (ILI; ~125msec) to determine lick cluster size—the number of licks 505 produced prior to a 500ms pause ( Figure 1A ). Individual taste preferences were 506 estimated using averaged lick cluster sizes obtained from a BAT session. We used lick 507

Furthermore, we verified that, 509 similar to average licks per trial, lick cluster size derived from brief licking trials (e.g., 10-510 s BAT trials in the current experiments) yields palatability estimates that are comparable 511 to—and less noisy than—those obtained from the more traditional 15-min ad libitum 512 licking sessions (Lin, et al., 2026). 513 A similar result was observed when using the first lick cluster, rather than 514 averaging clusters within a trial, to estimate taste palatability. Palatability correlation 519 The method of calculating correlation between GC activity and palatability has 520 been described previously (Katz et al., 2001; Sadacca et al., 2016): a vector of firing 521 rates in each taste response at each 25-ms timepoint in a moving average is Spearman 522 correlated (to control for nonhomogeneity) with the vector of preferences. The canonical 523 rankings for taste batteries are defined and labeled in Table 1 from most to least 524 palatable based on our BAT experiments and those of others (Sadacca et al., 2012; 525 Spector et al., 1993), which have shown that 0.1 M NaCl is more palatable than 0.05 M 526 NaCl. To incorporate individual preferences here, we also correlated neural activity to 527 animal-matched mean cluster values in the first or most recent BAT session (First BAT, 528 Final BAT). 529 530 

Taste responsiveness and neural excitability 531 Firing rates were z-scored to the pre-stimulus period by neuron and the mean 532 z-scored firing rate was extracted for each epoch (500ms, baseline: -500:0ms) and 533 compared using a mixed two-way ANOVA (Session × Epoch). 534 Neurons were categorized as taste responsive if the evoked response (0-2000ms 535 post-stimulus delivery) significantly deviated from pre-stimulus baseline firing (-2000ms-536 0ms) based on a paired t-test. To qualify as palatability-related, Spearman correlation 537 values to the Final BAT session values had to achieve significance ( p < 0.01) for ≥3538 consecutive bins within the palatability epoch. The proportion of palatability-related 539 neurons per session was compared using χ² contingency tests. 540 To quantify absolute changes in evoked responses across sessions for each 541 taste, we computed the absolute difference in mean evoked firing (0–2000ms) for each 542 taste between Session 1 and Session 2 and reported as the percent change. These 543 values were compared using a one-way ANOVA across tastes. Significance was set at 544 alpha < 0.5 unless indicated otherwise. 545 546
