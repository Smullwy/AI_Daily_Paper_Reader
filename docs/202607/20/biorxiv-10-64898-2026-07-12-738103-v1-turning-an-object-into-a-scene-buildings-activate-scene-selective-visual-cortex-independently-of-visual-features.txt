Abstract 33 

Human visual cortex contains regions that selectively respond to both scenes and large 34 

objects, particularly buildings. The cortical overlap between buildings and scenes has 35 

been attributed to shared visual features (e.g., cardinal orientations, rectilinearity). 36 

Alternative accounts propose that buildings may also activate scene representations 37 

indirectly, independently of specific visual features, for example because buildings evoke 38 

a sense of space. Here, we tested for such feature-independent activation by comparing 39 

EEG and fMRI responses in human participants (both sexes) to buildings and visually-40 

matched boxes, and relating these responses to scene-selective responses. Buildings 41 

and boxes were matched, across exemplars, using image-based metrics, deep neural 42 

networks, and a perceptual similarity task. Time-resolved EEG decoding showed that 43 

buildings and boxes evoked discriminable responses from 360ms post-stimulus onset, 44 

incompatible with feedforward visual feature processing. Importantly, the building-box 45 

classifier generalized to discriminate scenes from chairs, providing EEG evidence for a 46 

representational overlap between buildings and scenes. Temporal generalization 47 

analyses further showed that the late building-selective response corresponded to an 48 

earlier scene-selective response, with a temporal offset of ~130ms. Finally, ultra-fast fMRI 49 

(TR=140ms) revealed that these findings were mirrored in the response of the scene-50 

selective parahippocampal place area (PPA), which similarly showed a feature-51 

independent building-selective response that was delayed and prolonged relative to the 52 

scene-selective response. These results clarify the nature of building selectivity in visual 53 

cortex by showing that this selectivity can arise independently of visual features, 54 

putatively reflecting associative processes between buildings and scenes (or space). 55

Significance Statement: 56 

The human ventral stream contains regions responding selectively to behaviorally-57 

relevant categories such as places, faces, and words. Contrasting views attribute this 58 

selectivity either to the feedforward processing of visual features or to top-down input from 59 

domain-specific networks. Here, we show that neural selectivity for buildings persists in 60 

the absence of category-diagnostic visual features. EEG decoding showed that this high-61 

level building response emerged late during object processing and corresponded to an 62 

earlier scene-selective response. Ultra-fast fMRI revealed that these findings were 63 

mirrored in the response of the scene-selective parahippocampal place area (PPA). 64 

These results show that selectivity for some categories can be decoupled from visual 65 

feature processing, reflecting associative processes that follow object recognition. 66

Introduction 69 

Human visual cortex shows a categorical organization, with spatio-temporal response 70 

patterns evoked by pictures of objects organized along dimensions of animacy and real-71 

world size (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008; Konkle and Oliva, 2012; Carlson et al., 2013), and 72 

with focal selectivity for more specific categories of stimuli, including scenes, faces, 73 

bodies, words, and tools (Downing et al., 2006). This organization has been widely 74 

replicated using multiple methods, including fMRI and M/EEG, but the driving factors 75 

are still debated (Haxby et al., 2001; Op de Beeck et al., 2008; Peelen and Downing, 76 

2017; Ritchie et al., 2026). 77 

One of the first reports of category selectivity using fMRI, nearly three decades ago, 78 

was for buildings (Aguirre et al., 1998), in a region that overlaps scene-selective cortex 79 

(Epstein and Kanwisher, 1998). More recently, studies have shown that this selectivity 80 

extends to large/stable objects more generally (e.g., furniture; Mullally and Maguire, 81 

2011; Konkle and Oliva, 2012; He et al., 2013; Troiani et al., 2014). Here, we used EEG 82 

and fMRI to investigate what drives this building selectivity, and what could explain its 83 

representational overlap with scene selectivity. 84 

One possibility is that the selective response to buildings (and presumably large 85 

objects more generally) reflects the feedforward processing of visual features typical of 86 

such objects (e.g., rectilinear features, cardinal orientations), which may be shared with 87 

scenes (Nasr and Tootell, 2012; Nasr et al., 2014). For example, fMRI studies have 88 

shown that large-object-selective responses remain when stimuli are made 89 

unrecognizable while preserving low- and mid-level features, suggesting that this 90 

selectivity reflects the feedforward processing of such features (Long et al., 2018). 91

These findings were confirmed by studies using time-sensitive M/EEG decoding, 92 

showing that large and small objects evoke distinct response pattern as early as 120 ms 93 

after stimulus onset (Khaligh-Razavi et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022), even when made 94 

unrecognizable (Wang et al., 2022). This early time course suggests that large-object 95 

selectivity at least partly reflects visual feature processing. 96 

Other studies, however, have observed selectivity for buildings and large objects 97 

when participants merely imagined such objects (Ishai et al., 2000; Mullally and 98 

Maguire, 2011; Boccia et al., 2021), raising the possibility that this selectivity could 99 

reflect a relatively abstract (i.e., non-visual) representation of space, context, or 100 

navigation-related cognitive processes. Specifically, Mullally and Maguire (2011) 101 

proposed that buildings and large/stable objects evoke a sense of space and that neural 102 

selectivity to buildings reflects this sense of space. Such an account naturally explains 103 

the overlap between building and scene selectivity. 104 

In the present study, we aimed to shed light on the nature of building selectivity and 105 

its relation to scene selectivity. To do so, we developed a carefully controlled stimulus 106 

set consisting of buildings and visually-matched controls (boxes), and used EEG and 107 

fMRI to test: 1) whether neural selectivity to buildings can be observed when buildings 108 

are contrasted with visually-matched non-building objects (boxes); and 2) whether these 109 

building-selective responses correspond to scene-selective responses. As a preview of 110 

the results, in both EEG and fMRI experiments, we observed a clear building-selective 111 

response after equating perceptual similarity and controlling for low- and mid-level 112 

visual features across categories. In EEG, this response emerged relatively late, from 113 

around 360 ms after stimulus onset, ruling out that it was driven by the feedforward 114

Behavioral study 124 

The behavioral study used an oddball visual search task (Arun, 2012) that provides a 125

in the neuroimaging experiments. 127

Stimuli. Stimuli consisted of 8 images of buildings and 8 images of boxes (Figure 1A). 137 

Notably, images of buildings and boxes were carefully selected so that each building 138 

exemplar was matched to a box exemplar in terms of overall contour shape and viewing 139 

angle. These images were resized to the same size and fine-tuned in Photoshop to 140 

minimize any contour shape and perspective differences. This process aimed to 141 

eliminate potential low- and mid-level visual feature differences between buildings and 142 

boxes while preserving their diagnostic visual features (e.g., windows/doors for 143 

buildings and hinged lids and locks for boxes). An independent online pilot study (N = 144 

40) further ensured the images of buildings and boxes were highly recognizable when 145 

viewed one by one (as in the neuroimaging experiments). 146

Experimental design. The experiment was programmed using JsPsych 6.1.0 (de 159 

Leeuw, 2015) and presented online through the hosting service provided by Pavlovia 160 

(https://pavlovia.org/, Open Science Tools, Nottingham, UK). In each trial, following an 161 

initial 700 ms fixation period, participants viewed an array of spatially arranged objects. 162 

These objects were organized into 4 rows and 2 columns on each side of the monitor, 163 

separated by a vertical straight line at the central position (Figure 1B). Each array 164

“left”) and M (for “right”) as quickly as possible. Participants’ response ended the search 171 

array and initiated the next trial. With 8 objects in each of the two categories, any unique 172 

“target-distractor” assignment resulted in a total of 240 combinations (i.e., 16 ✕ 15). 173

Data analysis. For each unique combination of two objects, we calculated the average 182 

reaction time (RT) across the two reversed “target-distractor” assignments and the 183 

left/right appearances of the target in each assignment. The inverse of the RT (1/RT) 184 

served as a metric to reflect the perceptual distance between the two specific objects 185 

(Arun, 2012). This approach allowed us to measure the perceptual distance between 186 

pairs of objects for each participant, resulting in a symmetric perceptual distance matrix. 187 

To enhance the robustness of our findings, this matrix was then averaged across all the 188 

participants. 189 

190 

Statistical testing. We categorized the unique two-object combinations into three 191 

groups: visually matched different-category objects (building-box), unmatched different-192 

category objects (building-box), and unmatched same-category objects (building-193 

building or box-box), and then compared the perceptual distances among these groups. 194 

A non-parametric testing approach was employed, involving the shuffling of the 195 

perceptual distance matrix values 10,000 times while maintaining its symmetry and 196

Stimuli. Stimuli consisted of 56 individual colored object (and scene) images taken from 220 

7 basic-level categories (buildings, boxes, scenes, chairs, tools, instruments, and 221 

hands). Images of tools, instruments and hands were included for another experiment 222 

with a different purpose. Each category contained 8 exemplars. Notably, images of 223 

buildings and boxes were the same images as those used in the behavioral experiment. 224 

The images of buildings and boxes were further superimposed on a phase-scrambled 225 

background to equate the high spatial frequency (high-SF) component (defined as 5 226 

cycles/degree, as in (Rajimehr et al., 2011). Luminance was also matched for buildings 227 

and boxes using the SHINE color toolbox (Dal Ben, 2023). Examination of image-level 228 

visual property metrics (such as rectilinearity, as in Nasr et al., 2014), activations from 229 

the layers of a deep neural network (AlexNet, Krizhevsky et al., 2012), and comparison 230 

of mid-level visual features from a texture statistic model (P-S model, Portilla and 231 

Simoncelli, 2000; Henderson et al., 2023) confirmed that visual features were overall 232 

matched between buildings and boxes (Supplementary Figure 1, 2). Colored images of 233 

other categories were similarly resized and cropped to maintain a consistent retinal size 234 

with buildings/boxes and superimposed on a phase-scrambled background. Scenes 235 

images included 4 man-made scenes and 4 natural scenes, specifically selected to be 236 

void of large objects (e.g., buildings) and cropped by an aperture with the same 237 

diameter size as images of other categories. During the experiment, the stimuli were 238 

centrally displayed on a uniform gray background. The phase-scrambled background 239 

extended approximately 20 x 20 degrees and objects (and scenes) extended 240 

approximately 8 x 8 degrees. 241

Experimental design. The experiment took place in a separate testing room, which the 243 

experimenter monitored visually via webcam from the adjacent room. We used 244 

PsychoPy to display stimuli on a PC monitor (60 Hz refresh rate). Each image was 245 

displayed one-by-one for 500 ms, followed by a fixation cross for a randomly selected 246 

duration of 1,000, 1,100, or 1,200 ms. After approximately every 6±3 images, a probe 247 

stimulus within a blue frame was displayed (Figure 2). Participants were asked to 248 

indicate whether the probe stimulus had been presented among the immediately 249 

preceding 6±3 images by pressing F (“just seen”) or J (“not just seen”). For half of the 250 

probe stimuli, the stimulus was among the previous 6±3 images, and in the other half, 251 

the stimulus was randomly drawn from the rest of the image pool minus the previously 252 

displayed 6±3 images. The probe stimulus remained until participants made a response, 253 

after which visual feedback on the accuracy of participants’ response was immediately 254 

displayed. Participants were invited to take a break after every 31 images. There was a 255 

total of 616 trials, divided into 20 blocks, with each of the 56 stimuli repeated 11 times. 256 

Trial order was random. The entire session lasted approximately 30 minutes. 257

exemplar-pair cross-validation, which requires the classifier to generalize to a pair of 318 

visually-matched building and box exemplars that was not included in the training set. In 319 

each fold, the trials for 7 building exemplars and their 7 visually-matched box 320 

counterparts were used to train the classifier, which was then tested on the trials from 321 

the left-out 1 building exemplar and its visually-matched box counterpart. For each fold, 322 

we measured the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC ROC), 323 

which reflects a summary measure of the model's performance across various 324 

classification thresholds. An AUC from 0.5 to 1 represents a model with random (or 325 

chance-level) to perfect classification performance. The above procedure in a fold was 326 

iterated 8 times traversing all the 8 visually-matched building and box pairs. After 327 

completing all iterations of cross-validation, we averaged the AUC across all iterations 328 

as the final decoding performance. After this procedure was applied to each time point, 329

Statistical Testing. We assessed whether buildings (or scenes) could be distinguished 365 

from boxes (or chairs) and whether the classifier trained for buildings vs. boxes could be 366 

generalized to scenes vs. chairs in the -200 to 800 ms interval using a non-parametric 367 

cluster-based permutation test (Maris and Oostenveld, 2007) as implemented in the 368 

MNE toolbox. This method controls the family-wise error rate (FWE) in multiple 369 

comparisons. The AUC value of every sample (time point) was compared with 0.5 370 

(chance) using a one-sample t-statistic. Then, adjacent samples were clustered based 371 

on a t-value threshold corresponding to an uncorrected p-value of 0.05, one-tailed. The 372 

resulting clusters' FWE-corrected p-value was computed using the Monte Carlo method 373 

involving 10,000 random sign-flipping of AUC values per participant. We considered 374 

only FWE-corrected p-values below 0.05 (one-tailed) as significant. 375

fMRI Preprocessing. Images from the ultra-fast sequence (including functional 505 

localizer and experimental runs) were preprocessed using a multiple-stage approach, 506 

considering the low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and unfavorable sampling of periodic 507 

physiological noise (e.g., respiratory and cardiac activity) inherent in fast fMRI (Chen et 508 

al., 2019). First, images were minimally preprocessed using FSL (Oxford, UK) including 509 

removing the first 28 volumes (~ 4 s) to avoid magnetic field instability, motion 510 

correction (six-parameter affine transform, with the initial SBRef image of each run 511 

produced in multiband sequence as the reference image, given its higher image 512 

quality), and B0 distortion correction with fieldmap. For the functional localizer, an 513 

additional 5 mm Gaussian kernel spatial smoothing was applied. No spatial smoothing 514 

was performed for the experimental runs. 515 

In the second stage of preprocessing, images from each experimental run were 516 

realigned to the SBRef image of the functional localizer run. GLMdenoise was then 517 

applied specifically for denoising the event-related experimental runs (Kay et al., 2013). 518 

GLMdenoise is a model-free method for denoising event-related fMRI by estimating the 519 

noise regressors directly from the data. Consistent with previous reports (Kay et al., 520 

2013; Charest et al., 2018), the SNR in our experimental runs was substantially 521 

improved using this method. However, for the functional localizer images, this method 522 

was inapplicable, so this step was omitted. Next, images from both the functional 523 

localizer run and experimental runs were bandpass filtered (0.01 – 0.15 Hz) to remove 524 

periodic physiological noise above 0.5 Hz. 525 

In the last stage of preprocessing, independent component analysis (ICA) was 526 

performed for each run, which includes both functional localizer and experimental runs. 527

PPA Definition. A method similar to the Group-Constrained Subject-Specific (GSS) 533 

approach was employed to define each participant's bilateral parahippocampal place 534 

area (PPA) as functional regions of interest (ROIs) (Julian et al., 2012). The GSS 535 

method helps define ROIs in individuals while minimizing subjectivity typically present in 536 

traditional handpicked ROI approaches. To do this, onsets and durations of different 537 

categorical blocks in the ultra-fast functional localizer run were convolved with a single-538 

gamma hemodynamic response function (HRF) and fitted using a general linear model. 539 

For each participant, a one-sided t-contrast was calculated, comparing scene blocks to 540 

the average of tools and chairs blocks. The resulting statistical map was thresholded at 541 

p < 0.05 (one-tailed), uncorrected. 542 

Next, the PPA probabilistic activation map (PAM) from the probabilistic functional 543 

atlas (Zhen et al., 2015) was thresholded at 50%, serving as an a priori constraint mask 544 

for the PPA. Individual PPAs were defined as the 30 highest scene-selective voxels 545 

after intersecting the a priori mask with each participant's thresholded activation map. 546 

To assess the robustness of the results across different ROI sizes, the results were 547 

assessed at different ROI sizes from 10 to 70 voxels in increments of 10 voxels. 548

Amplitude, Time-to-Peak latency, and Full Width at Half Maximum. The time 550 

courses of the experimental runs were retrieved from the PPA ROIs and transformed 551 

into percent signal change unit by subtracting and dividing the time-averaged signal of 552 

each voxel. The time courses were then averaged across the voxels of the ROI for each 553 

participant. These time courses were then segmented into epochs spanning from 4 554 

seconds before the onset of each stimulus to 15 seconds after. Epochs corresponding 555 

to the same stimulus condition were first averaged within each individual run, and the 556 

averaged response was further averaged across all runs for each participant. The 557 

averaged epoch of each condition was then baseline-corrected by subtracting the 558 

average of the pre-stimulus 4-second interval. Additionally, the activity at corresponding 559 

time points in the blank background condition was subtracted from the epochs. 560 

Given the intricate nature of neurovascular coupling, relying solely on a single 561 

parameter like Time-to-Peak latency (TTP) for interpretation can lead to 562 

oversimplification. Indeed, past studies have shown significant inter- and intra-subject 563 

correlations between TTP and the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) (de Zwart et al., 564 

2005; Siero et al., 2011). This suggests that these two parameters capture different, but 565 

interconnected, aspects of the temporal dynamics of the hemodynamic response. 566 

Therefore, to better encapsulate the multifaceted nature of temporal dynamics of 567 

hemodynamic responses, both TTP and FWHM, as well as fMRI response amplitude 568 

were set as free parameters and estimated by fitting a conventional single-gamma HRF 569 

function to the PPA time course, separately for each condition. As the signal-to-noise 570 

ratio (SNR) of individual-averaged PPA time course was still not high enough to yield 571 

satisfactory goodness-of-fit for all conditions, to further increase the signal-to-noise 572

Statistical Testing. To assess the statistical significance of amplitude, TTP and FWHM 583 

differences between various conditions, we used a permutation-based paired sample 584 

test. This was performed by first randomly permuting condition assignments of two 585 

individual-averaged time courses in each participant. Next, time courses were re-586

gamma HRF function to estimate amplitude, TTP and FWHM in these new conditions. 588

recorded. This process was repeated 10,000 times, and a parameter (amplitude, TTP or 590

significant difference between the two conditions. With this null distribution, we were 592

the cross-decoding results, we observed a significant cluster in the temporal 709 

generalization matrix, demonstrating significant cross-decoding from buildings vs. boxes 710 

to scenes vs. chairs ( p = .002 , one-tailed, Figure 6B, left). Importantly, this cluster was 711 

not symmetrical with the diagonal, as shown by a significantly larger averaged AUC 712

ROI covering the cluster ( p = .010 , one-tailed permutation test). 714 

Further cluster-based analyses confirmed that the asymmetry was driven by a cluster 715

Discussion 842 

Here, we used EEG and fMRI to measure a selective response to buildings relative to 843 

visually matched control stimuli (boxes). This response emerged relatively late during 844 

object processing and corresponded to the scene-selective response. 845 

The current results inform our understanding of the representation of real-world 846 

object size in visual cortex. Previous studies showed that medial VTC, including PPA, 847 

responds selectively to large and stable objects such as buildings (Aguirre et al., 1998; 848 

Epstein and Kanwisher, 1998; Downing et al., 2006; Mullally and Maguire, 2011; Konkle 849 

and Oliva, 2012; Troiani et al., 2014). Similarly, using M/EEG, studies showed that real-850 

world size could be decoded from around 150 ms after stimulus onset (Khaligh-Razavi 851 

et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022). There is now convincing evidence that this selective 852 

response partly reflects the processing of low- or mid-level visual features that are 853 

typical of large objects (Andrews et al., 2010; Rajimehr et al., 2011; Nasr and Tootell, 854 

2012; Nasr et al., 2014; Coggan et al., 2016; Long et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2022). To 855 

our knowledge, the current study is the first to show large-object (building) selectivity 856 

that is unlikely to reflect such visual feature differences. While individual stimuli 857 

obviously varied in terms of their visual features, buildings and boxes were matched in 858 

terms of image-level metrics across the stimulus set (e.g., retinal size, luminance, 859 

spatial frequency, rectilinearity, and AlexNet representations). Thus, there were no 860 

consistent features (e.g., rectilinearity) that were uniquely and consistently associated 861 

with either building or box categories. Furthermore, EEG decoding was done across 862 

exemplars, and building-selective EEG response patterns generalized to an entirely 863 

different image set consisting of scenes vs chairs. A categorical (building versus box) 864

response was also observed when regressing out item-wise perceptual similarity from 865 

the behavioral visual search task (Supplementary Figure 3). Altogether, these results 866 

demonstrate that low- or mid-level features do not fully explain selectivity for buildings, 867 

suggesting an alternative route driving selectivity in PPA. This is in line with previous 868 

studies showing that the PPA response is modulated by the navigational relevance of 869 

objects in newly learned environments (Janzen and van Turennout, 2004; Schinazi and 870 

Epstein, 2010), and the finding of a selective PPA response to large objects in 871 

congenitally blind participants (He et al., 2013). 872 

If not visual features, what could the selective response to buildings reflect? When 873 

considering this question, we need to account for the overlap between the response to 874 

buildings and scenes, as well as the temporal delay we observed for buildings relative 875 

to scenes. One possibility is that buildings evoke a sense of space (Mullally and 876 

Maguire, 2011). Evidence for this hypothesis comes from a study showing that 877 

large/stable objects were consistently rated as defining local 3D space, and that such 878 

“space-defining” objects activated the PPA, even when they were merely imagined 879 

(Mullally and Maguire, 2011). This account readily explains the overlap between 880 

responses to buildings and scenes, as well as the temporal delay for buildings, since 881 

spatial information is more directly available from pictures of scenes. More generally, 882 

the current results raise the possibility that the PPA may not be directly involved in the 883 

recognition of buildings or large objects (based on visual feature processing), but rather 884 

reflects spatial information that is evoked by objects with certain characteristics (e.g., 885 

being large and stable); notably, these characteristics make these objects suitable as 886 

landmarks (Troiani et al., 2014), in line with the proposed role of the PPA in navigation 887

(Epstein and Baker, 2019). We propose that objects (both small and large) are identified 888 

through a common object recognition pathway (e.g., the lateral occipital cortex), with 889 

contextual and/or spatial expectations activating PPA indirectly following processing in 890 

this pathway, i.e., after objects have been identified. 891 

If building-evoked PPA activity follows object recognition, the specific timing of these 892 

responses will covary with the speed of object recognition. While we did not manipulate 893 

this in our experiment, it is possible that viewing buildings and boxes in the same block 894 

could have delayed recognition. As such, it is possible that building-selective activity 895 

may be observed earlier in other circumstances, for example when buildings and boxes 896 

are presented in separate blocks or sessions. Importantly, however, our stimuli 897 

consisted of sharp, colored photographs shown at fixation. The photographs were 898 

shown one at a time and were not manipulated or degraded; all features of buildings 899 

were visible. The fact that we did not observe a differential response to buildings and 900 

boxes in the first 300 ms after stimulus onset confirms that the response was not driven 901 

by systematic visual feature differences between buildings and boxes. Instead, the 902 

building-selective response revealed here reflects a higher-level, more conceptual 903 

representation of buildings, such as the inter-related concepts of real-world size, typical 904 

viewing distance, and the space that buildings define. 905 

To optimize our chances of finding a strong PPA response, we chose objects 906 

(buildings and boxes) that differed both in size and in stability, based on previous 907 

studies showing that large/stable objects most strongly activate the PPA (Troiani et al., 908 

2014) and evoke a sense of space (Mullally and Maguire, 2011). However, it is possible 909 

that one of these properties is sufficient to selectively activate the PPA. Furthermore, 910

However, as the buildings and non-building objects were not matched in terms of their 922 

visual features, it is possible that the still relatively early response to buildings reflected 923 

selectivity to such visual features (Nasr et al., 2014; Long et al., 2018). Alternatively, 924 

building-selective responses could have reflected a mixture of bottom-up visual feature 925 

selectivity and top-down scene associations. Indeed, in our daily-life environments, 926 

conceptual object properties (e.g., real-world size, animacy) systematically covary with 927 

mid-level visual features (Wang et al., 2022), such that these features provide a useful 928 

shortcut for activating high-level object representations. By equating visual features 929 

across object conditions, this shortcut was not available in the current experiment, 930 

allowing us to isolate top-down input to the PPA and show that buildings can indirectly 931 

activate scene representations. 932

While here we investigated selectivity for buildings in medial VTC, we expect that the 933 

finding of delayed internally-driven category selectivity may similarly hold for other 934 

categories. For example, previous studies have provided evidence for selective 935 

responses to tools and body parts when contrasted with visually matched controls 936 

(Bracci and Beeck, 2016; Kaiser et al., 2016), and congenitally blind individuals show 937 

category-selective VTC responses to these same categories (Peelen et al., 2013; 938 

Kitada et al., 2014; Striem-Amit and Amedi, 2014). Therefore, the current results 939 

support the idea that category selectivity in visual cortex can be driven not only bottom-940 

up, through the feedforward processing of visual features, but also top-down, likely 941 

reflecting input from connected downstream regions involved in functions such as tool 942 

use, reading, navigation, or social cognition (Martin, 2007; Mahon and Caramazza, 943 

2011; Price and Devlin, 2011; Peelen and Downing, 2017). Similar to how the PPA may 944 

not be involved in recognizing or identifying large objects, other category-selective 945 

regions may also not encode the identity of objects of their preferred category. For 946 

example, tool-selective responses may not encode tool identity, but rather the hand 947 

actions associated with tools (Perini et al., 2014). Future studies could extend the 948 

current approach to other categories, including categories (e.g., faces) that appear to be 949 

primarily visually driven (Wang et al., 2015; Bi et al., 2016). 950 

In summary, the current study provides evidence for a selective neural response to 951 

large objects (buildings) that is unlikely to reflect visual feature processing. The building-952 

selective response consisted of a delayed activation of scene-selective mechanisms. 953

PPA. More generally, they demonstrate that category selectivity in visual cortex can be 955
