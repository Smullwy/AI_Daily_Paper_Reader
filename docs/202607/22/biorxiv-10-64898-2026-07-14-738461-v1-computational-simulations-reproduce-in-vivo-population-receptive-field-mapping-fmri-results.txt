Abstract 7

Population receptive field (pRF) mapping is widely used to characterize retinotopic or-8

ganization based on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data. Despite its broad 9

adoption, the factors governing intra- and inter-subject variability in pRF estimates remain 10 

incompletely understood, limiting the ability to evaluate and optimize visual stimulation 11 

paradigms prior to data collection. Here, we investigate whether large-scale simulations 12 

can reproduce in vivo run-to-run variability patterns observed in pRF mapping and provide 13 

mechanistic insight into their origins. With GEMSim-pRF, our newly proposed computa-14 

tional framework for large-scale simulation and estimation of pRF responses, we generated 15 

millions of synthetic fMRI time courses across a wide range of receptive field parameters and 16 

noise conditions. We analyzed the variability of pRF estimation results derived from simu-17 

lations and compared them with in vivo data from the publicly available NYU Retinotopy 18 

Dataset. Here we show that our simulation results matched the characteristic eccentricity-19 

dependent variability observed in empirical pRF estimates. These findings show that key 20 

variability patterns observed in empirical pRF mapping can be successfully reproduced in 21 

large-scale simulations, establishing simulation-based analysis as a practical approach for un-22 

derstanding, predicting, evaluating and ultimately improving the behaviour of retinotopic 23 

mapping paradigms before empirical data collection. 24

# 1 Introduction 27 

Introduced in the late 2000s, population receptive field (pRF)-based mapping [Dumoulin and 28 

Wandell, 2008] has become the prime method for determining retinotopic associations between 29 

the visual field and the visual cortex. This approach is based on the acquisition of in vivo func-30 

tional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data to determine neural activation patterns in the 31

that typically shift throughout the visual field. Retinotopic mapping results are obtained by 33 

analysing the fMRI data using pRF mapping software packages such as mrVista [Dumoulin 34 

and Wandell, 2008], analyzePRF [Kay et al., 2013], popeye [DeSimone et al., 2016], SamSrfX 35 

[Schwarzkopf, 2018], Braincoder [de Hollander et al., 2024], or the very recently introduced 36 

GPU-based software GEM-pRF [Mittal et al., 2026]. Typically, these pRF mapping tools relate 37 

an empirically measured fMRI time course to a pRF defined by its specific location and size 38 

in the visual field. Although the specific parameter estimation strategies differ across software 39 

implementations, the underlying principle is shared: pRF parameters are estimated by deter-40 

mining the pRF parameter configuration with the best match between predicted and observed 41 

fMRI signal. As described in the existing literature, the predicted time courses depend on the 42 

pRF model used. Typical approaches include two-dimensional Gaussian modeling [Dumoulin 43 

and Wandell, 2008], difference-of-Gaussians modeling [Zuiderbaan et al., 2012], or compressive 44 

spatial summation modeling [Kay et al., 2013]. 45 

For adequate interpretation of retinotopic mapping results, particularly in clinical and trans-46 

lational applications, it is essential to understand not only the pRF estimates themselves but 47 

also any bias introduced by the spatiotemporal properties of visual stimulation paradigms them-48 

selves. For example, [Prabhakaran et al., 2021] showed that fMRI-based visual field reconstruc-49 

tions depend strongly on the visual stimulation paradigm, with different approaches exhibiting 50 

distinct sensitivity-specificity trade-offs. Similarly, several studies have reported systematic 51 

stimulus-dependent differences in pRF size, eccentricity, visual field coverage, and model fit 52 

quality [Alvarez et al., 2015, Chang et al., 2025a, Linhardt et al., 2021]. In order to com-53 

pare different analysis pipelines, Lerma-Usabiaga et al. [2020] introduced a powerful validation 54 

framework for assessing parameter recovery under controlled noise and modeling assumptions. 55 

Together, these findings indicate that the variability in pRF estimates is not solely determined 56 

by measurement noise, but also emerges from interactions between stimulus design, model as-57 

sumptions, and the estimation procedure itself. 58 

Understanding the origins of this variability remains challenging using empirical data alone. 59 

While repeated measurements can provide estimates of run-to-run variability, the number of 60 

repetitions that can be acquired within a single participant is inherently limited by scan du-61 

ration, participant burden, and experimental cost. Consequently, empirical studies are often 62 

statistically underpowered for systematically investigating how variability changes across visual 63 

field location, pRF size, noise level, or stimulus design. Existing large-scale retinotopy datasets 64 

further illustrate this limitation. The Human Connectome Project [Benson et al., 2018] provides 65 

multiple retinotopic stimuli across a large cohort but typically only a single run per stimulus. 66 

Conversely, the NYU retinotopy dataset [Himmelberg et al., 2021a] provides extensive repeated 67 

measurements but is restricted to a single stimulation paradigm. Similarly, the CHN retino-68 

topy dataset [Chang et al., 2025b] includes multiple paradigms and sessions, but only a limited 69 

number of effective repetitions per stimulus configuration. As a result, empirical datasets alone 70 

provide limited opportunities to systematically characterize the mechanisms governing pRF 71 

estimation variability. 72 

Simulations provide a potential solution to this problem as they could provide a powerful 73

To overcome these challenges, we are introducing GEMSim-pRF (GPU-Empowered Map-79

crease in variability with eccentricity emerges naturally in simulations and that a simple white 88

establish large-scale simulation as a practical approach for understanding and predicting vari-90 

ability in pRF mapping and provide a foundation for future simulation-driven evaluation of 91 

retinotopic stimulation paradigms. 92 

# 2 Methods 93 

2.1 Empirical retinotopy data 94 

To evaluate the correspondence between simulated and empirical measurements, we used the 95 

publicly available New York University (NYU) retinotopy dataset [Himmelberg et al., 2021a]. 96 

The dataset comprises functional MRI data from 44 healthy participants who completed a 97 

standard bar-aperture retinotopy experiment. 98 

Data were acquired at the NYU Center for Brain Imaging using a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM 99 

Prisma scanner with a 64-channel head coil. Structural images consisted of T1-weighted scans, 100 

with additional T2-weighted images available for a subset (n=11) of participants. Functional 101 

data were collected using a multiband T2*-weighted EPI sequence (TR = 1 s, 2 mm isotropic 102 

resolution), along with reverse phase-encoded spin-echo images to enable distortion correction. 103 

All preprocessing was performed by the dataset authors using fMRIPrep [Esteban et al., 2019]. 104 

Further details on acquisition and preprocessing can be found in Himmelberg et al. [2021b]. 105 

2.2 Visual stimulus 106 

The visual stimulation protocol used in this study corresponds to the bar-aperture paradigm 107 

employed in the New York University (NYU) retinotopy dataset [Himmelberg et al., 2021b]. 108 

This stimulus was used both for the empirical data and for the generation of simulated time 109 

courses, ensuring consistency between empirical and simulated analyses. 110 

For the in vivo data collection, participants viewed dynamic visual content presented within 111 

a moving bar aperture (width 3.1 °). The aperture contained a stimulus pattern of colorful ob-112

For the generation of simulated fMRI time courses and for pRF estimation in both empirical 118

stimulus geometry and timing, while omitting higher-order visual features that are not covered 121 

in pRF estimation. 122 

2.3 pRF estimations 123 

Population receptive field (pRF) parameters were estimated using GEM-pRF [Mittal et al., 124 

2026], a GPU-accelerated software package (gemprf-0.1.11, https://pypi.org/project/gemprf/) 125 

for large-scale pRF estimations. The same estimation pipeline was applied consistently to both 126 

simulated and empirical datasets. GEM-pRF was selected because it combines high computa-127 

tional efficiency with estimation accuracy comparable to established pRF analysis tools, such 128 

as mrVista [Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008], making it well-suited for the large number of sim-129 

ulations required in the present framework. A dense sampling space was specified to ensure 130 

high-resolution parameter estimation. The search space employed a 219 × 219 spatial grid up 131 

to 37 ° diameter, resulting in a spacing of approximately 0.17 ° between adjacent grid points. 132 

pRF size ( σ) was sampled using 24 linearly spaced values ranging from 0.1 ° to 3.0 °. In this 133 

study, only grid-based pRF estimation was performed. As described in the original GEM-pRF 134 

framework, sufficiently dense grid sampling provides accurate estimates, while refinement pri-135 

marily interpolates between neighboring grid points [Mittal et al., 2026]. Given the focus of 136 

this work is on evaluating overall variability trends and RMS error distributions rather than 137 

fine-grained parameter optimization, dense grid-based fitting was sufficient and substantially 138 

reduced computational overhead. 139 

The pRFs were modeled as two-dimensional isotropic Gaussians. HRF parameters were 140 

specified to select a canonical double gamma SPM-style hemodynamic response function [Friston 141 

et al., 1998], which was normalized to have unit sum. Low-frequency temporal drifts were 142 

modeled using discrete cosine transform (DCT) basis functions, including a constant term and 143 

additional low-frequency components (0.5 and 1.0 cycles per scan). In order to keep the residual 144 

degrees-of-freedom between the simulated and empirical data consistent, no further subject-145 

specific regressors were used for the empirical data analysis. 146

2.5 Characterisation of pRF estimation variability 192 

A series of analyses were designed to evaluate the extent to which simulations reproduce the 193 

variability observed in empirical measurements and to investigate the contribution of individual 194 

factors to pRF estimation variability under controlled simulation conditions. 195 

2.5.1 Variability at representative locations 196 

To illustrate stimulus-dependent variability in a controlled setting, two representative pRF 197 

locations were selected: one at the center of the visual field and one at a peripheral location. 198 

For both cases, the pRF size was fixed to σ=1 ° to isolate the effect of spatial position on 199 

estimation variability. 200 

For each location, 2000 noisy time courses were generated at the chosen CNR levels. The 201 

pRF parameters were then estimated independently for each time course. The resulting es-202 

timates (x,y) were visualized as scatter distributions to characterize the spatial dispersion of 203 

estimated pRF positions relative to the ground truth. 204 

2.5.2 Variability correspondence between empirical and simulated data 205 

To assess whether variability observed in simulations reflects empirical trends, we compared the 206 

spatial spread of pRF estimates as a function of eccentricity between simulated and empirical 207 

datasets. The empirical analysis was performed using the pRF estimates from the primary 208 

visual cortex (V1) region of the NYU Retinotopy Dataset. Only pRF estimates with a vari-209 

ance explained ( R2) of at least 10% were included in both empirical and simulated analyses. 210

# 3 Results 293 

3.1 Empirical CNR distribution 294 

Contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) values were computed from the empirical retinotopy dataset 295 

as described in Section 2.4.2. The empirical CNR distribution (Figure 2) was used to identify 296 

representative simulation conditions for comparison between simulated and empirical variability 297 

trends. The resulting CNR distribution was right-skewed and exhibited a median CNR = 2.36, 298 

with a substantial proportion of voxels extending toward higher CNR values. The central 50% 299 

of CNR values lay between 1.40 (Q1) and 3.63 (Q3). 300 

Based on the empirical CNR distribution, we selected simulation conditions centered around 301 

the approximated median (CNR = 2.5) together with lower and higher neighboring conditions 302 

(CNR = 1.5 and 3.5) to examine variability trends across a representative range of signal 303 

qualities. 304

3.3 Correspondence between empirical and simulated variability 317 

The magnitude of variability depended systematically on the simulated CNR level (Figure 4). 318 

Simulations performed at CNR = 1.5 consistently exhibited the largest MAD values across the 319 

eccentricity range, whereas simulations performed at CNR = 3.5 produced the lowest variability. 320 

The median empirical MAD values were largely bracketed by the corresponding median MAD 321 

values obtained for the CNR = 1.5 and CNR = 2.5 simulations across most eccentricity bins. 322 

The primary exception occurred in the highest eccentricity bin, where the empirical median 323 

fell below all simulated conditions. Across both empirical and simulated datasets, variability 324 

remained relatively stable over much of the visual field before increasing substantially at larger 325 

eccentricities. 326 

Quantitatively, strong agreement between simulated and empirical variability was observed 327 

for all three simulated CNR levels. Pearson correlation coefficients of median MAD values com-328 

puted across eccentricity bins were 0.97, 0.95, and 0.90 for CNR values of 1.5, 2.5, and 3.5, 329 

respectively, indicating a high degree of similarity in the overall eccentricity-dependent variabil-330 

ity distributions (Table 1). Corresponding Spearman correlation coefficients were 0.78, 0.91, 331 

and 0.89, demonstrating consistent monotonic relationships across eccentricity. The highest 332 

Spearman correlation was observed for the CNR = 2.5 condition, which also corresponds to the 333 

median CNR of the empirical dataset. 334 

The boxplot representation further demonstrates that the simulations reproduce not only the 335

# 4 Discussion 370 

The primary objective of this study was to investigate whether large-scale simulations can re-371 

produce the variability patterns observed in empirical population receptive field (pRF) mapping 372 

data and to identify factors that contribute to their emergence. Using the novel GEMSim-pRF 373 

framework, we performed millions of pRF simulations across a range of receptive field param-374 

eters and contrast-to-noise conditions and compared the resulting variability with repeated-375 

measurement variability observed in the NYU retinotopy dataset. The results demonstrate 376 

that the characteristic increase in variability with eccentricity observed in empirical data was 377 

successfully reproduced in our simulations. These findings establish a close correspondence 378 

between simulated and empirical variability features and provide a foundation for simulation-379 

driven investigation of pRF estimation behaviour and retinotopic stimulus design. 380 

4.1 Large-scale simulations reproduce in vivo variability trends 381 

One of the most interesting findings of this study is that the characteristic eccentricity-dependent 382 

variability observed in empirical pRF mapping results can be reproduced using large-scale simu-383 

lations. Notably, this was achieved without incorporating any empirical data-based constraints. 384 

Across all investigated CNR conditions, the simulated and empirical median MAD values ex-385 

hibited similar eccentricity-dependent behaviour, yielding high Pearson as well as Spearman 386 

correlation coefficient values (Table 1). One might expect that more realistic cortical pRF 387 

distributions, physiological noise sources, or other empirical constraints would be required to 388 

recover the observed variability behaviour. Instead, this analysis shows that the dominant 389 

characteristics of the empirical variability distribution emerged even in the absence of such 390 

assumptions. This suggests that the increase in variability with eccentricity is not solely a con-391 

sequence of the non-uniform distribution of pRF locations across the visual field in the empirical 392 

data (e.g., due to cortical magnification [Harvey and Dumoulin, 2011]). Instead, a substantial 393 

component of the observed variability appears to arise from the interaction between stimulus 394 

geometry, signal quality, the pRF model, and the estimation procedure itself. The median of 395 

empirical MAD values is largely bracketed by simulations performed at CNR values of 1.5 and 396 

Mittal et al. : Preprint Page 11 of 25 

> .CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license made available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted July 20, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.14.738461 doi: bioRxiv preprint

3.5, while showing particularly strong correspondence with the median empirical CNR condition 397 

(CNR = 2.5), supporting the view that these factors alone explain the major features of the 398 

variability trend. 399 

While the overall correspondence between simulations and empirical data was strong, a 400 

noticeable discrepancy remained at the highest eccentricity bin where empirical variability fell 401 

below all simulated conditions. At the most peripheral eccentricities, the empirical variability 402 

fell below all simulated conditions. Several factors may contribute to this behaviour. First, 403 

empirical estimates are based on comparatively few repeated measurements (up to 12 runs), 404 

and Figure 5 demonstrates that peripheral locations exhibit substantially higher estimation 405 

uncertainty. Consequently, fewer reliable estimates may remain after quality-control filtering, 406 

potentially leading to an underestimation of the empirical variability. Second, the noise model 407 

employed in the present simulations may not fully capture estimation behaviour at the visual 408 

field periphery, where additional nonlinear effects or physiological factors may become more 409 

important. Nevertheless, this local discrepancy does not alter the principal finding that both 410 

empirical and simulated data exhibit the same overall increase in variability with eccentricity. 411 

This correspondence has a practical implication; it establishes confidence that large-scale 412 

simulations can capture variability patterns observed in real data. If the major empirical trends 413 

can be recovered under controlled settings, simulations can characterize estimation behaviour 414 

retrospectively and also predict how changes in stimulus design, signal quality, or model as-415 

sumptions influence variability before empirical data are collected. 416 

4.2 Role of noise in pRF estimation variability 417 

The present findings also provide insight into the role of noise in shaping pRF estimation 418 

variability. Although the simulations employed a simple additive white Gaussian noise model, 419 

they reproduced the major variability trends observed in the empirical data. Importantly, this 420 

correspondence was not achieved merely by adding noise at any arbitrary scale to simulated 421 

time courses, but by introducing noise in a manner that preserved a consistent contrast-to-noise 422 

ratio (CNR) across different pRF configurations. 423 

Holding CNR constant is critical because noise-free model responses vary substantially in 424 

amplitude as a function of pRF size. For pRFs modeled as 2d Gaussian, larger pRFs integrate 425 

over a broader region of the visual field and therefore generate higher-amplitude model signals 426 

than smaller pRFs. Applying identical noise amplitudes to all simulated time courses would 427 

therefore yield systematically different effective CNR values across pRF sizes and small pRFs 428 

would appear artificially more variable simply because they were evaluated at lower CNR. 429 

To avoid this confound, model time courses were normalized to remove pRF size-dependent 430 

amplitude differences before noise was added as formulated in section 2.4.1. Noise was then 431 

scaled to the target CNR, ensuring all simulated pRF configurations were evaluated under 432 

comparable signal-quality conditions. This procedure allowed the influence of pRF size, stimulus 433 

geometry, and spatial location to be examined independently from trivial differences in signal 434 

amplitude. 435 

The empirical variability trends emerging under these controlled conditions suggest that 436 

many of the dominant features of pRF estimation variability can be simulated without using 437

complex physiological or scanner-specific noise. A substantial variability component arises from 438 

the interaction between signal quality, spatiotemporal pattern of the stimulus, and the pRF 439 

estimation process itself. This does not imply that physiological noise, motion artifacts, or 440 

scanner-related effects are unimportant, only that they are not necessary to recover the major 441 

population-level variability trends in the empirical dataset. More sophisticated noise models 442 

may further improve quantitative correspondence, but a controlled white Gaussian noise model 443 

is sufficient to capture the dominant characteristics of pRF estimation variability. 444 

4.3 Spatial structure of estimation uncertainty 445 

In addition to our results on variability aggregated across large populations of pRF estimates, 446 

simulations also enable examining uncertainty at the level of individual pRF parameters and 447 

spatial locations. Having established a close correspondence between simulated and empirical 448 

variability distributions, we used root-mean-square (RMS) error maps computed relative to 449 

known ground-truth parameters to characterize how estimation uncertainty is distributed across 450 

the visual field. 451 

RMS maps shown in Figure 5 reveal that estimation uncertainty is spatially structured 452 

rather than uniform, depending on both the pRF parameters being estimated and the underly-453 

ing ground-truth configuration. This observation is consistent with prior work demonstrating 454 

that pRF estimation is sensitive to noise, stimulus properties, and model-stimulus interactions 455 

[Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008, Lerma-Usabiaga et al., 2020]. Furthermore, empirical studies 456 

have shown that retinotopic estimates depend on both stimulus design and visual field location 457 

[Alvarez et al., 2015, Chang et al., 2025a, Linhardt et al., 2021]. The present simulations ex-458 

tend these observations by providing direct access to ground-truth parameters, thereby enabling 459 

spatial patterns of estimation uncertainty to be examined explicitly. 460 

An important observation is that the spatial structure of uncertainty differs substantially 461 

across pRF parameters. Horizontal and vertical position estimates exhibit anisotropic pat-462 

terns of uncertainty, indicating direction-dependent estimation uncertainty. Eccentricity errors 463 

generally increase with distance from the fovea, while polar-angle estimates display spatially 464 

structured regions of high and low uncertainty. The localized increase in RMS for polar angle 465 

at center is expected because polar angle is undefined at the center. In contrast, pRF size ( σ)466 

depends globally on the underlying ground-truth size. Across all visual field locations, RMS er-467 

ror for σ increases systematically with increasing ground-truth pRF size, indicating that larger 468 

receptive fields are more prone to estimation errors. 469 

The σ error maps show a pronounced X-shaped pattern that remains visible across a broad 470 

range of ground-truth pRF sizes. One plausible explanation is the potentially uneven spatiotem-471 

poral sampling properties of the stimulus. Specifically, when quantifying the number of times 472 

individual visual field locations are sampled by the bar stimulus used in the NYU retinotopy 473 

dataset [Himmelberg et al., 2021a], an X-shaped pattern emerges in which locations along the 474 

diagonal axes are sampled more frequently than surrounding regions. Denser sampling in these 475 

regions may constrain pRF size estimation, reducing estimation error. The effect may be pro-476 

nounced for σ rather than for position parameters, potentially because pRF center locations 477 

were sampled more densely in the simulations while pRF size used a coarser parameter grid. 478

This interpretation is consistent with the RMS structure, but disentangling stimulus sampling, 479 

parameter discretisation, and noise will require further work. 480 

Beyond error magnitude, the simulations also revealed systematic spatial characteristics of 481 

the estimation process. For example, peripheral pRFs exhibited an increasing radial outward 482 

displacement of the estimated pRF centers as CNR decreased (Figure 3). While assessing the 483 

origin of this behaviour is beyond this study, our results illustrate that large-scale simulations 484 

can be used to identify systematic estimation characteristics that are difficult to isolate using 485 

empirical data alone. 486 

More broadly, simulations expose behaviour that analyses of aggregated empirical datasets 487 

can not address. While empirical datasets typically provide access only to aggregate estimation 488 

outcomes, simulations show how uncertainty propagates through individual pRF parameters 489 

across the visual field. Such analyses can help identify regions of systematic estimation bias, 490 

reveal parameter-specific limitations of a stimulus design, and link population-level variability 491 

trends observed to local estimation behaviour. In this sense, the RMS maps complement the 492 

MAD-based analyses by linking global variability patterns to local estimation behaviour across 493 

the visual field. 494 

4.4 Implications for simulation-driven evaluation of retinotopic stimuli 495 

Because simulations provide access to known ground-truth parameters, they can be used to 496 

compare stimulation paradigms before empirical data are collected. For example, simulation 497 

can reveal whether a specific stimulus design systematically increases uncertainty in particular 498 

regions of the visual field, information that is difficult to obtain empirically, where the true 499 

underlying pRF parameters are unknown. 500 

An important aspect of the present simulation framework is the normalization strategy 501 

applied. It removes the trivial amplitude scaling introduced by pRF size, so noise can be 502 

injected at consistent CNR across sizes, while preserving amplitude differences arising from the 503 

stimulus-pRF interaction. This is particularly important for future comparisons of retinotopic 504 

stimulation paradigms, as normalization schemes based on peak response amplitude would 505 

discard exactly the stimulus-dependent response differences that distinguish one paradigm from 506 

another, and that may themselves affect estimation performance. 507 

4.5 Limitations and future work 508 

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, the anal-509 

yses used only focused on a single retinotopic stimulation paradigm from the NYU retinotopy 510 

dataset. The large-scale simulations can reproduce the variability trends for this stimulus, but 511 

whether similar correspondence is obtained for other paradigms, such as wedge/ring stimuli 512 

[Linhardt et al., 2021] or designs incorporating cortical magnification principles [Chang et al., 513 

2025a], remains to be tested. Second, the simulations used additive white Gaussian noise 514 

and did not explicitly model physiological fluctuations, motion artifacts, or scanner-specific 515 

noise sources [Poldrack et al., 2011]. While these are not necessary to reproduce the domi-516 

nant empirical variability trends, they may contribute to finer-scale estimation behaviour and 517 

parameter-specific biases. Future work should therefore investigate whether more realistic noise 518

idation was performed using the NYU retinotopy dataset alone. Although validation using 520 

independent datasets would be desirable, assessing within-subject variability requires multiple 521 

repeated measurements of the same subject and stimulus condition, a combination rarely avail-522 

able. While some datasets include large numbers of subjects, they often provide only a small 523 

number of runs per subject, making reliable estimation of within-subject variability difficult. 524 

Future work would therefore benefit from independent datasets containing extensive repeated 525 

measurements acquired with different retinotopic stimulation paradigms. Despite these limi-526 

tations, the present framework extends naturally to alternative paradigms and noise models, 527 

toward establishing simulation-driven evaluation as a complementary tool for optimizing retino-528 

topic mapping. 529 

# 5 Conclusion 530 

Variability is a fundamental characteristic of population receptive field mapping, yet its origins 531 

have remained difficult to disentangle based on in vivo measurements alone. In this study, we 532 

demonstrated that the characteristic eccentricity-dependent variability observed in empirical 533 

pRF estimates can be reproduced by large-scale simulations computed under controlled noise 534 

conditions. This finding suggests that a substantial component of the observed variability 535 

emerges from the interaction between stimulus properties, signal quality, and the estimation 536 

process itself, rather than being solely determined by physiological factors. By establishing a 537 

close correspondence between simulated and empirical variability trends and by enabling direct 538 

investigation of parameter-specific uncertainty, this work provides a new framework for studying 539 

the mechanisms that shape pRF estimation. And, this framework turns retinotopic stimulus 540 

design into a testable variable: paradigms can be evaluated, and their parameter-specific biases 541 

identified, before data are collected. It can complement in vivo retinotopic mapping studies and 542 

support the systematic evaluation of retinotopic mapping paradigms. 543
