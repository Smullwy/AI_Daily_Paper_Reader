Abstract 18 

Decline s in the cognitive  inhibition  that comes  with aging  impacts  daily  life and 19 

independence.  It is still debated whether  overactivation during cognitive inhibition tasks  in older 20 

adults  is due to  compensation or neural noise. To address this question, we examined age -related 21 

changes in inter -subject similarity (ISS) of functional connectivity  from  task -based and resting -22 

state fMRI.  Using Bayesian  hierarchical  modeling , we identified 27  Stroop  task -related regions of 23 

interest and found that ISS in these regions was significantly reduced in older adults during task 24 

performance. Furthermore, aging is associated with a loss of consistent functional connectivity 25 

patterns in frontal regions, accompanied by the emergence of a convergent compensatory 26 

mechanism within visual attention  region s. Principal component analysis of individual activation 27 

deviations from the group -mean pattern showed that task performance in older adults  cannot  be 28 

explained by overall task -related brain activation, but rather by a specific spatial component 29 

involving suppression of the default mode network and increased activity in visual attention regions. 30 

These findings  indicate  that task -related brain overactivation in older adults  is not due to  uniform 31 

noise or uniform compensation, but rather a spatially specific pattern of functional reorganization. 32

1.  Introduction 34 

Cognitive inhibition is  an important aspect  of e xecutive  function , which is a part of  higher 35 

cognitive  function  (1, 2 ). Cognitive inhibition  plays a role in supporting appropriate behavioral 36 

selection in daily life  (3, 4 ), and its decline  in older adults  leads to  a decreased  quality  of life ,37 

including an increased  risk of  falls  (5, 6 ). Therefore, understanding age -related  decline in  cognitive 38 

inhibit ion  is  required  in an  aging society. 39 

The Stroop task is  a representative paradigm for assessing  cognitive  inhibition  (7), in which 40 

participants were required to name the ink color of the presented word , rather than reading the 41 

word’s  meaning . In  the incongruent condition, in wh ich the word meaning and ink color did not 42 

match,  the response time  is  prolonged compared to  that in the congruent condition. This 43 

phenomenon is more pronounced in older  than younger adults  (8). Functional  magnetic resonance 44 

imaging (f MRI ) studies have consistently reported activation of the anterior cingulate cortex, 45 

dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and parietal cortex  during the Stroop task  in younger adults  (9).46 

Furthermore, in  older adults , increased activation  has been observed not only in these regions but 47 

also in additional brain regions, such as  the inferior frontal gyrus  and  supplementary motor area ,48 

compared to young adults  (10, 11 ). However, t he cause of this overactivation  is still debated . Some 49 

research  has indicated a  “compensation ” for functional decline  to maintain  cognitive  performance 50

(12 ). Another  study  suggest s the  “noise ” attributable to  reduced neural efficiency  (13 ).51 

Distinguishing these interpretations requires examining  inter -individual consistency in 52 

overactivation .53 

Inter -subject similarity (ISS)  is  a useful index for examining  the  stability  of  functional 54 

connectivity (FC)  patterns across individuals. A high ISS  indicates  common neural mechanisms 55 

across individuals , whereas  a low ISS indicates  variable  processing strategies.  A previous  study 56 

with re sting state  and task  fMRI  showed that older adults have  a significantly lower ISS  than 57 

younger adults  (14 ). However,  most studies have  us ed  the  ISS analyses at the whole -brain level, 58 

which inevitably includes signals from regions unrelated to the task. Therefore, it is essential to 59 

identify task -rel ated  regions and then perform ISS analyses within those regions.  A general linear 60 

model (GLM) is conventionally used  to identify  regions  activated during  task  in fMRI . However, 61 

this method  has  a difficulty  detecting  regions with  large individual differences  in activation patterns .62 

To overcome this limitation,  we employed hierarchical Bayesian modeling to estimate activation 63 

because  this model  regards  individual parameters as deviations from a group -level distribution , and 64 

it is possible to  flexibly capture individual differences  (15 ).65 

In addition to  the  ISS of  resting -state  FC,  prior  studies using diffusion -weighted  imaging 66 

(D WI)  have  report ed  that white matter microstructure changes with aging  (16 ). Structural 67 

connectivity (SC) reflects  the interregional anatomical connections derived from DWI data , and FC 68 

is formed within the constraints of this anatomical architecture  (17, 18 ). One  previous  study 69 

revealed that resting -state  functional network integration appears to increase primarily when SC 70 

begins to decline  (19 ).71 

This study aimed  to identify the underlying  factors  of  ISS decline during  the  Stroop task 72 

among  older adults from the perspective of overactivation , using task -related ROIs derived from 73 

hierarchical Bayesian modeling , and to examine whether specific brain activation patterns are 74 

associated with task performance. 75

2.4  Relationship  between  group  differences and  individual  variability 124 

Pearson correlations between  group difference parameter  δr and  ROI -specific standard 125 

deviation  σa,r were computed . A significant  positive correlation between  δr and  σa,r was observed 126 

across  the  Stroop -ROI s (r = 0. 49 , p < 0.01 ). However,  scaling analyses revealed that this 127 

relationship was partially confounded by the  mean -variance scaling effect. The correlation between 128 

task -related activation  μr and  σa,r was  significant (r = 0.5 8, p < 0.01 ),  indicating  that ROIs w ith 129 

higher mean activation tended to  have proportionally larger variance. When this scaling effect was 130 

controlled  via partial  correlation, the relationship  decreased  (r = 0.3 9, p < 0.0 5).  Furthermore , when 131 

variability was expressed as the  coefficient of variation, the correlation with  δ was eliminated (r = 132 

−0.001, p =  0.995). 133

2.5  Leave -one -out ROI  analysis 135 

We then  conducted  a leave -one -out ISS analysis  to  assess  the causal contribution of  each 136 

ROI to  the  ISS group difference . The  Δd  was defined as the difference between the effect size of 137 

the group difference across all Stroop -ROIs and that obtained after excluding a specific ROI, and 138 

was correlated with  σa,r and  δr. A significant negative correlation between  σa,r and  Δd (r = −0.59, 139 

p < 0.01) was  observed  (Supplementary  Fig.  3). Notably,  δr showed no significant correlation with 140 

Δd (r = −0.17, p = 0.40).  The  regions  increasing the ISS group difference  most strongly  were the 141 

frontal areas ( Fig.  3A), including  the  left  middle frontal gyrus  (MidFG ) (Δd = +0.30), right MidFG 142 

(Δd =  +0.23), left  inferior frontal gyrus, pars opercularis  (IFGoper ) (Δd =  +0.20), and right IFGoper 143 

(Δd =  +0.20) . In contrast, the regions suppressing the ISS group difference  was  the  occipital -visual 144 

areas, including  the  left  inferior division  of  lateral occipital cortex  (iLOC ) (Δd = −0.29) and right 145 

OFusG ( Δd =  −0.25).  Group -specific decomposition  reveal ed  that these two sets of regions  were 146 

derived from contributions from different groups ( Fig.  3B).  The f rontal regions contributed to the 147 

ISS group difference by maintaining high FC consistency in the younger group, whereas  the 148 

occipital -visual regions buffered it  by maintaining FC consistency in the older group  (Fig.  3C).149

2.6 Principal  component  analysis of  activation  patterns 151 

The hierarchical Bayesian model  provided  the  ai,r matrix , where each element represents 152 

the  subject -specific deviations in activation . Principal  component  analysis  (PCA ) applied to the  ai,r153 

matrix of the older group yielded  principal component 1 ( PC1 ) accounting for 72.9% of the variance, 154 

PC2  accounting for 6.8%, and PC3 accounting for 5.4%.  Only PC3 showed a significant positive 155 

correlation with ΔRT (r = 0.4 8, Bonferroni -corrected  P < 0.0 5) ( Fig.  4A).  In  PC3 , ROIs with the 156 

large positive loadings were the precuneus (+0.43), frontal medial co rtex (MedFC; +0.37), left 157 

thalamus (+0.30), and paracingulate gyrus (PaCiG; +0.21), whereas those with the largest negative 158 

loadings were the iLOC  (−0.37), occipital pole (OP; −0.2 6), and superior parietal lobule (SPL; 159 

−0.2 2) (Fig.  4B).  Together , older adults whose activation was biased toward s the  medial  default 160 

mode network ( DMN ) regions tended to exhibit larger ΔRT  values , indicating  poorer performance .161 

In contrast,  those whose activation was biased toward  visual attention regions  tended to exhibit 162 

smaller ΔRT values , indicating  better performance.  PC1 , which represents  variation in overall 163 

activation lev el  (Supplementary  Fig.  4), showed no significant correlation with ΔRT (r = −0.0 7, p 164 

= 0.7 3), indicating that global variations in activation level s were unrelated to Stroop performance. 165

2.7 Structural  connectivity 167 

SC analysis was  conducted  to verify whether changes in  FC  were attributable to  the 168 

anatomical degeneration of nerve fibers.  Edgewise group comparisons across all 5,460 connections 169 

revealed 213 edges with significantly lower SC in the older group  (p < 0.05, FDR -corrected)  (Fig. 170 

5A). The  Stroop -ROI pairs  were assessed using  enrichment analysis  to evaluate  the  concentrated 171 

age -related SC decreases . Three  connectivity  categories were defined:  Stroop -ROI  pairs, pairs of 172 

the  Stroop -ROI s and  non -Stroop -ROI s, and pairs of non -Stroop -ROI s (Fig.  5A) . An enrichment 173 

ratio greater than 1 indicates that significant SC decreases are  concentr ated  in that category.  Only 174 

four connections  among the  Stroop -ROI  pairs showed significant SC reduction  (p < 0.05,  FDR -175 

correct ed) , yielding an enrichment ratio of 0.34. This was substantially lower than the rate observed 176 

among non -Stroop -ROI  pairs (enrichment ratio = 1.19), indicating that age -related white matter 177 

degeneration  tends to  occur  within  non -Stroop -ROI s. Furthermore, n o significant correlations were 178 

found between SC strength in  the hub regions and  ΔRT ( Fig.  5B). 179

3. Discussion 182 

This  study  clearly  demonstrate s not only  factors underlying ISS reduction  during 183 

performing  the Stroop task in older adults from the perspective of overactivation , but also  the 184 

relationship between brain activity patterns and task performance.  We first , using  hierarchical 185 

Bayesian  estimation,  identified  the  Stroop -ROI s and , importantly , these  Stroop -ROIs include s the 186 

several brain regions related to the cognitive inhibition  (20 ). The  WB -ROIs showed significant ISS 187 

group differences  both  task  condition  and  resting state . In contrast,  the Stroop -ROI s showed 188 

significant ISS group difference s during  the  task condition only . Th ese  observations  indicate  that 189 

the ISS decline in task -rel ated  regions reflects functional change  related to the  task , rather than a 190 

general age -related deterioration already present in  the rest ing state .191 

The combination of hierarchical Bayesian estimation and leave -one -out ISS analysis 192 

showed  that frontal regions maintain high FC  patterns  consistency in younger  group  but  not  in  the 193 

older group . In contrast, occipital regions maintain FC consistency  in  the older group.  Furthermore, 194 

by using  the individual deviation matrix  ai,r derived from th is  model as the input for PCA , specific 195 

brain activity patterns associated with Stroop task performance  was identified .196

3.2 Task specificity of ISS reduction 212 

In  the  Stroop -ROI s, no significant difference  in ISS  was  observed during the resting state, 213 

while  significant group difference s were  found during task performance. In contrast,  the  WB -ROIs 214 

show ed group differences  in both  the resting  state  and task  condition  (Fig.  2B). These results 215 

indicate that ISS reduction in  the  Stroop -ROI s reflects  the  functional change that  occur s during task 216 

performance, rather than a general age -related change at rest ing state . Previous studies have 217 

reported whole -brain  level ISS reduction in older adults,  which  becomes more pronounced during 218 

task performance  (25, 26 ). Furthermore, a detailed examination of  the  FC change patterns revealed 219 

widespread FC decreases within  the  frontal and occipital regions from rest to task  but  fronto -220 

occipital FC increased  in the older group  (Fig.  2C ).  This  widespread changes  in FC were  not 221 

observed in the younger group.  These results indicate the i ntermodule  integration increases during 222 

task performance in older adults , supported by previous study  (27 ). T he enhancement of frontal -223 

occipital connectivity  possibly  reflect s a part of this age -related network  change .224

3.3 Determinants of ISS reduction 226 

If overactivation reflects  the  noise, regions with greater overactivation should exhibit 227 

greater  inter -individual variability  and this variability should  lead to  ISS reduction. Given the 228 

positive correlation  between  the magnitude of overactivation  ( δr) and  inter -individual variability 229 

across ROIs  (σa,r) (r = 0.49)  was was  observed , it was considered that these factors  might  reflect  the 230 

noise . However, this association  could be  partially explained by the statistical properties of mean -231 

variance scaling , whereby variance increases proportionally with the mean . When normalized by 232 

the coefficient of variation, the correlation with  δr disappeared (r = −0.001) . These findings 233 

suggest that the association between overactivation and inter -individual variability is more likely 234 

explained by mean -variance scaling, rather than indicating that overactivation reflects noise. 235 

Furthermore , the  results of the leave -one -out ISS analysis  showed  that  σa,r was significantly 236 

correlated with the contribution to group differences in ISS  (Δd) (r = −0.59), whereas  δr did not 237 

correlate  with  Δd (r = −0.17).  This indicates that  smaller  variability in regional activity is associated 238 

with a  larger  contribution  to the  difference in ISS between younger and older groups. In addition, 239 

the magnitude of overactivation  ( δr) was not significantly correlated with Δd (r = −0.17 ). The 240 

regions driving ISS reduction  primarily included  the frontal regions , such as  the  MidFG  and 241 

IFGoper ( Fig.  3A); conversely, the  occipital regions , such as  the  iLOC  and  OFusG,  which show 242 

large inter -individual  variability , suppressed  ISS group differences. This result represents the 243 

opposite pattern from the  noise model  that “regions with greater variability drive ISS reduction” .244 

We therefore conclude that the ISS reduction from the  perspective  of overactivation is not due to 245 

“noise ” but to  “compensation ” for functional decline  to maintain  cognitive  performance. 246

the younger group , and smaller in the older group  (Fig.  3B). This i ndicates that  frontal regions 250

3.4 Principal  component  analysis of  activation  patterns 262 

PCA  reveal s that Stroop task performance (ΔRT) in the older adult group  can be  predicted 263 

not by the variation in overall activation level (PC1) , but  by a specific spatial pattern (PC3) ( Fig. 264 

4A).  Previous studies have shown that the first principal component in fMRI data is almost entirely 265 

equivalent to the global signal  (30 ). PC3 reflected the contrast between the precuneus,  MedFC , and 266 

left thalamus (positive loadings) , as well as  the i LOC , OP , and  SPL  (negative loadings), indicating 267 

that older adults with  an  increased DMN  activity were  negatively correlated with  performance ,268 

whereas  posterior visual and dorsal attention activity  were  positively associated with  performance. 269 

Additionally,  iLOC  was a region that showed significantly higher activity in older  group  than in 270 

younger  group ( Supplementary  Fig.  1) . Th ese  result s indicate  that overactivation in older adults is 271 

not a uniform phenomenon , and that behavioral consequences depend on the spatial distribution of 272 

activation. Furthermore, the loading pattern of PC3 can be interpreted as a failure of  DMN 273 

deactivation during task performance. Previous studies have  similarly  reported that DMN 274 

suppression during task performance is insufficient in older adults  (31, 32 ); further,  it has been 275 

suggested that this  deactivation  failure causes resource competition with task processing  (33 ).276 

These  results  are consistent with  the present study . Although PC3 explained only 5.4% of the 277 

variance, it was significantly associated with behavior,  indicating  that age -related brain activity 278 

changes influence behavior through specific spatial contrasts , rather than  large variance 279 

components. 280

Although significant SC reductions were  observed across  213 edges at the whole -brain level 283 

(Fig.  5A), they were not concentrated in connections within  the  Stroop -ROI s (enrichment ratio ,284 

0.34). Furthermore, no significant correlation w as  identified  between  the SC strength of  each  region 285 

in the Stroop -ROI  and  ΔRT ( Fig.  5B).  Prior  studies have reported that white matter structural 286 

changes with aging are associated with declines in executive function  (34 ); however,  the  Stroop -287

FC changes and ISS reduction in  the  Stroop -ROI s reflect functional , rather than anatomical  change s.289

4. Conclusion 292 

We clearly showed that brain regions related to  performing  Stroop task behave differently 293 

between the younger and older adults in task condition but not in resting state.  The hierarchical 294 

Bayesian model is a better approach for  Stroop -ROI estimation, as it allows for individual variation 295 

while coherently pooling information across individuals, compared with the general linear model. 296 

Stroop  task performance in older adults was associated not with overall activation levels , but  rather 297 

with a specific spatial pattern reflecting the balance between  DMN  and visual attention regions. 298 

These findings suggest that such mechanisms play an important compensatory role in age -related 299 

functional decline. 300

5. Methods 303 

5.1 Participants 304 

This study was conducted with 30  healthy  older adults (mean age 68.5 ± 5.5 years, 15 males, 305 

15 females) and 22 young adults (mean age 23.8 ± 5.2 years, 15 males, 7 females). Participants 306 

were recruited  via  online bulletin boards , and data  were collect ed from October 2024 to October 307 

2025. Age groups were defined as young adults (18 –39 years  old) and older adults (60 –79 years 308 

old).  The inclusion criteria  were  individuals with  the ability to provide written informed consent 309 

for voluntary participation in the  study , age d between  18  and  80 years at the time of consent, and 310 

native Japanese speakers. Exclusion criteria were as follows:  pregnan t women, individuals with 311 

higher brain dysfunction or psychiatric disorders, individuals with visual impairments, individuals 312 

requiring assistance with walking, individuals with a history of cerebrovascular or cardiovascular 313 

disease, individuals with metal, medical devices, or implants in the body, individuals with tattoos, 314 

individuals with claustrophobia, individuals weighing 100 kg or more. All participants underwent 315 

the MMSE  and MRI . Additionally,  the  Trail  Making Test (TMT) A and B, Timed Up and Go test 316 

(TUG),  and dual -task TUG  were used for other experiments . This study was approved by the Ethics 317 

Co mmittee of  the National  Institute of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology (AIST). 318

5.2 MRI  acquisition 320 

All MRI experiments were conducted using a 3 T esla  MRI scanner (Ingenia, Philips) 321 

equipped with a 32 -channel head coil. Imaging sequences included fMRI during Stroop task 322 

execution, resting  state fMRI, structural images, and diffusion -weighted imaging (DWI). For 323 

Stroop  task fMRI, each session consisted of an initial 10 -second rest period followed by alternating 324 

30  second task blocks and 30  second rest blocks presented five times, with a total of two sessions 325 

performed. During task blocks, one of three  Japanese  words (" red ", " green ", or " blue ") was 326 

presented every 2 seconds, and participants were instructed to respond to the ink color rather than 327 

the word meaning. Responses were made using button presses with the right hand, selecting the 328 

correct color from three buttons corresponding to each color. The difference between the mean 329 

reaction time for incongruent trials and that for congruent trials (ΔRT) was calculated as an index 330 

of each participant's performance on the Stroop task.  Each task block included congruent conditions 331 

where word meaning and color matched and incongruent conditions, with stimulus presentation 332 

order randomized. Participants practiced the Stroop task outside the MRI room beforehand to 333 

ensure adequate understanding before scanning. Task presentations used PsychoPy  (35 ), and 334

5.4 Hierarchical Bayesian  model 365 

The  β values estimated for each subject, each block, and each ROI were used as observed 366

5.5 Inter -subject  similarity  analysis 411 

All  fMRI d ata were processed using  the CONN toolbox version conn22a. Six parameters of 412 

head motion and signals from the white matter and cerebrospinal fluid were regressed out, and 413 

detrending was applied to the residuals of the fMRI signal. For the resting -state  fMRI  data, low -414 

frequency fluctuation components were extracted using a bandpass filter (0.008 –0.09 Hz). 415 

Subsequently, ROI -to -ROI analysis was  conducted  using  WB -ROIs  and  the  Stroop -ROI s for both 416 

conditions  during  the Stroop  task and resting  state, creating  FC matrices for each subject. The upper 417 

triangular components of each FC matrix were vectorized to generate  the feature vectors for each 418 

subject. Correlation coefficients between  the  feature vectors across all subjects were calculated,  and 419 

ISS  analysis was employed.  The  mean correlation coefficient with other subjects within the same 420 

group was defined as the mISS.  This procedure was applied separately during the  Stroop  task and 421 

in  the  resting  state.  The Mann –Whitney U test was used to compare  the mISS between the younger 422 

and older groups . Cohen's d was calculated as an index of  the effect size. Analyses were conducted 423 

separately for the WB -ROIs and  the  Stroop -ROI s.424 

Within -group analyses were conducted to identify ROI pairs showing significant FC 425 

modulation from rest to task  to characterize the FC changes underlying ISS group difference s. For 426 

each participant, the difference between task  condition  and resting -state FC  was computed for all 427 

unique ROI pairs. W ithin each age group, one -sample t -tests were  conducted  separately to identify 428 

ROI pairs with significant FC changes from rest to task. The Bonferroni correction was applied to 429 

control  for  multiple comparisons across all 351 ROI pairs. 430

5.6 Relationship  between  group  differences and  individual  variability 433 

The group difference parameter  δr and the  ROI -specific standard deviation  σa,r for each 434 

ROI were derived from the hierarchical Bayesian model. Pearson correlations were computed 435 

between  the  δr and  σa,r values . Because  a larger mean activation can produce proportionally larger 436 

variance,  we  assessed  the correlation between  δr and the coefficient of variation, which normalizes 437 

the  variability by mean activation level , and  the partial correlation between  δr and  σa,r controlling 438 

for the overall mean activation  μr to confirm the presence of a scaling effect. 439

5.7 Leave -one -out ROI  sensitivity  analysis 441 

A leave -one -out ISS analysis , in which each ROI was excluded,  was  subsequently 442 

conducte d. The change in the effect size of the group difference  of ISS  upon exclusion of each ROI 443 

(Δd) was computed and its Pearson correlation with  σa,r and  δr were  calculated.  Positive  and 444 

negative  Δd  values  indicate that the ROI  increases  and decreases  the group difference  of ISS ,445 

respectively . We also  examined the group difference in variance and its correlation with Δd to 446 

assess whether  group differences in neural variability contributed to the overall ISS group 447 

difference.  Furthermore, we decomposed the leave -one -out effect for each ROI into group -specific 448 

contributions to clarify whether the ISS group difference was driven by changes in older, younger, 449

5.9 Structural  connectivity  analysis 466 

One older adult with missing DWI data was excluded, resulting in a sample of 28 older and 467 

22 younger adults.  For analysis of SC,  DWI data were preprocessed using MRtrix3 version 3.0.4 468 

(37 ) and FSL  (38 ). Preprocessing steps included denoising, distortion correction, eddy current 469 

correction, motion correction, B1 field inhomogeneity correction, and removal of non -brain tissue. 470 

ANTs version 2.5.0  (39 ) was used for registration to the template image. ROIs used were the same 471 

as 105 whole -brain regions as in FC analysis.  After estimating fiber orientation distributions using 472 

MRtrix3, whole -brain probabilistic tractography was performed using the tckgen function (100 473 

million streamlines). The resulting streamlines were optimized using the tcksift2 function, and a 474 

105 × 105 SC matrix was generated for each participant, with each element representing the 475 

streamline count connecting a given ROI pair. Each element was normalized by the total number 476 

of streamlines in the whole brain.  Edge -wise two -sample t -tests were performed on all connections 477 

to identify connections with significant age -related SC reduction. Multiple comparison correction 478 

was applied using false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.  Enrichment analysis was performed by 479 

comparing the proportion of FDR  significant edges . In addition, correlations between SC strength 480 

and ΔRT were examined for the hub regions identified by the  FC  analysis. 481
